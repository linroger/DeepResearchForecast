# Forecast the EU-China electric-vehicle tariff dispute as of 30 June 2027: whether the definitive EU countervailing duties on Chinese battery-electric vehicles…

## Executive Summary

As of 30 June 2027 the EU's definitive countervailing duties on Chinese battery-electric vehicles will most likely still be in force, structurally intact, for every exporter that has not secured an individual undertaking. Three timing facts drive that judgement. The five-year clock does not bind: the duties entered into force on 30 October 2024 for five years, with expiry on 31 October 2029 per the Commission's TRON case record [S1][S2], and an expiry-review request is due at latest three months before expiry [S3] — so no expiry-review decision point falls before the horizon. The WTO panel in DS630 does not expect its final report before the second quarter of 2027 [S4], leaving no time for a ruling to force implemented change inside the window. And the sole completed interim review, concluded on 10 February 2026, accepted a company-specific undertaking from Volkswagen (Anhui) under Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/328 — with Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/330 exempting undertaking-holders — while all other exporters remain subject to the definitive rates of 7.8% to 35.3% on top of the ordinary import tariff [S5][S6][S7]. A partial carve-out already exists; a general replacement does not.

The canonical scenarios, with probabilities from the report's fixed frame:

- Duties hold unchanged — 50%. Talks stall without a general minimum-price agreement and the instrument persists; trade-defence measures mostly survive to the first expiry review, and the political configuration — the Commission defending its instrument, a protectionist consensus that now includes Berlin, MOFCOM holding calibrated retaliation cards — points the same way.
- Undertaking replaces duties — 20%. Negotiations are active and one undertaking has been accepted, but the reference class is demanding: historically almost half of EU anti-dumping measures were price undertakings, a share that "drastically changed in recent years," and undertakings were rarely used against China because of monitoring difficulty [S8].
- Duties modified after review — 15%. A further interim or WTO-driven review adjusts rates without removing the instrument; the panel timetable pushes the WTO driver to the very end of the window [S4].
- Escalation and retaliation — 15%. China's response so far has been calibrated: in the brandy, dairy and pork files provisional or final duties have been imposed in all three, each time with final rates well below provisional levels [S9][S10][S11][S12].

Measured effects point to redirection rather than removal. EU imports of battery-electric cars fell 8% while plug-in-hybrid imports jumped 48%, the clearest measured substitution away from the duty-covered product [S13]. In units, China held almost 60% of EU electric-car imports in 2025, equivalent to less than 20% of EU demand — a share the IEA describes as similar to the previous year [S14]. ACEA reported battery-electric vehicles at 19.4% of EU new-car registrations in Q1 2026 [S15], while Rhodium Group reports Chinese car imports into Europe accelerating overall a year and a half after the duties [S16].

The leading indicators cluster in a tight window: the hybrid safeguard decision in December 2026; any second undertaking decision in the Official Journal, which would move weight toward the 20% scenario; the DS630 panel report, not before Q2 2027 [S4]; initiation of a further interim review of Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2754; and any new MOFCOM measure against EU automotive or agricultural exports, the trigger for escalation.

## Legal status of the duties

The operative instrument is Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2754 of 29 October 2024, applicable from 30 October 2024, imposing definitive countervailing duties on imports of new battery electric vehicles for the transport of persons originating in China [S1]. Its legal foundation is the finding that the Chinese BEV value chain benefits from unfair subsidies causing a threat of economic injury to Union BEV producers, under the basic anti-subsidy Regulation (EU) 2016/1037 [S1][S5]. The Council had voted on 4 October 2024 — 10 member states in favour (including France), 5 against (including Germany), 12 abstentions (including Spain) — and the Commission proceeded to duties of up to 45%, combining the 10% base car tariff with the countervailing margin [S17].

Company-specific ad valorem rates, in force for five years: BYD Group 17.0%; Geely Group 18.8%; SAIC Group 35.3%; Tesla (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. 7.8%, granted on application for individual examination; other cooperating companies 20.7%; non-cooperating companies 35.3% [S1]. Individual rates apply only against a valid commercial invoice with a dated declaration (additional code D008) signed by an official of the issuing entity; absent it, the 35.3% residual applies [S1]. Amounts secured by the provisional duty of 4 July 2024 are not collected, and importers may request refunds evidencing no or lower subsidisation [S1].

The only formal change since imposition came via a partial interim review initiated by the Commission on its own initiative on 4 December 2025 under Article 19(2) of Regulation (EU) 2016/1037, limited to "the examination of the form of the measure" — the acceptability of an undertaking offered by Volkswagen (Anhui) Automotive Co., Ltd. [S5][S6]. It closed with Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/330 and Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/328, both adopted 9 February 2026 and published 10 February 2026 [S5][S6]. Amended Article 1(2) exempts imports manufactured, shipped and invoiced by a company whose undertaking is accepted and listed in Decision 2026/328 — currently only VW (Anhui) [S5]. The undertaking pairs an annual quota with a minimum import price on all exports to the Union, plus documentation requirements (undertaking declarations, commercial and re-sale invoices, sales via a sole related Union importer) and monitoring duties (sales reports, verification visits, consultations) [S6]. The Commission recorded the VW offer as the only undertaking formally submitted since the definitive measures [S5]; no BYD, SAIC, Geely or Tesla offer is on the record since, and Rhodium rates BYD unlikely, partly because its plug-in-hybrid exports fall outside the duty's scope [S16]. Acceptance can lapse on withdrawal under Article 13(9) [S5].

## WTO litigation path

China requested WTO consultations with the EU on 4 November 2024 concerning the definitive countervailing duties on new battery electric vehicles from China and the underlying investigation [S4]. Consultations were held in December 2024 without resolving the dispute, according to monitoring of the panel-request record [S22]. China requested panel establishment on 13 March 2025; the Dispute Settlement Body deferred at its 24 March 2025 meeting and established the panel on 25 April 2025 [S4]. Seventeen members reserved third-party rights — Australia, Brazil, Canada, India, Japan, Kazakhstan, Korea, Mexico, Norway, Russia, Singapore, Switzerland, Thailand, Türkiye, Ukraine, the UK and the United States — and the panel was composed on 13 October 2025, its members unnamed on the WTO record [S4].

Decisively for the appeal question, on 31 July 2025 China and the EU informed the DSB they had agreed MPIA-style arbitration procedures under DSU Article 25 to decide any appeal from a final panel report in DS630 if the Appellate Body is unable to hear an appeal under Articles 16.4 and 17 [S4][S23]. Both parties have thereby pre-committed to a route that forecloses an "appeal into the void": a losing side cannot strand the report before a paralysed Appellate Body, though arbitration would extend final resolution.

The timetable slipped on 1 April 2026, when the panel Chair informed the DSB that both parties had requested significantly more time for their submissions, which the panel granted [S4][S23]. The panel does not expect to issue its final report to the parties before the second quarter of 2027, with public circulation dependent on translation into the three official languages [S4][S23]. Substantive proceedings ran through mid-2026: the United States, as third party, submitted a filing on 20 April 2026, an oral statement on 2 July 2026, answers to panel questions on 24 July 2026 and a third-party executive summary on 31 July 2026 [S23].

Three consequences follow for the horizon. First, the report floor barely overlaps it: since no final report is expected before Q2 2027 and circulation then awaits translation [S4], a WTO-forced modification of Regulation 2024/2754 before 30 June 2027 is not the modal path — the "Duties modified after review — 15%" scenario rests chiefly on Commission interim reviews rather than DS630. Second, the pre-agreed Article 25 route makes an appeal-in-the-void strategy unavailable to either party [S4][S23], but an appeal — whose duration the record does not specify — would push resolution beyond the horizon. Third, litigation runs parallel to negotiation: minimum-price talks resumed in December 2025 [S24], and under the 9 October 2026 EU-China understanding, in Bruegel's reading of the Commission statement, trade in electric vehicles "will continue to be regulated through the application of either countervailing duties or price undertakings" — so a negotiated undertaking, the "Undertaking replaces duties — 20%" scenario, could moot the panel before any report [S25]. Falsifiers: a report issued earlier than signalled; an EU statement of non-appeal with immediate implementation; or a notified bilateral settlement withdrawing DS630.

## Price-undertaking negotiations

As of 20 October 2026 the definitive duties coexist with one accepted, company-specific undertaking — a partial, not general, replacement: Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/328 of 9 February 2026 accepted Volkswagen (Anhui)'s offer, and Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/330 exempts imports from companies whose undertaking is accepted, while all other exporters remain subject to the definitive rates of 7.8%–35.3% [S5][S6][S7].

### The Commission's conditions

The Commission issued its Guidance Document on submission of price-undertaking offers for Chinese battery electric vehicles on 12 January 2026, following Commission–MOFCOM discussions held since the investigation, framed as exploring "alternative WTO-compatible solutions" to the duties [S7]. It covers minimum import price, sales channels, cross-compensation and future investments in the EU, with each offer assessed "in an objective and fair manner, following the principle of non-discrimination and in accordance with WTO rules" [S7]. Minimum import prices may be set either by adjusting exporters' historical CIF prices to reflect the applicable duty margin, or by reference to prices of comparable non-subsidised BEVs produced in the EU; offers may be individual or joint, and non-compliance can trigger withdrawal of the undertaking and retroactive duty collection [S18]. Reuters reported that the guidance requires minimum prices per EV model and configuration, referenced to the price to the first independent customer [S26].

The undertaking has four elements: an annual quota; a minimum import price on all exports to the Union; formal requirements (Undertaking Declarations, commercial and re-sale invoices, exports exclusively via a sole related Union importer); and monitoring through sales reports and verification visits [S6]. The Commission recorded it "was the only undertaking offer formally submitted to the Commission since the imposition of the definitive countervailing measures" [S5]; no primary record shows BYD, SAIC, Geely or Tesla filing one since, and Rhodium rates BYD's prospects low, partly because its plug-in-hybrid exports fall outside the duties' scope [S16].

Historically, EU price undertakings covered homogeneous commodities, not complex goods like automobiles, and "almost half" of EU anti-dumping measures were once undertakings — a share that has since "drastically changed," with rare use against China because of monitoring difficulty [S8][S24]. The 9 October 2026 understanding is reported to curb Chinese hybrid exports and, per MOFCOM, to "immediately begin negotiations on electric vehicle price undertakings and discuss investment cooperation" [S27][S28]; Bruegel reads it as leaving BEV trade "regulated through the application of either countervailing duties or price undertakings" [S25]. A social-media claim that EV minimum prices were already agreed is unconfirmed [S29]. "Undertaking replaces duties — 20%" therefore still requires a government-endorsed, multi-exporter deal accepted by the Commission under the January 2026 guidance — a step beyond the single-company carve-out now in force.

## Chinese retaliation track

China answered the EU's BEV duties with implemented but calibrated retaliation. By 2025 it had opened three investigations widely perceived as retaliation for the EU measures — brandy, dairy and pork — and provisional or final duties have been imposed in all three files, each time with final rates well below provisional levels [S9][S10][S11][S12].

One aggregator's headline pork rate of "up to 18.9%" conflicts with the final rates of 4.9%–19.8% reported by Reuters, which stand [S32][S10].

### Bargaining pattern and linkage

All three final rulings came in far below provisional rates, with exemptions and refunds — behaviour consistent with bargaining chips tied to the EV talks rather than pure protection [S10][S11][S30]. Pork rates were cut five days after MOFCOM announced that EV minimum-price talks had restarted [S10][S24]. Reuters reported on 27 June 2025 that China would finalise the cognac minimum-price arrangement only if progress was made on the EU EV tariffs [S11]. China itself accepted price undertakings from some brandy exporters alongside duties of 27.7%–34.9% — undertakings used as the settlement currency [S9]. The Commission has challenged the dairy and brandy measures at the WTO, calling them "unjustified and unwarranted" [S33][S12].

### Pending escalation options

On automotive, Chinese automakers urged Beijing in June 2024 to raise tariffs on imported EU gasoline cars with large engines, and a Chinese business group warned that temporary extra tariffs on large-displacement imports were being considered; no such measure had been announced or implemented as of 20 October 2026 [S34][S35]. No MOFCOM action against EU aviation exports appears in the record. The remaining levers are new anti-dumping or anti-subsidy probes on other EU sectors and tightening or revoking the brandy minimum-price undertakings, whose price levels and review terms are undisclosed [S11][S10].

Under "Escalation and retaliation — 15%", talks collapse and China imposes visible measures on EU automotive or agricultural exports while the duties remain. The record so far is calibrated, reversible retaliation — agricultural duties in force, automotive untouched — and the 9 October 2026 understanding is reported to improve China market access for €4 billion of EU products [S27][S36]. The decisive signposts are any MOFCOM step on large-displacement cars or a new probe beyond agriculture.

## Member-state and industry politics

### The vote that fixed the map
The political geography was set by the Council vote of 4 October 2024 on the definitive duties: per Reuters, 10 member states voted in favour (including France and Italy), 5 against (including Germany) and 12 abstained (including Spain) [S17]; the Guardian reported leaders backing the tariffs despite the split [S37]. No Council vote on the duties appears in the retrieved record after October 2024 — member-state leverage since has run through review consent, lobbying and the parallel hybrid negotiation.

### France's protectionist anchor
France voted in favour in October 2024 [S17] and by mid-2026 was pressing, now with Germany, for stronger trade-defence instruments [S38]. Rhodium reported that a record 9.3% of new cars sold in the EU in December 2025 were made in China, sustaining pressure for protection in France and, increasingly, Germany [S16]. The coalition's constraint is retaliation exposure: duties of up to 34.9% on EU brandy [S11] and provisional duties of up to 42.7% on EU dairy [S12] fall on French exports. No dated public position from Renault or Stellantis appears in the retrieved record; their weight enters through Paris's vote.

### Germany's turn toward defence
Berlin voted against the duties in 2024 [S17], when German carmakers — about one third of whose 2023 sales were in China — lobbied against them alongside IG Metall (Reuters) [S39]. Under Chancellor Merz it converged with Paris: in 2026 Merz and President Macron jointly wrote to Commission President von der Leyen seeking a new instrument to cut a trading partner off from the single market if unfair practices continue [S38]. China fell from Germany's second-largest export market in 2021 to ninth by the first half of 2026 (Germany Trade & Invest data) [S38], and pressure from the German automotive lobby itself for trade defence grew [S38]. By mid-2026 the member-state argument was over additional protection — the Industrial Accelerator Act's local-content requirements — not repeal of the existing duties [S16]; a Franco-German deal supporting the car industry, praised by Volkswagen, settled parallel emissions-rules controversy (reported) [S40][S41].

### Where the leverage sits
Formal rights sit with the Commission, which alone accepts undertakings and conducts reviews: its 12 January 2026 guidance promises offers will be assessed "in an objective and fair manner, following the principle of non-discrimination and in accordance with WTO rules" [S7], and its 9 February 2026 acceptance of the Volkswagen (Anhui) undertaking — the only offer formally submitted since the definitive measures [S5] — carved one company out of the duty regime [S6]. Rhodium judges few Chinese OEMs will follow, since undertakings constrain discount-driven strategies such as BYD's subsidy-plus-incentive discounts in Germany [S16]; that pricing behaviour runs against BIS-measured credit to China's private non-financial sector of 204.1% of GDP in 2026-Q1, up from 201.9% in 2025-Q1 [S21]. MOFCOM insists on government-to-government negotiation and urged the EU not to negotiate with individual manufacturers [S24]; Reuters reported China would finalise the cognac minimum-price deal only if progress were made on the EV tariffs [S11]. Šefčovič's 9 October 2026 Beijing understanding on hybrid exports and market access for €4 billion of EU products [S27] keeps the channel open, with electric-vehicle trade still regulated by "either countervailing duties or price undertakings" (Bruegel's reading) [S25].

### What the data show

Units and values point in different directions. In units, EU electric-car imports grew strongly in 2025 and China held almost 60% of them, equivalent to less than 20% of EU demand — a share the IEA describes as similar to the previous year [S14]. In value, EU imports of battery-electric cars fell 8% while plug-in-hybrid imports jumped 48%, the clearest measured substitution away from the duty-covered product [S13]. ACEA reported EU new-car registrations up 4% in Q1 2026 with battery-electric vehicles at 19.4% of the market [S15]. Rhodium Group reports that Chinese car imports into Europe are accelerating overall a year and a half after the duties [S16]. Official EU series stop at 2025: no official monthly 2026 series for Chinese BEV imports has been published [S13].

### Revenue, causation and localisation

The statutory ad valorem rates (17.0%–35.3%, additional to the 10% standard import duty) have applied unchanged since 30 October 2024 [S1]. No official duty-collection series exists; the only quantified figure is the CEPR/VoxEU authors' estimate of roughly €500 million per quarter, about €2 billion per year, based on actual 2024–25 import patterns [S42].

Causation is contested. Gros et al. estimate China's value share in EU EV imports fell from about 55% in early 2024 to about 42% by mid-2025, but by a similar or larger margin in tariff-free Norway, Switzerland and the UK (Q2 2025: −40% in non-EU markets vs −33% in the EU); their difference-in-differences estimate of the duties' effect on Chinese import shares is negative, small and statistically insignificant, and import unit values fell only marginally [S42] — in tension with the protective intent recorded in the Regulation [S1].

On localisation, plant-level output volumes for BYD Hungary, Chery Spain and Leapmotor/Stellantis are not quantified in the retrieved official sources [S13][S14]. The one formal modification of the duty regime so far is company-specific rather than localisation: the Volkswagen (Anhui) undertaking — annual quota plus minimum import price, accepted 9 February 2026 — which exempts a single exporter while duties otherwise remain [S5][S6]. Chinese export capacity overall shows no compression: U.S. imports of goods from China stood at 27133.8159 Millions of Dollars in 2026-08, up from 25371.0252 Millions of Dollars in 2025-08 (+6.948%) [S20].

## Historical base rates

### Undertakings in EU trade-defence practice

EU law permits price undertakings to be accepted instead of duties, the exporter committing not to sell in the Union below a minimum price [S44][S8]. Historically this was a common outcome: Steinbach (Journal of Economic Integration, 2014) records that "in the past, almost half of EU anti-dumping measures were price undertakings," that this "drastically changed in recent years," and that undertakings were rarely used against China because of monitoring difficulty [S8]. No verified modern rate exists: the Commission's 2024 annual report counts 192 definitive measures in force at end-2024 (124 anti-dumping, 38 anti-dumping extensions, 22 anti-subsidy, 7 anti-subsidy extensions, 1 safeguard) but publishes no count of undertakings accepted in lieu of duties [S3]. The best-documented precedent of duties replaced by an undertaking before the five-year expiry is the 2013 solar case, settled by Commission Decision of 2 August 2013 accepting an undertaking with minimum prices and a volume cap [S45]; contemporary analyses record provisional duties of 11.8% against an initially expected 47% [S46]. In the BEV case itself, the first company-specific undertaking — Volkswagen (Anhui) — was accepted on 9 February 2026 as a carve-out inside the duty regime, and the Commission stated it was the only undertaking offer formally submitted since imposition, as of that date [S5][S6].

### Chinese retaliation after EU measures

Retaliation is the base-rate default, not the exception. In 2013 China opened anti-dumping investigations into EU wine and polysilicon within months of the provisional solar duties [S47]; in the current dispute it opened three investigations widely perceived as retaliation — brandy, pork and dairy [S9] — and implemented final duties on brandy (up to 34.9% from 5 July 2025, with minimum-price exemptions for major cognac houses) [S11] and pork (4.9%–19.8% from 17 December 2025, far below the provisional 15.6%–62.4%) [S10]. Final dairy duties of 7.4%–11.7% effective 13 February 2026 are reported by Caixin and The Straits Times [S30][S31]. The revealed pattern is calibrated, reversible escalation — final rates set well below provisional levels, exemptions and refunds granted — consistent with bargaining-chip use tied to the EV talks [S11][S10]. Macro exposure reinforces calibration: China's exports of goods and services were 21.071% of GDP in 2025, up from 20.443% of GDP in 2024 [S19].

The five-year expiry clock does not bind: the definitive duties entered into force on 30 October 2024 for five years [S1], with expiry on 31 October 2029 per the Commission's TRON case record [S2], and an expiry-review request is due at latest three months before expiry [S3] — so no expiry-review decision point falls before the horizon.

### WTO panel window

The DS630 panel, composed 13 October 2025 [S4], stated on 1 April 2026 that it does not expect to issue its final report to the parties before the second quarter of 2027 [S4][S23]; third-party submissions ran through 31 July 2026 [S23]. The window therefore straddles the horizon: circulation by 30 June 2027 is feasible but not required, and the precise latest date within Q2 2027 is unpublished. Any appeal is pre-routed to the MPIA-style DSU Article 25 arbitration both parties agreed on 31 July 2025, pre-empting an appeal into the void [S4]; such arbitration would itself run past the horizon. An adverse panel report could therefore change duty rates before 30 June 2027 only if the Commission opens an interim review in response — the "Duties modified after review — 15%" path — not through direct WTO compulsion.

### Negotiation and review milestones

The 9 October 2026 understanding caps Chinese hybrid exports — reported at around 350,000 vehicles per year over four years, implemented via an EU safeguard with provisional measures advertised for December 2026 — and, per MOFCOM, the sides will "immediately begin negotiations on electric vehicle price undertakings" [S27][S28][S25][S48]. Bruegel's reading is that BEV trade "will continue to be regulated through the application of either countervailing duties or price undertakings", with no withdrawal clause [S25]; the Commission's own statement text remains unconfirmed. Legally, new undertakings follow the February 2026 pattern: a partial interim review limited to the form of the measure plus an implementing decision, which took from initiation on 4 December 2025 to decision on 9 February 2026 for Volkswagen (Anhui) [S5][S6] — roughly nine weeks, my computation from the cited dates. As of February 2026 the Commission recorded VW (Anhui)'s as the only formally submitted offer [S5]; no subsequent offer is published. Movement toward "Undertaking replaces duties — 20%" thus requires new company submissions and Commission decisions inside H1 2027, while MOFCOM's position on the company-specific model remains unrecorded. Whether the December safeguard instrument links quota treatment to BEV-undertaking progress is unknown until it is launched [S48].

## Scenarios and probabilities

### Scenario Probability Distribution
- **Duties hold unchanged (50% probability):** No agreement is reached and the definitive duties stay in force at current rates through 30 June 2027; prior: trade-defence measures mostly persist unchanged to first expiry review.
- **Undertaking replaces duties (20% probability):** A negotiated minimum-price or price-undertaking agreement accepted by both MOFCOM and the Commission replaces the duties before mid-2027; prior: undertakings accepted in a minority of EU anti-subsidy cases, but talks are active.
- **Duties modified after review (15% probability):** An interim or WTO-driven review adjusts duty rates without removing the instrument before the horizon.
- **Escalation and retaliation (15% probability):** Talks collapse and China imposes visible retaliatory measures on EU automotive or agricultural exports while the duties remain.

The four canonical states partition outcomes on 30 June 2027 along two dimensions: the legal state of Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2754 (rates up to 35.3% [S1]) and whether China adds visible new retaliation. A stall in talks without escalation falls to Duties hold unchanged; a stall with new Chinese measures maps to the escalation state. Two timing facts bound every path: the WTO panel does not expect its final report before the second quarter of 2027 [S4], and the sole interim review concluded on 10 February 2026 by accepting a Volkswagen (Anhui) undertaking as a carve-out while leaving the duty structure intact [S5][S6] — so "unchanged" now means unchanged for exporters without accepted undertakings.

### Duties hold unchanged — 50%
Path: no further acceptance, no rate review; the Commission's TRON case database lists expiry on 31 October 2029 [S2]. The Commission recorded the VW (Anhui) offer as the only one formally submitted since imposition [S5]; in 2024, 11 expiry reviews concluded with duties confirmed and 2 measures lapsed [S3]. Signposts: Official Journal silence on new acceptance decisions; the separate hybrid-safeguard track toward provisional measures by December 2026 [S48]. Implementing decisions for BYD, SAIC, Geely or Tesla would move mass away.

### Undertaking replaces duties — 20%
Path: offers under the 12 January 2026 guidance [S7] displace the duties as the operative instrument. Supporting: per Bruegel's reading, the 9 October 2026 understanding keeps EV trade regulated by "either countervailing duties or price undertakings" [S25]; MOFCOM's readout says EV price-undertaking negotiations start immediately [S28]; MOFCOM has pressed for government-to-government minimum-price talks since December 2025 [S24]. Against: the Commission doubts a single minimum price offsets the injury [S24]; Rhodium rates BYD's fit low because its PHEV exports sit outside the duty's scope [S16]; undertakings saw rare use against China over monitoring difficulty [S8]; the one documented replacement (solar, 2013) covered homogeneous modules [S45]. Signposts: MOFCOM endorsing the company-specific model; publication of the VW-annex price and quota benchmarks.

### Duties modified after review — 15%
Path: a new interim review resets margins; the WTO route cannot conclude in-window — the report is not expected before Q2 2027 and Article 25 appeal arbitration is pre-agreed [S4]. The 2025–26 review touched only the measure's form [S5]; 4 interim reviews were initiated in 2024, with outcomes unreported here [S3]. Signposts: a review notice limited to duty levels; early-Q2 2027 panel circulation.

### Escalation and retaliation — 15%
Path: talks collapse and MOFCOM adds automotive measures while duties remain. The record is modulated: brandy finals up to 34.9% with minimum-price exemptions for major cognac houses [S11]; pork finals of 4.9%–19.8%, below provisional [S10]; dairy finals of 7.4%–11.7% per Caixin [S30]; Reuters reported brandy relief was conditioned on EV-tariff progress [S11]; large-engine-car tariffs, signalled since June 2024, have no implementing record in the evidence [S34]. China reached duties in at least 2 of 3 retaliation-linked probes (own count) [S9][S49], but mutual exposure — China's exports of goods and services at 21.071% of GDP in 2025 [S19] — and time bought by the October 2026 understanding favour managed outcomes [S25]. Signposts: a large-displacement-car probe; tighter brandy minimum prices.

### Reading the signposts

On the WTO track, the panel was composed on 13 October 2025 and both parties pre-committed on 31 July 2025 to Article 25 arbitration for any appeal, foreclosing an appeal into the void [S4]. The 1 April 2026 delay pushed the report to Q2 2027 at the earliest [S4][S23], so WTO-driven modification before the horizon requires an unusually early report plus rapid Commission action — the weakest path for "Duties modified after review — 15%".

On negotiation, the December 2026 hybrid safeguard is the nearest dated discriminator: adoption without any BEV linkage favours "Duties hold unchanged — 50%", while linkage or a term sheet with minimum prices favours "Undertaking replaces duties — 20%". Absence of a second accepted offer by roughly Q1 2027 would weigh toward the duties holding, given the Commission's statement that VW (Anhui) was the sole offer [S5], and Rhodium's assessment that BYD is unlikely to seek one because PHEV exports sit outside the duty scope [S16].

On retaliation, MOFCOM's revealed pattern is calibrated and reversible: pork finals were cut to 4.9%–19.8% from provisional rates of up to 62.4% [S10][S49], and dairy finals were reported at 7.4%–11.7% after provisionals of 21.9%–42.7% [S30][S12]. A fresh initiation notice on large-engine cars is therefore the clean falsifier for "Escalation and retaliation — 15%"; quiet maintenance of the brandy exemptions signals continued bargaining rather than escalation [S11].
