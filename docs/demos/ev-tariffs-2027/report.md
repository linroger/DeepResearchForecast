## Part 2 — Framework & Synthesis

### Framework and Synthesis

**The analytical question.** This part of the submission answers one question: what is the state of Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2754 — the EU's definitive countervailing duties on Chinese battery-electric vehicles — as of 30 June 2027? The Part-1 table resolves this into four mutually exclusive bins: duties hold essentially unchanged (50%); a negotiated price-undertaking or minimum-price agreement replaces them (22%); an interim review modifies rates while retaining the instrument (11%); and a residual/escalation bin (17%). The probabilities were fixed before the narrative was written; this chapter defends them.

### The Framework: Bargaining Under Legal Inertia

The forecast rests on a three-layer framework. The first layer is **legal inertia**: EU trade-defence instruments, once definitive, are sticky. They lapse only through defined legal channels — acceptance of undertakings under the basic anti-subsidy regulation, an interim review, or expiry review — each with its own clock, evidentiary threshold, and institutional owner. Absent a deliberate act by the Commission, the default is continuation.

The second layer is **bargaining structure**. The dispute is a bilateral negotiation between the European Commission and MOFCOM, in which the duties are the EU's principal leverage and Chinese retaliatory capacity — demonstrated in agriculture and spirits — is Beijing's. Outcomes that require both sides to move (a negotiated undertaking) are structurally harder and slower than outcomes that require no one to move (continuation).

The third layer is **political economy within the EU**. The Council vote of 4 October 2024 (10 in favour, 5 against, Germany among the dissenters, France leading the pro-duty coalition) revealed a divided member-state landscape. The forecast assumes no Council-level coalition emerges with both the will and the procedural means to force the Commission to withdraw or fundamentally renegotiate the measure before mid-2027. The Franco-German axis has, if anything, shifted toward the pro-defence camp as French industrial policy hardened and German opposition softened after the initial vote — a reversal that removes the most plausible fast-track to dismantling.

### The Causal Logic: Why 50% for Unchanged Duties

The modal scenario is not an event but the absence of one. Three causal chains converge on it.

First, **the default path is the status quo**. Regulation (EU) 2024/2754 applies its original company-specific schedule — BYD at 17.0%, Geely at 18.8%, SAIC at 35.3%, Tesla (Shanghai) at 7.8%, other cooperating companies at 20.7%, residual rate 35.3% — on top of the 10% conventional import duty. Every scenario except continuation requires a positive, discretionary act: a Commission decision to accept undertakings, a notice initiating an interim review, or a negotiated settlement. Base rates for such acts in contested, high-visibility cases within a fixed window are low; the burden of proof sits with the change scenarios.

Second, **Chinese adaptation reduces pressure for change**. Our scenario analysis holds that Chinese producers absorb the duties through localization, price repositioning, and margin compression rather than demanding their government trade away the issue. The macro data are consistent with an economy for which EU auto tariffs are marginal: China's exports of goods and services stood at 21.071% of GDP in 2025, at the top of its five-year range of 18.27%–21.071% (World Bank WDI), a year-over-year rise of 0.628 percentage points. This is not the profile of an export machine being choked by a regional tariff and forced to concede across the board. Meanwhile Chinese domestic credit conditions — private non-financial-sector credit at 204.1% of GDP in 2026-Q1, up 2.2 points year over year and at the top of its five-year range of 182.5%–204.1% (BIS) — suggest a state with fiscal-monetary room to subsidize its producers' adjustment rather than pay a diplomatic price to remove the EU measure.

Third, **the retaliation channel stays calibrated**. Every Chinese countermeasure actually implemented has landed in agriculture and spirits — politically salient, economically containable sectors. China has not touched EU automotive exports. The asymmetry is deliberate: Beijing wants leverage without escalating into the sector where German jobs are at stake, precisely because escalating there would harden rather than soften the EU coalition. Our scenario analysis therefore treats major automotive retaliation before 30 June 2027 as a modest-probability event absorbed within the residual bin, not as a central driver of the other three.

### The Causal Logic: Why Only 22% for a Negotiated Undertaking

The undertaking path is genuinely open — the legal door never closed after October 2024, and price undertakings are a recognized instrument with historical precedents in EU trade defence. But three frictions compress it below a coin flip.

First, **scope**. Resolution requires an implementing act published in the Official Journal accepting undertakings covering a majority of Chinese BEV exports to the EU. Any deal covering only one or two producers fails the criterion. Holding a cartel-like minimum price across competing Chinese manufacturers — BYD, Geely, SAIC and others with divergent cost structures and Europe strategies — is hard for Beijing to enforce and hard for the Commission to monitor to its satisfaction.

Second, **the Commission's verification doctrine**. The Commission has consistently insisted that any undertaking must be effectively monitorable. Chinese subsidy opacity — the very fact that produced the duties — makes verification the sticking point that has sunk previous negotiation rounds.

Third, **timing**. A window opens after 9 October 2026 (when the first review-adjacent procedural milestones pass), leaving roughly nine months to 30 June 2027 — enough for a deal, but not enough to make one the modal outcome. Our scenario analysis weights the diplomatic momentum (both sides have reasons to want a face-saving settlement) against the structural obstacles, and 22% is where they balance. It is the second-most-likely branch, and the one with the widest genuine uncertainty, but it is not the center of the distribution.

### The Causal Logic: Why Only 11% for an Interim Review

This is the branch casual forecasters overweight. Interim reviews are common in EU trade defence generally, and China is actively litigating the measure at the WTO. But an interim review concluding — or even materially advancing — before 30 June 2027 collides with the legal clock: interim reviews require a change of circumstances evidenced over a sustained period, and the Commission's doctrinal posture discourages reviews that would reward litigation pressure. An initiation notice alone, without conclusions, leaves the original schedule in force and does not satisfy this bin's full resolution criteria. The 11% reflects the small probability of a genuine, concluded rate adjustment within the window.

### The Residual Bin: Why 17%

The 17% "Other / Status Quo" bin absorbs everything the named scenarios' complete criteria do not capture: partial undertakings below the majority threshold (the most likely single occupant), mixed outcomes such as incremental new company-specific undertakings layered onto the retained duties, WTO panel developments that change rhetoric but not the regulation, and major Chinese automotive retaliation coexisting with unchanged duties. Its size reflects honest epistemic humility: the interaction of two large bureaucracies over two and a half years produces outcomes that no clean bin captures.

### Decisive Evidence and How It Was Weighed

The forecast hierarchy privileges (i) the legal text and procedural record of Regulation (EU) 2024/2754, (ii) observed state behavior — the Council vote composition, China's actual retaliation pattern, the failure of prior undertaking talks — and (iii) macro indicators as conditioning context. On the third: U.S. imports of goods from China were $27.13 billion in August 2026, up 6.948% year over year (U.S. Census), within a five-year range of $18.90 billion to $50.38 billion — a reminder that the U.S.-China channel, not the EU channel, dominates Chinese trade politics and absorbs Beijing's retaliatory attention. Chinese macro data (World Bank, BIS as cited above) are used only as publisher-reported observations for closed periods; no open period is treated as a completed observation, and publisher projections were never treated as observed values.

### Prediction-Market Anchors

Where prediction markets price related EU-China trade questions, we treated them as weak anchors, not inputs. Markets of this kind price political attention cycles and headline frequency, which systematically overweights change scenarios in high-visibility disputes; our legal-inertia framework implies markets skew toward action where institutions default to inaction. Where market-implied probabilities sat above our continuation estimate, we did not move toward them; where they sat below our undertaking estimate, we likewise held. The anchors served as a sanity check on gross miscalibration, not as evidence.

### Bottom Line

The spine — 50/22/11/17 — encodes one central judgment: legal instruments outlast headlines. The duties persist not because the dispute is frozen, but because every actor's best alternative is the status quo: the Commission keeps its leverage, Beijing's retaliation remains calibrated below the automotive threshold, and Chinese producers adapt. The negotiated path is real but structurally obstructed; the review path is clock-bound; and the residual absorbs the genuinely unknowable. Confidence is medium because the two key discretionary actors — the Commission and MOFCOM — can jointly move this to the 22% branch at any time, and no analytical framework can fully price that discretion.

This chapter states the forecast plainly. As of 30 June 2027, the EU definitive countervailing duties on Chinese battery-electric vehicles will most likely still be in force, essentially unchanged: Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2754 applying its original company-specific schedule — BYD at 17.0%, Geely at 18.8%, SAIC at 35.3%, Tesla (Shanghai) at 7.8%, other cooperating companies at 20.7% and the residual rate at 35.3%, on top of the 10% base tariff for a combined ceiling of up to 45%. Around that central case, three alternatives carry the remaining probability mass: a negotiated price-undertaking or minimum-price agreement replacing the duties for a majority of Chinese BEV exports; a modification of duty rates through an interim review; and a residual bin of mixed outcomes — partial carve-outs, hybrid-safeguard friction, negotiation deadlock, and a small Chinese escalation tail that stays pointed away from EU automotive exports. The headline finding is that regulatory inertia plus active-but-unfinished negotiation makes "duties persist while talks continue" the modal outcome. Overall confidence is rated medium, principally because the durability of the 9 October 2026 EU-China understanding rests on secondary accounts of an unsigned framework whose presupposed safeguard investigation had not even been launched as of the research cut-off.

### The Four Scenarios and Their Probabilities

**Scenario 1 — Duties hold unchanged: 50%.** The resolution criterion is concrete: at 30 June 2027, Regulation 2024/2754 remains in force with its original rate schedule for every exporter without an accepted undertaking — Volkswagen (Anhui) remains the sole carve-out under Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/328 and Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/330, adopted 9 February and published 10 February 2026 — no Official Journal act has accepted undertakings covering a majority of Chinese BEV exports, and China has imposed no new measures on EU automotive exports. Incremental company-specific undertakings alongside the VW carve-out would still count here so long as coverage stays below a majority of exports.

The 50% weight rests on four mutually reinforcing structures. First, the legal clock: the duties run to 31 October 2029 per the Commission's TRON case database, and no expiry-review decision point — whose request window opens only in the measure's final months — arises before the horizon; the instrument's default state is continuation. Second, the institutional base rate: of 13 expiry reviews concluded in 2024, 11 confirmed duties, and only four interim reviews were initiated across all 192 definitive measures in force. Third, exporter behaviour: after four months of a formal undertaking pathway under the Commission's 12 January 2026 guidance, exactly one offer has been filed — VW (Anhui)'s, which the Commission itself recorded as the sole formal submission — and BYD's duty-free plug-in-hybrid channel ($7.5 billion of EU-bound exports in H1 2026, tripled year-on-year ) keeps the largest exporter content with the status quo. Fourth, member-state politics: the Franco-German coalition that Berlin's 2024 "no" vote (in the 10-5-12 Council vote of 4 October 2024 ) once threatened has inverted into a joint demand for stronger trade defence, closing the main political channel through which rapid retreat could have been imposed.

**Scenario 2 — Undertaking replacement: 22%.** This resolves if, before 30 June 2027, an implementing act in the Official Journal accepts undertakings covering a majority of Chinese BEV exports — operationally, BYD plus at least one of Geely or SAIC, following the partial-interim-review-plus-decision pattern of February 2026. The probability sits materially above token levels because the 9 October 2026 Beijing understanding commits both sides to "immediately begin negotiations on electric vehicle price undertakings"; because both historical EU-China duty episodes ended in undertakings (solar 2013 in full; VW Anhui in part ); and because Trade Commissioner Maroš Šefčovič has a personal settlement stake in converting his framework into a signed package. It is held below a coin flip by four obstacles: the Commission's company-specific model versus MOFCOM's government-to-government demand, each side's preferred format being the other's red line; exporter incentives that penalise price restraint; the absence of any forcing deadline — the DS630 panel report is not expected before Q2 2027, with pre-agreed Article 25 appeal arbitration beyond it; and a Franco-German coalition that wants settlement without erosion of protection. The most plausible deal shape, if one comes, is a hybrid: MOFCOM orchestrating a wave of company filings the Commission can accept under its existing guidance, dissolving the format clash without either government visibly capitulating.

**Scenario 3 — Interim-review modification: 11%.** This requires an Official Journal notice initiating or concluding an interim review of Regulation 2024/2754, or an amending regulation adjusting rates, before 30 June 2027 — with the crucial caveat that a review limited to accepting a new undertaking (the case R847 pattern of December 2025 to February 2026 ) does not qualify. The scenario is suppressed by three facts: no evidentiary trigger under Article 19(2) — changed subsidy or injury circumstances — is on the record; the WTO clock cannot compel Commission action inside the window given the extended panel timetable and pre-committed arbitration; and no domestic constituency wants a review, since dutied exporters have the cheaper undertaking channel, EU producers are protected, and Paris and Berlin both prefer the instrument as it stands. Its 11% survives mainly as the face-saving fallback the Commission would need if undertaking talks collapse while leaving both sides obliged to show movement.

**Scenario 4 — Residual and mixed outcomes: 17%.** This bin has positive content, not merely leftovers. Its most likely residents are: one or two additional company-specific carve-outs short of majority coverage; hybrid-safeguard friction that changes the trade-policy mix without touching the BEV duties; negotiation rounds that conclude in conditional, unsigned texts; and a small escalation sub-branch should talks collapse acrimoniously. The escalation tail is deliberately small: China's entire retaliation record — brandy finals up to 34.9% with cognac-house exemptions, pork finals of 4.9–19.8% against provisionals up to 62.4%, dairy finals reported at 7.4–11.7% after provisionals of 21.9–42.7% — shows loud provisionals, quiet finals, agricultural targets only, and relief conditioned on EV-talk progress. The large-displacement car tariffs signalled since June 2024 remain unimplemented as of 20 October 2026, and playing them would destroy both the negotiating framework Beijing itself demanded and the German constituency most sympathetic to settlement.

On the separate retaliation question: this report judges Chinese retaliatory measures against EU automotive exports before 30 June 2027 to be unlikely — conditional on talks remaining alive in some form, which Scenarios 1 and 2 jointly make roughly three-quarters likely.

### Why Inertia Wins: The Mechanism

The probability ordering is not a judgement about diplomatic goodwill; it follows from the distribution of formal authority and incentives. The Commission holds a monopoly on every legal path to change — undertaking acceptance, review initiation, WTO defence [S7] — and its recorded positions give it weak reasons to use that authority: it has publicly doubted that a single minimum price would offset subsidy-caused injury [S7]; it collects roughly €500 million per quarter in duty revenue, about €2 billion per year; and its instrument credibility is at stake in DS630, where a retreat under litigation pressure would be read across all 192 definitive measures as evidence that litigation pays. MOFCOM, the actor with the strongest motive to change the regime, cannot do so unilaterally and rejects the only format Brussels has demonstrated — company-specific offers. The exporters whose filings would mechanically trigger change are declining the invitation, because undertakings cap the discount-driven strategies that carried Chinese brands from 50% to more than 70% of EU imports from China. Meanwhile the WTO panel, composed only on 13 October 2025 with 17 third parties including the United States, and a timetable extended on 1 April 2026, constrains rhetoric more than outcomes inside the window. The formal-authority geometry is unambiguous: the Commission regulates the duty regulation, the undertaking guidance, the VW carve-out and every Chinese exporter simultaneously, while the DS630 panel can influence the regulation but cannot itself amend it — every legally binding change must travel through Brussels.

The trade data reinforce rather than disturb this equilibrium. EU BEV import value fell 8% in 2025 while units grew about 35% to more than 900,000; China's value share fell to 42% of extra-EU electric-car imports, yet Chinese-made cars hit a record 9.3% of EU new-car sales; and CEPR's difference-in-differences analysis finds the duties' measurable effect small and statistically insignificant, with similar or larger share declines in tariff-free Norway, Switzerland and the UK. This ambiguity is stabilising: it gives the Commission no reason to abandon the instrument and MOFCOM no demonstrable leverage to force abandonment, lowering urgency on both sides. An instrument with contested real effects is, paradoxically, an easy instrument to leave in place.

A steelman deserves recording. The strongest case against the modal forecast is that the October 2026 understanding changes everything: it caps BYD's hybrid escape valve at roughly 350,000 vehicles per year, restoring the largest exporter's incentive to seek terms on its dutied BEV trade; Beijing has invested political capital in the undertaking track and rations retaliation concessions to reward its progress; and history is two-for-two for undertakings. This report takes that case seriously — it is why replacement carries 22%, and why the residual bin rather than the duties-hold scenario would absorb a burst of company filings arriving too late for majority acceptance. But a framework understanding is not an implementing act. Undertakings become effective only through Official Journal decisions, and the roughly nine-week VW review cycle means a majority-covering package must be substantially agreed by roughly April–May 2027 to be in force by the horizon. As of the research cut-off there is one accepted undertaking, zero additional submissions on record, an unfulfilled commitment to "immediately begin" talks, and a safeguard investigation not yet launched. Momentum without filings is the definition of duties-hold-plus-talks, not replacement.

### The Verification Calendar: What to Watch, Month by Month

The forecast is falsifiable on a dated schedule of observable events:

- **December 2026 — the hybrid-safeguard decision.** The single nearest discriminator in the entire forecast. Adoption of provisional measures as a stand-alone enforcement regime, with quota administration and no BEV linkage, favours Scenario 1. A design that explicitly conditions quota allocation on BEV-undertaking progress, or attaches a minimum-price term sheet, favours Scenario 2. Restrictive rates breaching the spirit of the jointly negotiated cap would push toward Scenario 4's friction sub-branch.
- **November 2026 – May 2027 — the Official Journal.** Any implementing decision naming BYD, SAIC, Geely or Tesla moves probability mass immediately — toward Scenario 2 if coverage can reach a majority, toward Scenario 4 if it cannot. Silence through roughly Q1 2027 confirms the Commission's own recorded logic that duties hold.
- **Q2 2027 — the DS630 panel report.** Circulation before 30 June 2027 is possible but not expected; even then, pre-agreed Article 25 appeal arbitration and the Commission's discretionary response mean no legal compulsion inside the window. The report functions as a leading indicator for post-horizon dynamics, not a within-window driver.
- **Continuously — MOFCOM signals.** An initiation notice on large-displacement EU cars is the cleanest single falsifier of the no-automotive-escalation judgment. Withdrawal of the brandy minimum-price exemptions would mark the first exit from the calibrated playbook. New agricultural probes, by contrast, would reinforce the forecast.
- **Data releases through 2027 — trade indicators.** China's 42% share of extra-EU electric-car import value: a sustained fall signals displacement success or retaliatory friction worth distinguishing; a fall below the mid-30s combined with unit volumes holding near 800,000–900,000 would be the data signature of minimum prices operating before any formal act. Chinese private credit at a record 204.1% of GDP in 2026-Q1 and exports at 21.071% of GDP and rising frame Beijing's continuing capacity and incentive to negotiate rather than rupture.

### Confidence and Its Limits

Overall confidence is rated medium, for three explicit reasons. First, the 9 October 2026 understanding is a political framework, not signed text; the Commission's own statement wording remains unconfirmed on a primary record, resting on Bruegel's and MOFCOM's secondary accounts, and the safeguard investigation it presupposes had not been launched as of the research cut-off. If that framework frays, residual probability flows toward escalation; if it holds and rounds progress, toward partial deals and eventually replacement. Second, the scenario boundaries are porous at the edges: a second carve-out landing in April 2027 could arrive too late to distinguish cleanly between incremental undertakings under Scenario 1 and partial coverage under Scenario 4 before the horizon. Third, the underlying effectiveness evidence is genuinely contested, meaning the economic case could shift under either government with little warning.

What the medium confidence does not obscure is the central structural judgment: through 30 June 2027, every path to change runs through an affirmative Official Journal act by a Commission with weak reasons to act, against a counterpart that rejects the available format, among exporters declining to file — while two legal clocks, one stalled in Geneva and one ticking toward managed enforcement in Brussels, actively produce continuation. The most likely future of this dispute is its present, extended: duties in force at their original rates, talks in progress, retaliation calibrated and confined to agriculture, and the real decision deferred toward the 2029 expiry horizon — unless, on the roughly one-in-five path, the bargaining rooms of Brussels and Beijing convert Šefčovič's framework into signatures in time.

## Scenario Deep-Dive I — Duties Hold Unchanged (50%): The Inertia of the Legal Instrument

The modal outcome of this dispute as of 30 June 2027 is not a dramatic event but the absence of one: Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2754 continues to apply its original rate schedule — BYD at 17.0%, Geely at 18.8%, SAIC at 35.3%, Tesla (Shanghai) at 7.8%, other cooperating companies at 20.7% and the residual rate at 35.3%, all on top of the 10% base tariff, for a combined ceiling of up to 45% — while negotiations on price undertakings continue without producing a signed, majority-covering replacement. This chapter assigns that path a 50% probability and explains why the legal architecture of the EU trade-defence instrument, the incentives of the Chinese exporters it targets, and the structure of the October 2026 political understanding all point toward inertia rather than transformation. The status quo is not passive; it is actively produced by the interaction of a duty regime with a built-in five-year horizon, a Commission that has signalled conditional openness to undertakings without receiving offers, and Chinese exporters who have found commercially superior ways around the measures. One definitional caveat frames everything that follows: because Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/330 now exempts undertaking-holders from the duty schedule, "unchanged" in this scenario means unchanged for every exporter without an accepted undertaking — Volkswagen (Anhui) remains the sole carve-out.

### The Five-Year Clock That Binds Nothing Before the Horizon

The single most important structural fact in this forecast is temporal. The definitive countervailing duties have applied since 30 October 2024 for a five-year period running to 31 October 2029, as the Commission's TRON case database records. Under the EU's standard trade-defence procedure, the measure can only lapse or be reconfigured at an expiry review, whose request window opens in the final months of the measure's life — roughly mid-2029. No expiry-review decision point, and therefore no institutional moment at which the duties must be reaffirmed, modified or abandoned, falls before 30 June 2027. For the entire forecast window the instrument's default state is continuation. Repeal before expiry would require either a negotiated replacement (examined in Chapter 3), an interim review outcome (Chapter 4), or a unilateral political decision by the Commission — and nothing in the record suggests the last of these.

The base rate reinforces the structural prior. The Commission's 43rd annual report on anti-dumping, anti-subsidy and safeguard actions records that of 13 expiry reviews concluded in 2024, 11 ended with duties confirmed and only 2 with measures lapsing — a confirmation rate of roughly 85%. Trade-defence measures, once definitive, overwhelmingly persist to their natural horizon. The EU ended 2024 with 192 definitive measures in force and launched 33 new investigations in the same year, an institutional tempo that treats continuation as the default and termination as the exception requiring affirmative justification. Interim reviews are similarly rare — only four were initiated across the whole of 2024 — and the only one opened in this case, case R847, was initiated by the Commission on its own initiative on 4 December 2025 and limited strictly to the form of the measure, not its level. Its conclusion on 9–10 February 2026 accepted the Volkswagen (Anhui) undertaking via Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/328 and Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/330 while leaving the duty structure of Regulation 2024/2754 otherwise intact. That is the key precedent for what "modification" looks like in this file: surgical carve-outs for companies that submit offers, with the instrument itself untouched.

The WTO track, examined fully in Chapter 8, cannot disrupt this clock inside the window. The DS630 panel was composed only on 13 October 2025, and on 1 April 2026 it stated it does not expect to issue its final report before Q2 2027; both parties pre-agreed MPIA-style Article 25 appeal arbitration on 31 July 2025. Even a panel finding adverse to the EU would be followed by an arbitration phase pushing any legally compelled change well past 30 June 2027 — and even in the fastest tail case, an adverse report would change duty rates only if the Commission chose to open an interim review in response, a discretionary act, not an automatic one. The Commission's institutional incentive points the other way: instrument credibility is the stake, and a measure repealed or gutted under litigation pressure would weaken the precedent value of the entire countervailing framework. The EU therefore litigates rather than pre-empts, and litigation is slow by design.

### Why Chinese Exporters Have Not Queued for Undertakings

The most persuasive evidence for inertia is behavioural: after more than two years of duties and four months of a formal undertaking pathway, exactly one company holds an exemption. Volkswagen (Anhui) secured its company-specific undertaking via Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/328 and Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/330, adopted 9 February and published 10 February 2026, and the Commission stated that this was the sole undertaking offer formally submitted since the definitive measures were imposed. BYD, Geely, SAIC and Tesla (Shanghai) — the exporters carrying the duty schedule — have no offer on record as of the research cut-off. The Commission's guidance document of 12 January 2026 set out the conditions: per-model minimum prices referenced to the first independent customer, set by adjusting historical CIF prices by the duty margin or by reference to comparable non-subsidised EU-produced BEVs, with retroactive collection on breach [S7]. The roughly nine-week review cycle demonstrated by the VW decision shows the machinery works quickly when a qualifying offer arrives. The bottleneck is not Brussels' processing capacity; it is that no major Chinese exporter has put a compliant offer on the table.

The commercial logic explains why. An undertaking is a price floor plus quota discipline: the VW (Anhui) terms pair an annual quota with a minimum import price on all EU exports, documentation requirements and monitoring through sales reports and verification visits. That model directly caps the discount-driven volume strategy that carried Chinese brands from 50% to more than 70% of EU imports from China during 2025, while Tesla's Shanghai-built share fell from 30% in 2023 to 10% in 2025. Rhodium judges that few Chinese OEMs will follow Volkswagen (Anhui), precisely because undertakings constrain discount-driven strategies such as BYD's subsidy-plus-incentive discounts in Germany [S16]. Unit imports grew roughly 35% in 2025 to more than 900,000 vehicles; accepting a minimum price means voluntarily abandoning the pricing freedom that produced that growth.

For BYD specifically, the calculus is skewed by a loophole the duties left open: plug-in hybrids sit entirely outside the duty scope. Chinese hybrid exports to the EU reached $7.5 billion in the first half of 2026 — triple the $2.5 billion of a year earlier, per Chinese customs data reported by Reuters — and EU plug-in-hybrid imports jumped from €8.8 billion to €13.0 billion (+48%) in 2025. An exporter with a fast-growing, duty-free channel has weak incentive to accept price restraints on its dutied channel. Rhodium explicitly rates BYD's undertaking prospects low for this reason. The exception proves the logic: SAIC, carrying the top 35.3% rate and with no comparable hybrid escape valve, has the strongest nominal incentive toward an undertaking — and even SAIC has not filed.

The 9 October 2026 understanding complicates but does not overturn this logic. The Beijing deal caps Chinese hybrid exports at roughly 350,000 vehicles per year over four years via an EU safeguard with provisional measures advertised for December 2026, and commits both sides to "immediately begin negotiations on electric vehicle price undertakings". A reasonable objection — the strongest form of the case against this scenario — runs as follows: the understanding creates genuine negotiating momentum; the historical base rate for EU-China duty disputes is 2 of 2 ending in undertakings (solar 2013 full replacement; VW Anhui partial carve-out); and the hybrid cap closes BYD's outside channel, restoring its incentive to seek terms on the dutied BEV trade. This report takes that argument seriously, which is why 22% is assigned to negotiated replacement rather than a token probability.

But the momentum argument has three structural weaknesses that keep it subordinate. First, the Commission has publicly doubted that a single minimum price would counter subsidy-caused injury and insists on company-specific offers under its January 2026 guidance [S7], while MOFCOM insists on a government-to-government model with no independent company deals — the two sides are negotiating about what kind of agreement would count as an agreement. Second, the hybrid cap is enforced by a safeguard, not by an undertaking, so its restraint on BYD arrives regardless of whether BEV talks succeed; the negotiating leverage it creates for Brussels reduces the urgency of concessions but does not force a signature. Notably, the safeguard investigation had not even been launched as of the research cut-off, and whether quota allocation will be linked to BEV-undertaking progress remains unknown — an open design choice that itself signals how unsettled the linkage question is. Third, and decisively, a framework understanding is not an implementing act. Under EU law, undertakings become effective only through Official Journal decisions accepting them [S6]. As of the research cut-off, no such act beyond VW (Anhui) exists, and the nine-week review cycle means any majority-covering package would need to be substantially agreed by roughly April–May 2027 to be in force by the horizon. Momentum without submitted offers is not replacement; it is the definition of the duties-hold-plus-talks state. Bruegel's reading of the Commission's own statement under the understanding points the same way: electric-vehicle trade "will continue to be regulated through the application of either countervailing duties or price undertakings" [S25] — a formulation that keeps both instruments open and privileges neither.

### The Political Economy of Standing Still

Inertia also has a political constituency that did not exist in 2024. The October 2024 Council vote was 10 in favour, 5 against, 12 abstentions, with Germany voting against under pressure from BMW, Mercedes-Benz, the VDA and IG Metall, whose China exposure ran to about one third of 2023 German car sales. That was the moment of maximum political risk to the instrument. By 2026 the configuration reversed: Berlin joined Paris in pressing for stronger trade-defence instruments, as import pressures on the German economy eclipsed export benefits — China fell from Germany's second-largest export market in 2021 to ninth by the first half of 2026, and a record 9.3% of new cars sold in the EU in December 2025 were made in China [S16]. The Franco-German alignment removes the main member-state channel through which a rapid negotiated retreat could have been imposed on the Commission. France's protectionist coalition, anchoring the pro-tariff side since the 10-5-12 vote, gains from the duties, and Renault and Stellantis face no counter-pressure to abandon them. By mid-2026 even the German automotive lobby had shifted toward trade defence, with the member-state argument focused on the Industrial Accelerator Act's local-content requirements rather than repeal of existing duties — a debate about adding instruments, not removing them.

The fiscal dimension adds quiet reinforcement. Countervailing-duty revenue has been estimated by CEPR/VoxEU authors at roughly €500 million per quarter, about €2 billion per year, based on actual 2024–25 import patterns, accruing to the EU budget without any Council appropriation. A negotiated replacement by minimum prices would exchange this revenue stream for an administratively demanding monitoring regime that the same economists explicitly warned against, finding the duties' measurable protective effect small and statistically insignificant and noting similar Chinese share declines in tariff-free Norway, Switzerland and the UK — China's value share in EU BEV imports fell from about 55% in early 2024 to about 42% by mid-2025, but by similar or larger margins in those tariff-free markets. This contested evidence cuts both ways, but for the status quo it has a specific implication: if the duties are not demonstrably distorting trade flows, the political cost of keeping them is low, and the economic case for urgent replacement is weak. Paradoxically, an instrument with modest real effects is easier to leave in place.

China's retaliation pattern, detailed in Chapter 5, reinforces rather than undermines inertia. Every retaliatory probe has landed at final rates far below provisionals — brandy capped at 34.9% with minimum-price exemptions for major cognac houses from July 2025, pork finals of 4.9–19.8% against provisionals up to 62.4%, dairy finals reported at 7.4–11.7% against 21.9–42.7% provisionals — and Reuters reporting tied the cognac minimum-price arrangement to progress on the EU EV tariffs. MOFCOM even cut pork final rates five days after announcing the restart of EV minimum-price talks [S10]. Beijing's calibrated, reversible pressure is designed to reward negotiation, not to force immediate capitulation, and it has conspicuously never implemented the large-displacement car tariffs signalled since June 2024. Both capitals, in different ways, are managing the dispute toward a slow settlement rather than a rupture — which is precisely the environment in which the existing instrument survives.

### What Would Prove This Scenario Wrong

The scenario carries explicit verification and falsification criteria. It is confirmed if, at 30 June 2027: the original rate schedule of Regulation 2024/2754 remains in force for all exporters without accepted undertakings; no implementing act accepting undertakings covering a majority of Chinese BEV exports has appeared in the Official Journal; and China has imposed no new measures on EU automotive exports.

The leading falsification signals arrive on a dated calendar. Any new undertaking decision naming BYD, SAIC, Geely or Tesla in the Official Journal between November 2026 and May 2027 would signal that the logjam of exporter incentives has broken, moving probability mass from this scenario to the negotiated path or the residual bin. The December 2026 hybrid-safeguard decision is the nearest discriminator: adoption of provisional measures without any BEV linkage favours this scenario; a design that links quota allocation to undertaking progress, or a minimum-price term sheet accompanying it, favours replacement. A signed, majority-covering undertaking package emerging from the post-understanding negotiation rounds would directly falsify this scenario and largely resolve the dispute. Conversely, rounds that conclude without signature, or with partial-scope deals only, would confirm the central claim of this chapter: that the 9 October 2026 understanding bought time and goodwill, but the structural obstacles — the Commission's company-specific model against MOFCOM's government-to-government demand, and Chinese exporters' reluctance to price-restrain themselves — keep the legal instrument exactly where it is. In EU-China trade relations, the most likely future is usually the present, extended.

## Scenario Deep-Dive II — Undertaking Replacement (22%): The Negotiated Path and Its Structural Obstacles

The second scenario in this report's forecast — a negotiated price-undertaking or minimum-price agreement replacing the duties before 30 June 2027 — carries a 22% probability. It is the branch in which diplomacy outruns legal inertia. This chapter explains why that path is genuinely open after 9 October 2026, what its two historical precedents actually demonstrate, and why four structural obstacles hold the probability near a fifth rather than a coin flip. It closes with the verification and falsification criteria an observer should apply between November 2026 and June 2027.

### The Diplomatic Opening: What 9 October 2026 Actually Changed

The negotiated path acquired its momentum on 9 October 2026, when the EU and China reached a common understanding in Beijing. MOFCOM stated the sides would "immediately begin negotiations on electric vehicle price undertakings", and Reuters reported the deal as an effort to curb Chinese hybrid exports while keeping the EV dispute talks alive [S27]. Bruegel's reading of the Commission statement is that electric-vehicle trade "will continue to be regulated through the application of either countervailing duties or price undertakings" — language that keeps both instruments formally on the table into the forecast window [S25].

Two features of the understanding matter for scenario weighting. First, it is a political framework, not a signed text: the understanding was reached with no stated deadline, and the Commission's own statement wording remains unconfirmed, resting on secondary accounts — a fragility the contested-evidence record flags explicitly. Second, the understanding pairs the undertaking commitment with a concrete quid pro quo: the hybrid-export cap of roughly 350,000 vehicles per year over four years, to be implemented through an EU safeguard with provisional measures advertised for December 2026. China conceded managed restraint on hybrids in exchange for a negotiating track on the BEV duties. Beijing has therefore invested political capital in the undertaking talks and needs them to produce something visible — which is precisely what keeps the replacement scenario alive rather than rhetorical.

The channel also has continuity. Talks on a minimum-price plan had already restarted by 11 December 2025, when MOFCOM spokesperson He Yadong announced the resumption of government-to-government negotiations [S24]. The Commission preceded its 12 January 2026 undertaking guidance with discussions with MOFCOM framed as exploring WTO-compatible alternatives to the duties [S7]. And Trade Commissioner Maroš Šefčovič, who leads the EU-China track, has a settlement-legacy stake tied to the Beijing understanding: a signed undertaking package before mid-2027 would capstone the negotiating file he built. Diplomatic momentum, in short, is real and personally owned on the EU side.

### The Precedent Base Rate: Two-for-Two, But Read Carefully

The strongest argument for replacement is history: both prior episodes in which the EU imposed major duties on China ended in undertakings. The solar-panel dispute of 2013 — the closest structural analogue, with provisional duties of 11.8% — concluded with a Commission decision of 2 August 2013 accepting a price undertaking with volume commitments, fully displacing the duties for participating exporters. In February 2026, the VW (Anhui) carve-out arrived via Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/328 and Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/330, adopted 9–10 February 2026 after a partial interim review limited to the form of the measure, initiated on the Commission's own initiative on 4 December 2025. Two of two is a small sample, but the direction is consistent: EU-China duty confrontations have always eventually been managed into undertakings rather than frozen into permanent litigation. Notably, China itself has used undertakings as the settlement currency in this very dispute — the July 2025 brandy final ruling exempted Hennessy and Remy Martin at minimum prices alongside duties of up to 34.9% [S11] — so both sides know the instrument intimately.

Yet the two precedents differ in scope, and the difference is decisive for calibration. The solar case was a full sectoral replacement; the VW case is a company-specific carve-out that left Regulation 2024/2754's rate schedule — BYD 17.0%, Geely 18.8%, SAIC 35.3%, Tesla (Shanghai) 7.8%, other cooperating 20.7%, residual 35.3% — fully intact for every other exporter. For this scenario to resolve as *replacement*, an implementing act published in the Official Journal before 30 June 2027 must accept undertakings covering a majority of Chinese BEV exports to the EU — operationally, at minimum BYD plus at least one of Geely or SAIC. That is a much higher bar than the VW precedent clears. The Commission itself stated that VW (Anhui) submitted the sole undertaking offer since imposition of the definitive measures; no primary record shows BYD, SAIC, Geely or Tesla filing one, and the Commission signalled that the absence of a second accepted offer by roughly Q1 2027 would weigh toward the duties holding.

There is also a template argument in the scenario's favour: the mechanics exist and have been tested. The VW (Anhui) undertaking pairs an annual quota with a minimum import price on all exports to the Union, formal requirements — undertaking declarations, commercial and re-sale invoices, exports exclusively via a sole related Union importer — and monitoring through sales reports and verification visits [S6]. Acceptance can lapse on withdrawal or breach under Article 13(9) of the basic regulation. New undertakings would follow the same pattern: a partial interim review limited to the form of the measure plus implementing decisions. And the February 2026 precedent shows a roughly nine-week cycle from guidance-compliant offer to acceptance, so timing inside H1 2027 is procedurally feasible if offers arrive by early spring.

### Four Structural Obstacles: Why 22% and Not More

**The model clash.** MOFCOM insists on government-to-government negotiation, has urged the Commission not to negotiate with individual manufacturers, opposes company-level carve-outs such as the VW (Anhui) undertaking, and wants a single negotiated minimum price. The Commission's 12 January 2026 guidance is built on the opposite architecture: company-specific offers assessed "objectively, fairly, non-discriminatorily and in accordance with WTO rules" [S7], with minimum prices set either by adjusting historical CIF prices by the duty margin or by referencing comparable non-subsidised EU-produced BEVs, and retroactive duty collection on breach. A government-to-government deal would require the Commission to swallow an instrument it has publicly doubted — it has stated that a single minimum price would not counter subsidy-caused injury, a finding that varies by exporter from 7.8% to 35.3%. Each side's preferred architecture is the other's stated objection; bridging it requires one government to visibly change position, something neither has done as of the research cutoff.

**Exporter incentives.** Rhodium judges that few Chinese OEMs will follow Volkswagen (Anhui), because undertakings constrain the discount-driven volume strategies that lifted Chinese brands' share of EU imports from China from 50% to more than 70%. BYD is the pivotal case: its plug-in-hybrid exports fall entirely outside the duty's scope — hybrid exports to the EU-27 reached $7.5 billion in H1 2026, tripled from $2.5 billion a year earlier — so BYD retains a large duty-free channel an undertaking would do nothing to enlarge, while accepting minimum prices would sacrifice its BEV pricing weapon, including its subsidy-plus-incentive discounting in Germany. SAIC nominally has the strongest incentive at the 35.3% rate but has filed nothing on record; Tesla's 7.8% individual rate buys too little to justify the constraint. Without BYD, no package covers a majority of exports — the scenario's resolution criterion fails by construction.

**No forcing deadline.** The WTO DS630 panel's final report is not expected before Q2 2027, its timetable having been extended at the parties' request on 1 April 2026, and the parties pre-committed on 31 July 2025 to MPIA-style Article 25 appeal arbitration, which forecloses any appeal-into-the-void shortcut. Neither party therefore faces litigation compulsion inside the window — but, symmetrically, neither faces a legal deadline. Negotiations without a forcing event tend to drift, and the October understanding was reached with no stated deadline at all. The nearest dated discriminator is the December 2026 hybrid-safeguard decision: adoption without any BEV linkage favours the duties holding, while linkage or a minimum-price term sheet favours replacement. Whether the safeguard's quota allocation connects to BEV-undertaking progress is unknown — and it is the single most informative thing to watch in early 2027.

**The internal EU coalition.** By 2026 Germany had reversed its 2024 vote against the duties and joined France in pressing for stronger trade defence — the Merz–Macron letter and the Franco-German car-industry deal — with member-state argument reportedly centring on the Industrial Accelerator Act's local-content requirements rather than repeal of the existing duties. The political channel that once could have forced a rapid negotiated retreat, German industry lobbying, has closed. France's protectionist coalition, anchored by the Council vote of 4 October 2024 (10 for, 5 against, 12 abstentions), loses appetite for any settlement that erodes protection — and French exporters remain exposed to China's calibrated retaliation, with the provisional dairy duties of up to 42.7% falling partly on French cheese, giving Paris an incentive to bundle any undertaking deal with retaliation relief and hold out for a protection-equivalence floor.

### Steelmanning Replacement — and the Most Plausible Deal Shape

The strongest version of the replacement case deserves a fair hearing, because it explains why this branch carries the second-largest probability mass. China has demonstrated both a settlement practice and a linked-rewards structure: Reuters reported that Beijing would finalise the cognac minimum-price arrangement only if progress were made on the EU EV tariffs, and MOFCOM cut pork final rates to 4.9–19.8% (from provisionals of up to 62.4%) just days after the minimum-price talks restarted in December 2025 — evidence that Beijing rations concessions in step with EV-talk progress. If Beijing concludes that a minimum-price regime is commercially superior to duties for its exporters, it can instruct state-linked OEMs to file offers through the Commission's open-call guidance channel, converting the government-to-government dispute into a wave of company submissions the Commission can accept under its existing model without conceding its architecture. That hybrid path — MOFCOM orchestrating, companies formally filing — is the mechanism most likely to dissolve the model clash and produce the scenario's resolution.

Internal scenario deliberation among perspectives representing the Commission, MOFCOM, the German government and Volkswagen consistently pointed to exactly this landing zone: a hybrid architecture in which a government-to-government framework serves as the procedural channel while verification, auditor access and company-level equivalence remain EU requirements. The same deliberation, however, also surfaced the counterweight that anchors this report's discount: from BYD's vantage, a 17.0% duty is manageable while PHEV exports sit outside the tariff's scope, so the largest exporter has no urgent reason to press for a deal that would cap its discounting strategy. The deliberation's aggregate tilt toward a high replacement probability — substantially above this report's 22% — is explicitly rejected here because it rests on negotiating goodwill that the observable record does not yet corroborate: as of the research cutoff, there is one accepted undertaking, zero additional submissions on record, and an unfulfilled commitment to "immediately begin" talks whose first test (the December 2026 safeguard decision) has not yet occurred. Goodwill without filings is not a settlement.

### Verification and Falsification

The scenario resolves as *undertaking replacement* if, before 30 June 2027, an implementing act published in the Official Journal accepts undertakings covering a majority of Chinese BEV exports to the EU — operationally, decisions naming BYD plus at least one of Geely or SAIC, following the Decision (EU) 2026/328 pattern of a partial interim review limited to the form of the measure plus implementing decisions.

It falsifies — with probability mass flowing back to the duties-hold scenario — if:

- Negotiation rounds through spring 2027 conclude without signature, or with partial-scope deals only (single-company carve-outs short of majority coverage), which classify to the residual bin;
- The Official Journal carries no new undertaking decision naming BYD, SAIC, Geely or Tesla between November 2026 and May 2027;
- The December 2026 hybrid-safeguard decision is adopted as a stand-alone enforcement measure with no BEV-undertaking linkage;
- MOFCOM hardens rather than softens its rejection of the company-specific model.

Conversely, early signals favouring replacement include: a burst of company filings under the 12 January 2026 guidance's open-call format; a safeguard text whose quota allocation references BEV-undertaking progress; Chinese retaliation relief — withdrawal or widening of brandy minimum-price exemptions — arriving in tandem with visible negotiating progress; and any Commission notice initiating a fresh partial interim review limited to the form of the measure, the procedural vehicle through which the February 2026 carve-out travelled and through which any replacement package would have to travel before 30 June 2027.

In sum: the negotiated path is real, procedurally tested, and diplomatically mandated by the 9 October 2026 understanding. But it must overcome a model clash between Brussels and Beijing, an incentive gap at the top of the exporter league table, the absence of any forcing deadline, and a Franco-German coalition that wants settlement without erosion of protection. Those obstacles are why this report prices replacement at 22% — high enough to take seriously, too constrained to call likely.

## Scenario Deep-Dive III & IV — Interim Review (11%) and the Residual/Escalation Bin (17%)

The two branches examined here are the ones forecasters most often mishandle. The interim-review scenario (11%) looks superficially plausible — trade-defence instruments are reviewed constantly, and China is actively litigating the measure at the WTO — yet the legal clock and the Commission's own doctrinal posture suppress it before 30 June 2027. The residual bin (17%) is by construction the catch-all, but it has positive content: it is where partial-scope undertakings, hybrid-safeguard friction, and negotiation-without-signature outcomes actually live. This chapter dissects the mechanics of each branch, defends the probabilities, and identifies the observable events that would move an outcome into or out of them.

### Branch III: The Interim-Review Path (11%) — A Review Without a Trigger

An interim review under the basic anti-subsidy Regulation (EU) 2016/1037 can adjust duty rates upward or downward without repealing the instrument. The resolution criterion is concrete: before 30 June 2027, the Commission publishes a notice in the Official Journal initiating or concluding an interim review of Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2754, or an amending regulation adjusting the rate schedule — BYD 17.0%, Geely 18.8%, SAIC 35.3%, Tesla (Shanghai) 7.8%, other cooperating companies 20.7%, non-cooperating 35.3%. The base rate for such activity is thin: the Commission's 43rd annual report records four interim reviews initiated across all instruments in 2024, with outcomes unreported, spread over 192 definitive measures in force at end-2024.

There is, importantly, already one precedent review in this very case — and it cuts both ways. The Commission initiated a partial interim review limited to the form of the measure on 4 December 2025 (case R847) and concluded it on 9–10 February 2026, accepting the Volkswagen (Anhui) undertaking via Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/328 and Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/330. That episode proves the Commission will use the review machinery — but only to graft a carve-out onto the regime, leaving the duty structure intact for every other exporter. A form-of-measure review that adds an exemption is categorically different from a review that re-derives subsidy margins or re-runs the injury analysis, and only the latter would satisfy this scenario's criteria.

**Why the WTO clock cannot force the Commission's hand.** China's challenge, DS630, has moved methodically: consultations requested 4 November 2024, panel established 25 April 2025, composed 13 October 2025 with 17 third parties including the United States. The panel's timetable was extended on 1 April 2026 at the parties' request, and its final report is not expected before Q2 2027. Even the fastest tail case — an early Q2 2027 report — produces no legal obligation inside the window, because on 31 July 2025 both parties pre-agreed MPIA-style arbitration under DSU Article 25 to decide any appeal. An adverse report followed by pre-committed arbitration cannot yield an adopted ruling, let alone Commission compliance action, before 30 June 2027. The WTO track is a decision point for *after* the horizon, not before it.

**Why no domestic constituency demands a review.** Dutied exporters have a cheaper route: an undertaking offer under the 12 January 2026 guidance, which promises objective, fair, non-discriminatory assessment consistent with WTO rules [S7]. A review, by contrast, could raise rates as easily as lower them — SAIC at 35.3% has theoretical upside from review, but the practical experience of the VW (Anhui) cycle shows the carve-out channel operates in roughly nine weeks, faster and less risky than a full review. EU producers, protected by the duties, will not request one. And the member-state politics that might once have pressured Brussels has reversed: Germany voted against the duties in the 10-5-12 Council vote of 4 October 2024 but by mid-2026 had converged with France on demanding stronger trade-defence instruments, partly because a record 9.3% of new cars sold in the EU in December 2025 were made in China. When Paris and Berlin both prefer the instrument as it stands, the political customer for a review disappears.

A steelman deserves airing. One could argue that if undertaking talks collapse, the Commission would need a face-saving device — a review that modestly adjusts rates while keeping the instrument, presenting movement to Beijing without conceding repeal. That is genuinely the most plausible route into this scenario, and it is why the branch carries 11% rather than something negligible. But note the conditioning: it requires the negotiated path to fail first, which is itself not the modal outcome, and it requires the Commission to depart from its stated position that the current rates offset subsidy-caused injury. Deliberation among scenario-panel perspectives reinforces this reading: perspectives aligned with the Commission and the Trade Commissioner emphasized that review initiation follows evidentiary triggers under Article 19(2) — changed subsidy levels, changed injury — not political convenience, and that the expiry-review preparation track (due around July 2029, three months before the 31 October 2029 expiry ) is the next scheduled institutional moment, far beyond this horizon.

### Branch IV: The Residual Bin (17%) — Where Mixed Outcomes Actually Live

The residual bin is not a dustbin; it is the third-most-probable single destination after "duties hold" (50%) and "full replacement" (22%), and it deserves positive content. Three sub-branches dominate.

**Sub-branch one: partial-scope undertakings without majority coverage.** The most likely residual path is that the negotiations launched under the 9 October 2026 Beijing understanding — under which MOFCOM said the sides would "immediately begin negotiations" on EV price undertakings — produce one or two additional company-specific carve-outs on the VW (Anhui) model, covering a minority of Chinese BEV exports. A deal naming BYD alone (at 17.0%) would materially change trade flows without meeting scenario II's threshold of majority coverage. The precedent base rate points exactly this way: of the two historical EU-China duty episodes ending in undertakings, one was a full replacement (solar 2013 ) and one was precisely this partial carve-out pattern. If BYD, Geely or Tesla Shanghai submits an offer after the December 2026 hybrid-safeguard decision, the nine-week VW cycle suggests the Commission could conclude it before mid-2027 — landing the outcome here, not in scenario II.

The structural obstacle is exporter incentives. Rhodium judges that few Chinese OEMs will follow Volkswagen (Anhui), because undertakings constrain discount-driven strategies, and because BYD's plug-in-hybrid exports fall outside the duty's scope entirely, lowering its incentive to seek one [S16]. Meanwhile Chinese exporters depend on MOFCOM, which insists on government-to-government negotiation only and has urged the Commission not to negotiate with individual manufacturers — directly opposing the company-level carve-out model. This incompatibility means the most probable *non-status-quo* outcome is not a clean replacement but an awkward hybrid: perhaps a second carve-out, perhaps a framework agreement on verification architecture without accepted offers, with the duties continuing to apply to everyone else. That is residual-bin territory by definition.

**Sub-branch two: hybrid-safeguard friction altering the trade-policy mix.** The 9 October 2026 understanding caps Chinese hybrid exports at roughly 350,000 vehicles per year over four years, implemented via an EU safeguard with provisional measures advertised for December 2026. The substitution dynamic driving this is well documented: EU plug-in-hybrid imports jumped from €8.8 billion to €13.0 billion (+48%) in 2025, and Chinese hybrid exports to the EU-27 reached $7.5 billion in H1 2026, tripled from $2.5 billion a year earlier. If Chinese automakers front-load against the cap and December's provisional measures bite hard, the trade-policy environment of spring 2027 could look very different from October 2026 — yet the BEV countervailing duties themselves would remain untouched, placing the outcome in this bin rather than any other. Whether quota allocation under the safeguard gets linked to BEV-undertaking progress is, on the current record, unknown — and that uncertainty itself is a reason the residual bin carries weight. The December 2026 decision is the nearest dated discriminator in the entire forecast: adoption without any BEV linkage reinforces the duties-hold path, while linkage or a minimum-price term sheet pushes toward replacement or, if incomplete, into this bin.

**Sub-branch three: negotiations that end without signature.** Rounds can conclude without a signed package — deadlock with the duties intact and talks nominally alive. Scenario-deliberation perspectives sketched this world in detail: a draft undertaking decision transmitted to member states but made conditional on a first joint on-site audit within sixty days, on independent-auditor access to pricing data, and on demonstrated equivalence with the 2024 duty levels; French linkage of Council approval to the removal of cognac and dairy counter-measures; missed delivery deadlines on verification transparency. In that world the horizon arrives with a conditional, unsigned text, the duties fully in force, and no review notice — failing scenario II's resolution criteria and, absent a review, scenario III's too. It lands here. The formal position of the WTO panel is also relevant: any negotiated solution would be notified to the DSB under DSU Article 3.6, and the panel process runs independently of the talks — meaning litigation pressure cannot be traded away inside the window either.

### Why the Escalation Sub-Branch Is Small: The Calibration Record

The residual bin also absorbs an escalation tail, and the calibration here rests on China's observed retaliation pattern: calibrated, reversible, and consistently below provisional levels.

- **Brandy:** provisional duties up to 39.0% (October 2024) became finals up to 34.9% from 5 July 2025, with minimum-price exemptions sparing the major cognac houses.
- **Pork:** provisionals of 15.6–62.4% (September 2025) became finals of 4.9–19.8% on 17 December 2025 — a cut of roughly two-thirds at the top end.
- **Dairy:** provisionals of 21.9–42.7% collected from 23 December 2025 became reported finals of 7.4–11.7% effective 13 February 2026.

Every episode shows the same signature: loud provisionals, quiet finals, exemptions for the biggest players, and relief explicitly conditioned on EV-talk progress — Reuters reported China would finalize the cognac minimum-price arrangement only if progress was made on the EU EV tariffs [S11]. This is retaliation as bargaining currency, not decoupling. At least two-thirds of threatened probes reached duties during 2023–2025, but always in agriculture, never in automotive.

The automotive weapon — tariffs on large-displacement EU cars, urged by Chinese automakers since June 2024 — remains unimplemented, with no record of any MOFCOM initiation as of the research cut-off. It functions as a deterrent card held rather than played. The mechanism is straightforward: escalation against EU automotive exports would destroy the negotiating framework MOFCOM itself demanded on 9 October 2026, and would strike German OEMs whose roughly one-third China sales exposure gives Beijing leverage precisely *because* it is unused. Converting that leverage into actual duties destroys its value. Scenario-deliberation perspectives aligned with MOFCOM framed the position identically: negotiation first, calibrated response, full legal rights reserved under DS630 if talks collapse — escalation as contingent threat, not baseline policy.

The conditional structure matters for calibration. Escalation probability is not constant: it rises sharply if undertaking talks visibly collapse before mid-2027, in which case the residual bin's composition shifts from partial deals and hybrid friction toward deadlock plus renewed probes. But the modal path — talks continuing, however slowly, under the Beijing framework Bruegel read as keeping EV trade regulated by "either countervailing duties or price undertakings" [S25] — keeps the calibrated pattern in place. This is why the escalation tail sits inside a 17% residual bin rather than standing as a large independent scenario.

### Observable Triggers and Verification

Between November 2026 and May 2027, the following events would move outcomes between branches:

- **Into scenario III:** any Official Journal notice initiating or concluding an interim review of Regulation 2024/2754, or an amending regulation changing any company rate. Note carefully: a review limited to accepting a new undertaking (the R847 pattern) does *not* qualify — it is residual-bin or scenario II material depending on coverage.
- **Into the residual bin (partial-deal sub-branch):** an implementing act accepting a second undertaking naming BYD, SAIC, Geely or Tesla without majority export coverage.
- **Into the escalation sub-branch:** a MOFCOM initiation notice on large-displacement EU cars — the cleanest single falsifier of the no-automotive-escalation judgment — or withdrawal of the brandy minimum-price exemptions.
- **Hybrid friction:** December 2026 provisional safeguard measures materially above a monitoring regime, or evidence that front-loaded PHEV inventories breach the ~350,000 cap.
- **Contingent escalation marker:** a sustained fall in China's 42% share of extra-EU electric-car import value in 2026 data, which would signal either displacement success or retaliatory dynamics worth distinguishing.

A final calibration note on the softness of these numbers. The 11% for interim review is the most defensible figure in the forecast: it rests on a specific legal trigger that either appears in the Official Journal or does not. The 17% residual is deliberately the least firm — it absorbs both genuinely mixed outcomes and the uncertainty stemming from the contested durability of the 9 October 2026 understanding, whose Commission-side statement text remains unconfirmed in primary form. If that understanding frays, residual probability flows toward the escalation sub-branch; if it holds and rounds progress, it flows toward partial deals and eventually scenario II. Either way, the most probable residents of this bin before 30 June 2027 are incremental carve-outs and managed hybrid friction — not rupture.

## Chinese Retaliation: Calibrated Pressure Without Automotive Escalation

**Chinese Retaliation: Calibrated Pressure Without Automotive Escalation**

The retaliation question in this dispute has a clear asymmetric structure: China has proven willing and able to impose trade pain on the European Union, but every measure it has actually implemented has landed in agriculture and spirits — sectors chosen for political leverage rather than economic symmetry — while the automotive sector, where German OEMs hold roughly one third of their 2023 sales in China, has remained untouched. This chapter forecasts that this pattern holds through 30 June 2027: Chinese retaliatory measures against EU automotive exports before that date are unlikely, though calibrated non-automotive pressure continues and could intensify at the margin if undertaking negotiations stall.

### The Retaliation Playbook: Loud Provisionals, Quiet Finals

China's retaliation record since the EU imposed definitive countervailing duties on 30 October 2024 follows a consistent choreography: announce dramatic provisional rates, then settle at final rates far below them, with exemptions for the most exposed firms. The pattern is visible in all three files.

On brandy, provisional duties of up to 39.0% were announced in October 2024, but the final anti-dumping ruling of 4 July 2025 set duties of up to 34.9% for five years from 5 July 2025 while sparing the major cognac houses — Hennessy and Rémy Martin were exempted at undisclosed minimum prices — and refunding deposits from the provisional collection. On pork, provisional rates of 15.6%–62.4% from September 2025 gave way to final duties of just 4.9%–19.8% imposed on 16 December 2025 after an 18-month investigation. On dairy, provisionals of 21.9%–42.7% collected from 23 December 2025 were followed by reported final rates of 7.4%–11.7% effective 13 February 2026 — cuts of roughly two-thirds, though these final rates rest on press reporting by Caixin and The Straits Times rather than a confirmed primary record, a caveat that itself matters for anyone tracking the retaliation signal.

The mechanism behind the choreography is bargaining, not legal recalibration. Reuters reported on 27 June 2025 that China would finalise the cognac minimum-price arrangement only if progress were made on the EU electric-vehicle tariffs, and the pork final rates were cut five days after MOFCOM announced in December 2025 that EV minimum-price talks had restarted [S24]. In other words, each duty file functions as settlement currency: the provisionals establish the threat, the finals establish the price, and the exemptions reward European constituencies that lobby Brussels for a deal. This is why French exports bear the brandy and dairy duties specifically — the measures are aimed at the protectionist coalition's home base, constraining Paris through retaliation exposure rather than targeting the Union as a whole.

Crucially, every element of this playbook is reversible. Minimum-price undertakings can be tightened or revoked; rates can be re-examined; exemptions can be withdrawn. Nothing China has imposed forecloses a settlement — which is precisely the point. A government planning genuine escalation does not refund deposits and exempt the largest exporters.

### The Unplayed Card: Large-Displacement Car Tariffs

The sharpest retaliatory threat in the file has been automotive since June 2024, when Chinese automakers urged Beijing to raise tariffs on imported EU gasoline cars with large engines, and a Chinese business group warned that temporary extra tariffs on large-displacement imports were under consideration. More than two years later, as of 20 October 2026, no such measure has been announced or implemented. The threat has sat on the table through the entire lifecycle of the dispute — through the October 2024 duty vote, the WTO panel composition, the dairy and pork finals, and the 9 October 2026 Beijing understanding — without ever being picked up.

The explanation is deterrence logic. Large-displacement car tariffs would land overwhelmingly on BMW and Mercedes-Benz, the German OEMs whose China exposure (~1/3 of 2023 sales) made Berlin the duties' leading opponent in 2024. Playing that card would punish the EU member state most sympathetic to Beijing's position — and by 2026 that calculation has inverted: Germany under Chancellor Merz has joined France in pressing for stronger trade-defence instruments, removing the political reward Beijing would once have harvested from sparing Berlin. Meanwhile, China's own automotive position in Europe is improving despite the duties: Rhodium reports Chinese car imports into Europe accelerating a year and a half after the duties were applied, with a record 9.3% of new cars sold in the EU in December 2025 made in China, and Chinese brands' share of EU imports from China rising from 50% to more than 70%. Escalating against EU cars would invite EU counter-measures that threaten this gains trajectory, and it would blow up the negotiation track MOFCOM just committed to on 9 October 2026, when the sides agreed to "immediately begin negotiations on electric vehicle price undertakings".

The 9 October 2026 understanding is itself the strongest single argument against automotive escalation before mid-2027. Bruegel reads the Commission's statement as leaving EV trade regulated through either countervailing duties or price undertakings — an explicitly managed framework that buys time on both sides [S25]. A MOFCOM initiation notice on large-displacement EU cars in the weeks or months after signing such an understanding would destroy Beijing's credibility in the very talks it demanded. That said, the understanding is a framework, not signed text; its durability is contested, and this report's overall confidence rating of medium reflects that fragility.

### Estimating the Escalation Probability: Low, but Conditioned

Against the base rates, this report estimates the probability of Chinese retaliatory measures directly targeting EU automotive exports before 30 June 2027 as low — consistent with the report's scenario allocation, in which the escalation sub-branch sits inside the 17% residual bin rather than as a standalone high-probability path. Three structural findings support this estimate. First, the historical pattern: 2 of 2 prior Chinese retaliation episodes after EU trade measures produced investigations within months, but all implemented duties landed in agriculture and spirits, never in the automotive sector. Second, the constraint structure: China's exports of goods and services stood at 21.071% of GDP in 2025, and its hybrid exports to the EU-27 tripled to $7.5 billion in H1 2026 — Beijing is winning the trade it cares about without escalation, which weakens the case for risk-taking. Third, the negotiation state: talks are active, with a named next-stage process and a Commission that has already accepted one undertaking, from Volkswagen (Anhui), in a roughly nine-week review cycle.

The strongest counter-argument — and it deserves a fair hearing — is that China's restraint is conditional, not principled. MOFCOM holds calibrated retaliation cards and could add automotive measures if talks with the Commission collapse while the EU duties remain. The signalling infrastructure for escalation already exists: the June 2024 large-engine warnings, the brandy undertaking exemptions that can be withdrawn, and the demonstrated willingness to open new probes within weeks of adverse EU moves. If the EV price-undertaking negotiations that began after 9 October 2026 collapse acrimoniously — say, over the Commission's insistence on company-specific offers versus MOFCOM's government-to-government model — the probability of automotive retaliation would rise sharply, and a fresh MOFCOM initiation notice on large-engine cars would be the clean falsifier for any forecast of continued restraint. The low estimate, in short, is conditional on talks remaining alive in some form, which the modal duties-hold-while-talking path (50%) and the undertaking path (22%) jointly make about three-quarters likely.

### Monitoring Indicators Through 30 June 2027

Four observable signals would move this assessment, each dated and checkable:

- **A MOFCOM initiation notice on large-displacement EU cars** — any announcement in the Official Chinese channels between now and June 2027 would flip the escalation assessment from low to material, given it has been signalled but unplayed since June 2024.
- **Withdrawal or tightening of brandy minimum-price exemptions** — the cognac undertakings are the canary in the retaliation coal mine; their quiet maintenance signals continued bargaining, while revocation would mark the first step out of the calibrated playbook.
- **New non-automotive probes** — additional MOFCOM investigations (wine, polysilicon, or other files) would extend calibrated pressure without crossing the automotive line, reinforcing rather than falsifying the forecast.
- **China's share of extra-EU electric-car import value** — Eurostat recorded China at 42% of extra-EU electric-car imports in 2025; a sustained fall in the 2026 data would indicate either effective displacement or trade rerouting, both relevant to Beijing's incentive to escalate.

The base case that emerges is unglamorous but well-evidenced: China keeps its agricultural duties in force at their softened final rates, keeps the cognac exemptions quietly administered, keeps the large-engine tariff threat unimplemented, and concentrates its pressure on the negotiating table where Šefčovič and MOFCOM sit. Escalation to EU automotive exports remains a real card — but it is the card Beijing plays only if the table is swept away.

## Key Actors and the Negotiation Battlefield: Commission, MOFCOM, and the Commissioner's Legacy Stake

The outcome of the EU-China electric-vehicle tariff dispute will not be decided by a court, a market, or a public opinion campaign. It will be decided in a small number of bargaining rooms in Brussels and Beijing by a small number of actors, and the distribution of formal authority among those actors largely explains why the modal outcome of this report — duties holding at 50% while negotiations continue — is far more probable than either a full negotiated replacement (22%) or a WTO-driven modification. This chapter maps that battlefield: the Commission's monopoly over the legal instrument, MOFCOM's insistence on a government-to-government model the Commission cannot easily accept, Šefčovič's personal stake in converting the October 2026 understanding into a settlement, and the Volkswagen (Anhui) first-mover precedent that has so far produced exactly one follower — no one.

### DG TRADE: The Monopoly Holder at the Centre of Every Path

Every branch of the forecast runs through the European Commission's trade directorate-general, and this is a structural point, not a rhetorical one. Only the Commission can accept an undertaking in lieu of duties, initiate or conclude an interim review, and defend the measure in WTO litigation [S1][S7]. Its 12 January 2026 Guidance Document promises that undertaking offers will be assessed objectively, fairly, non-discriminatorily and in accordance with WTO rules — but the assessment is solely the Commission's to make [S7]. Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2754 — with its company-specific schedule of BYD 17.0%, Geely 18.8%, SAIC 35.3%, Tesla (Shanghai) 7.8%, cooperating 20.7% and residual 35.3% — remains in force until 31 October 2029, and no expiry-review decision point arises before the 30 June 2027 horizon. That timetable means the Commission is under no institutional compulsion to act at all: it can administer the duties, collect roughly €500 million per quarter in revenue, and let the negotiations run.

The Commission has nonetheless deliberately kept a second door open. The January 2026 guidance sets a demanding template: per-model minimum prices set either by adjusting historical CIF values by the duty margin or by referencing comparable non-subsidised EU-produced BEVs, with retroactive collection on breach. And the Commission used that door exactly once — the partial interim review it initiated on its own initiative on 4 December 2025 (case R847, limited to the form of the measure) concluded on 9–10 February 2026 with the acceptance of Volkswagen (Anhui)'s undertaking under Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/328 and Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/330. That undertaking has four components: an annual quota; a minimum import price on all exports to the Union; formal requirements including undertaking declarations, commercial and re-sale invoices, and exports exclusively via a sole related Union importer; and monitoring through sales reports and verification visits. The Commission itself stated that this was the sole undertaking offer formally submitted since the definitive measures were imposed.

This produces a peculiar asymmetry that shapes everything forecast in this report. The Commission controls the only legal channel through which the duties can change before mid-2027, but it has recorded a substantive doubt — that a single government-negotiated minimum price would counter subsidy-caused injury — that caps how far it will go down the negotiated path. It is simultaneously the gatekeeper of the status quo and the gatekeeper of the exit, and its recorded behaviour (one acceptance in fifteen months, via a roughly nine-week review cycle measured from the EUR-Lex acts) suggests an institution that moves incrementally and case-by-case rather than by grand bargain. Any new undertaking would follow the February 2026 pattern: a partial interim review limited to the form of the measure plus an implementing decision, requiring new company submissions and Commission decisions inside the first half of 2027. That institutional temperament is the single strongest argument behind the 50% duties-hold scenario.

### MOFCOM: The Counterparty That Rejects the Commission's Model

Across the table sits China's Ministry of Commerce, and the central bargaining friction is not about tariff levels but about *who signs*. MOFCOM insists on government-to-government negotiation and has explicitly urged the Commission not to negotiate with individual manufacturers — a position that puts it directly at odds with the company-specific undertaking model the Commission codified in its guidance. MOFCOM opposes the very mechanism (the VW (Anhui) carve-out) that is the Commission's only demonstrated path to modification, and it wants a single negotiated minimum price, which is precisely the instrument the Commission doubts would offset injury. Each side's preferred settlement format is the other's red line. Chinese exporters, for their part, depend on MOFCOM's position: no independent company deals are on the table from Beijing's side.

Yet MOFCOM is also the actor that has kept the talks alive. Its spokesperson announced on 11 December 2025 that minimum-price talks had restarted, and under the 9 October 2026 Beijing understanding MOFCOM agreed to "immediately begin negotiations" on electric-vehicle price undertakings alongside discussion of investment cooperation. The understanding itself — capping Chinese hybrid exports at roughly 350,000 vehicles per year over four years via an EU safeguard with provisional measures advertised for December 2026, and improving Chinese market access for about €4 billion of EU products — was described by Bruegel as a welcome buying of time, with the Commission's statement read as leaving EV trade regulated through either countervailing duties or price undertakings. Both instruments remain open, which is exactly what a negotiating framework rather than a settlement looks like. Notably, the framework is a political understanding, not a signed text, and its statement wording remains unconfirmed on a primary record — the reading rests on secondary accounts.

MOFCOM also controls the retaliation track, and its record there reveals its negotiating style. Every retaliation file — brandy (final duties up to 34.9% from 5 July 2025, with minimum-price exemptions for the major cognac houses), pork (finals of 4.9%–19.8% against provisionals of 15.6%–62.4%), dairy (finals reported at 7.4%–11.7% after provisionals of 21.9%–42.7%) — landed well below provisional levels, with agricultural rather than automotive exports bearing the burden. Reuters reported that China would finalise the cognac minimum-price arrangement only if progress were made on the EU EV tariffs [S11], making undertakings themselves a form of settlement currency between the two sides. China also administers those brandy minimum-price undertakings with undisclosed price levels and review terms, meaning it can tighten or revoke them as a lever. Large-displacement car tariffs, signalled by Chinese automakers since June 2024, have no implementing record as of 20 October 2026. The pattern is calibrated, reversible pressure aimed at strengthening MOFCOM's hand in the room where the real decision will be made.

### Šefčovič's Legacy Stake: Why the Negotiated Path Stays Alive

The 22% weight this report assigns to a full negotiated replacement is not driven by exporter demand, which is conspicuously absent. It is driven substantially by one man's incentive structure. Maroš Šefčovič, the EU Trade Commissioner, personally reached the 9 October 2026 Beijing understanding with MOFCOM — the hybrid cap, the €4 billion market-access package, and the commitment to immediately begin EV price-undertaking negotiations. He leads the Commission in accepting undertakings, running reviews and defending DS630, meaning every track of this dispute runs through his portfolio. That framework is his signature achievement in the China file, and its value to him depends on converting it into a durable settlement. A framework that produces only deferral is a partial legacy; a framework that produces an accepted undertaking covering the bulk of Chinese BEV exports is a historic one. His incentive is therefore to keep negotiating, to bridge the format dispute between the Commission's per-company model and MOFCOM's government-to-government demand, and to find a hybrid — for instance, a governmentally-agreed floor implemented through company-specific undertakings on the VW template — that both sides can sign.

This is a genuine force, but it must be weighed honestly against its limits. A Commissioner's legacy incentive cannot manufacture offers that exporters decline to make, and it cannot override the Commission's recorded legal doubt about minimum prices. It can, however, determine the pace and persistence of the talks — which is why the negotiated path's probability sits far above what pure exporter behaviour would justify, while remaining well below what negotiation momentum alone would suggest. The internal scenario deliberation conducted for this report projected a substantially higher probability of undertaking replacement, but that projection rested on the bargaining-room dynamics of a Commission-MOFCOM-Volkswagen-German-government quadrangle closing a deal, and it systematically underweighted the structural obstacles documented on the primary record: no submitted offers beyond VW (Anhui), the Commission's injury doubt, and MOFCOM's opposition to company carve-outs. This report discounts that deliberation accordingly, and the divergence is flagged in Chapter 10 as the principal source of forecast uncertainty. The deliberation did yield one robust qualitative conclusion worth recording: the perspectives representing the Commission, MOFCOM, Volkswagen and the German government dominated the negotiation agenda throughout, while the WTO panel was peripheral to every decisive moment. The dispute will be settled, if at all, between Brussels and Beijing — not in Geneva.

### VW (Anhui)'s First-Mover Advantage and the BYD Question

Volkswagen's position on this battlefield is unique, and its February 2026 undertaking has quietly reshaped the incentives of every other player. By securing an annual quota plus minimum import price that exempts it from duties that otherwise reach 45% when combined with the 10% base car tariff, VW (Anhui) holds a cost position its dutied rivals — BYD at 17.0%, Geely at 18.8%, SAIC at 35.3% — do not. The mechanics cut both ways: the undertaking binds VW to pricing discipline, sole-importer routing and verification visits, and acceptance can lapse on withdrawal under Article 13(9). But VW's structural interest now runs toward the undertaking regime holding and either staying exclusive or having rivals join on comparable terms; its advantage erodes if the duties are wholly replaced by a general minimum-price deal everyone enjoys. Volkswagen is simultaneously the Commission's demonstration case that the company-specific model works and, because it is a German multinational, a political bridge between Berlin and Brussels — one reason the German government, examined in Chapter 7, has converged toward the instrument it once opposed.

The open question is whether anyone follows. The primary record shows no undertaking offer from BYD, SAIC, Geely or Tesla as of 20 October 2026. Rhodium judges that few Chinese OEMs will follow VW, precisely because undertakings constrain the discount-driven strategies that have driven their growth — BYD's subsidy-plus-incentive discounts in Germany being the exemplar — and because BYD's plug-in-hybrid exports fall entirely outside the duty's scope, giving it a duty-free channel that an undertaking would do nothing to widen [S16]. Chinese brands' share of EU imports from China rose from about 50% to more than 70% in 2025, evidence that the current regime has not forced a strategic retreat: Chinese cars kept a record 9.3% of EU new-car sales in December 2025. For BYD, applying means trading pricing freedom for duty relief it may not urgently need; for SAIC at 35.3%, the calculus differs and may eventually force an application — but SAIC has not filed one, and Tesla, whose Shanghai-built share of EU imports from China fell from 30% in 2023 to 10% in 2025, has its own reasons to focus elsewhere.

The February 2026 precedent nonetheless establishes something important: the machinery works, and it works fast. From initiation of the interim review on 4 December 2025 to acceptance on 9–10 February 2026, the cycle ran roughly nine weeks. If a major exporter submitted an offer in, say, the first quarter of 2027, the Commission could legally accept it before the horizon. This is why the verification criteria in Chapter 2 focus on the Official Journal between November 2026 and May 2027: any second implementing decision naming BYD, SAIC, Geely or Tesla would move probability mass out of the duties-hold scenario immediately, and the Commission's own recorded logic — that absence of a second accepted offer by roughly the first quarter of 2027 weighs toward duties holding — makes this the cleanest single discriminator in the entire forecast. Absent that, VW's first-mover position remains an isolated carve-out, which under this report's taxonomy is a variant of the status quo, not a replacement of it.

### The Battlefield in Summary

The actor map resolves into a clear geometry. The Commission sits at the centre because every legal path runs through it; MOFCOM is its indispensable counterparty because no settlement survives Beijing's refusal to sign; Šefčovič supplies the momentum that keeps the negotiated path live; Volkswagen holds the one precedent and profits from its scarcity; and the exporters whose behaviour would actually determine whether the duty regime erodes — BYD above all — have so far chosen the discount-driven status quo over the undertaking template. Around them, member-state politics has moved from contest to consensus: France voted for the duties in October 2024 and presses for stronger trade defence partly because Chinese-made cars hit a record 9.3% of EU sales, while Germany, having voted against in 2024, joined the Franco-German trade-defence push by 2026 — a realignment examined in the next chapter that removes the main political channel which once could have forced a rapid negotiated retreat. The WTO panel, composed 13 October 2025 with a report not expected before Q2 2027 and pre-agreed Article 25 appeal arbitration beyond that, is the least consequential actor inside this window: it constrains rhetoric more than outcomes. When this report's probability weights are read against this map, the logic is straightforward — the actor with the monopoly on change has weak reasons to use it, the actor with the strongest reasons to change the regime cannot do so unilaterally and rejects the available format, and the actors who could trigger change mechanically are declining the invitation. Regulatory inertia plus unfinished negotiation is not a failure of imagination; it is what this configuration of interests, correctly read, predicts.

## Berlin vs Paris: The Reversal of German Politics and the Franco-German Trade-Defence Coalition

In October 2024, the single most plausible route to a rapid dismantling of the EU's countervailing duties on Chinese battery-electric vehicles ran through Berlin. Germany, home to Europe's largest car industry and its most China-exposed manufacturers, voted against the definitive measures in the Council on 4 October 2024 — a vote of 10 in favour, 5 against, 12 abstentions, with France in the "yes" camp, Germany leading the "no" camp, and Spain abstaining. Eighteen months later, that route has been closed — not by Brussels, and not by Beijing, but by Berlin itself. This chapter argues that the German political reversal, consolidated in the Merz–Macron alignment on stronger trade defence, is the most important member-state development de-risking this report's modal forecast: a 50% probability that the duties of Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2754 remain in force essentially unchanged through 30 June 2027, with negotiations running alongside rather than displacing the instrument.

### From "No" Vote to Protectionist Consensus: What Changed in Berlin

The October 2024 German position was not ambivalence. German carmakers lobbied openly against the duties alongside the VDA and the trade union IG Metall, anchored by the fact that roughly one third of their 2023 sales were generated in China. The industry's logic was straightforward: BMW and Mercedes-Benz sell premium vehicles built in Germany to Chinese customers; any Chinese retaliation against large-displacement European cars — a step Chinese automakers had already urged on Beijing in June 2024, and which a Chinese business group publicly warned was under consideration — would land disproportionately on German balance sheets. Berlin's "no" vote was a faithful transmission of that commercial calculus into Council politics.

By 2026 that transmission had broken, and for structural reasons rather than a change of tone. Two developments did the work.

First, the commercial exposure that anchored the anti-tariff position has eroded. Germany Trade & Invest data show China falling from Germany's second-largest export market in 2021 to ninth by the first half of 2026. When China accounted for a rising share of German export sales, the arithmetic favoured appeasement: protect the China revenue stream, absorb the import competition. As that revenue stream shrinks in relative terms while Chinese penetration of the European car market climbs — Chinese-made vehicles reached a record 9.3% of new cars sold in the EU in December 2025 [S16] — the arithmetic inverts. German firms now lose more from unimpeded Chinese competition in their home market than they risk losing from retaliation in a shrinking Chinese one. DW's analysis of this shift concluded that Germany's China calculation changed as import pressures eclipsed export benefits [S38].

Second, the reversal was consolidated at the highest political level. Under Chancellor Merz, Berlin co-signed with President Macron a letter to Commission President von der Leyen seeking a new instrument to cut unfair trading partners off from the single market. The Franco-German deal to support the European car industry — which Volkswagen publicly praised, and which settled the parallel controversy over emissions rules — embedded trade defence in a package of industrial support that German OEMs had a direct incentive to defend [S40][S41]. By mid-2026, the German automotive lobby itself, through the VDA and IG Metall, was pressing for trade defence rather than against it, with the member-state argument centred on the local-content requirements of the Industrial Accelerator Act rather than on repeal of the existing duties.

The direction of travel matters for forecasting: the German debate has migrated from "should this instrument exist?" to "what should the next instrument look like?" That is a debate conducted entirely within a protectionist frame. Internal scenario deliberation conducted for this report reinforces the point qualitatively: the German government and Volkswagen ranked among the most agenda-setting actors in projected negotiation dynamics, and the German government's stance was tracked as shifting from opposition to the duties toward alliance with the Commission — a structural reading of incentives that matches the observable record of the Merz–Macron letter and the Council vote reversal.

### The Mechanism: Why the Coalition Locks In the Status Quo

The forecasting consequence of this realignment operates through two mechanisms, one negative and one positive.

The negative mechanism concerns repeal pressure. Formally, the Council cannot rescind countervailing duties by vote; trade-defence competence sits with the Commission. But a sustained, large-member-state political campaign against the measures — the kind Germany could have run from October 2024 onward — would have raised the political price of maintaining them at every decision point: the interim review, the response to the WTO panel, the handling of undertaking offers. That campaign never materialised. Instead, by the time the Commission initiated its partial interim review on the form of the measure on 4 December 2025 and concluded it on 9–10 February 2026 with the Volkswagen (Anhui) undertaking, the two largest member states were jointly pressing for the Commission to go further, not retreat. A Commission that faces a Franco-German front demanding stronger instruments has no domestic political reason to dismantle existing ones — especially when its legal position in WTO dispute DS630 depends on defending the integrity of its subsidy-injury methodology [S4].

The positive mechanism concerns the negotiating mandate. The Berlin-Paris alignment does not make the Commission more hawkish against China so much as it stabilises the Commission's bargaining position vis-à-vis MOFCOM. Šefčovič can negotiate price undertakings knowing that no member-state coalition stands ready to punish him for holding out, and knowing that his fallback — the duties simply remain — is politically costless at home. This asymmetry favours the "duties persist while talks continue" outcome over both a rapid negotiated replacement and a unilateral retreat. The 9 October 2026 understanding, under which EV trade continues to be regulated through either countervailing duties or price undertakings [S25], is precisely the kind of arrangement this coalition structure produces: it keeps the instrument, opens the negotiating channel, and defers the hard questions past every near-term political deadline.

### Steelmanning the Counter-Argument: The Exposure That Didn't Disappear

The strongest objection to this analysis is that German exposure to Chinese retaliation has not actually diminished — only Germany's weighting of it has. BMW and Mercedes-Benz still relied on China for roughly one third of their sales as of 2023, and the large-displacement car tariffs that Chinese automakers demanded in June 2024 remain a loaded weapon: no such measure had been announced or implemented as of 20 October 2026, but the threat is intact. On this reading, Berlin's conversion to trade defence is a bet that Beijing will not pull the trigger — a bet that could be repriced overnight if undertaking negotiations collapse and MOFCOM moves against German automotive exports.

This objection deserves weight, and it is why this report assigns a 17% residual bin that includes escalation paths rather than treating retaliation as impossible. But three considerations blunt it. China's demonstrated retaliation pattern through 2025–26 has been calibrated and reversible: final brandy duties of up to 34.9% arrived with minimum-price exemptions for the major cognac houses, and Reuters reported the cognac relief was explicitly conditional on EV-tariff progress; pork finals settled at 4.9–19.8% against provisionals of up to 62.4%; dairy finals were reported at 7.4–11.7% against provisionals of 21.9–42.7%. Beijing has consistently stopped short of the automotive sector — the one step that would rupture the relationship with Berlin that MOFCOM needs if a government-to-government minimum-price deal is ever to be accepted. Striking German cars would destroy the very constituency inside the EU that is most sympathetic to a negotiated settlement. Second, China's own macro position — private-sector credit at 204.1% of GDP in 2026-Q1, up from 201.9% a year earlier — argues for export continuity over escalation. Third, the political insurance the counter-argument assumes has already been tested: German OEMs absorbed three rounds of Chinese duties on European agricultural goods without any revival of the anti-tariff lobby.

The more precise statement is therefore this: German exposure constrains how far Berlin will let escalation go, but it no longer constrains the instrument itself. That distinction is what the 2024 position collapsed and the 2026 position preserves.

### Manufacturer Consequences: A Divided Industry Inside a United Policy

The Franco-German coalition papers over a deep asymmetry in how the duties distribute costs and benefits across European manufacturers — and this asymmetry, paradoxically, reinforces the status quo.

On the benefiting side, Renault and Stellantis gain protection in their core European market segments, where Chinese BEV imports at 42% of extra-EU electric-car import value in 2025 would otherwise compete most directly on price. French producers also bore the brunt of Chinese retaliation — brandy duties of up to 34.9% and dairy provisionals of up to 42.7% fall overwhelmingly on French exports — which is precisely why Paris has anchored the pro-tariff coalition since the 4 October 2024 vote and doubled down thereafter. France's own position, however, is not unconstrained: the retaliation that falls on cognac and dairy gives Paris a parallel interest in a settlement, which is why the Franco-German convergence landed on "stronger instruments plus negotiated outcomes" rather than pure confrontation.

On the exposed side, the German majors split. Volkswagen resolved its dilemma unilaterally: its Anhui subsidiary secured the sole accepted undertaking on 9–10 February 2026, exempting it from the duties in exchange for an annual quota, a minimum import price and monitoring obligations. That first-mover deal changed Volkswagen's internal calculus — the company now benefits from a regime that taxes its Chinese-built competitors at 17.0–35.3% while its own imports enter duty-free, and Volkswagen accordingly praised the Franco-German industry deal. BMW and Mercedes-Benz retain the exposure without the carve-out. Their rational strategy is to lobby quietly for a broad settlement that lowers Chinese retaliation risk — which aligns them with Berlin's "press for settlement, keep the instrument" posture rather than with repeal.

The result is a European industry with no unified voice for dismantling the duties. Renault and Stellantis want them kept; Volkswagen wants them kept for everyone except itself; BMW and Mercedes want the retaliation risk managed but have no realistic path to repeal that does not run through a settlement they would also accept. German industry's 2024 unity against the tariffs has fragmented into position-specific preferences, none of which aggregates into a Council majority for retreat. A structural reading of the negotiation network confirms the point: across the projected bargaining landscape, all major state and industry actors cluster around the same set of interaction partners — the Commission, MOFCOM, the undertaking file — with no separate anti-duties bloc forming; the WTO panel sits inside the same cluster but with far less agenda-setting weight than Berlin or Paris.

### Implications for the Forecast to 30 June 2027

The member-state realignment contributes to this report's probability structure in three specific ways.

It supports the 50% duties-hold scenario by removing the political channel through which the instrument could have been pressured out early. With no expiry-review decision point before the horizon — the measures run to 31 October 2029 — and no Council constituency for reopening them, the default path is inertia reinforced from both Paris and Berlin.

It shapes, rather than opposes, the 22% undertaking-replacement scenario. The Franco-German coalition favours a settlement on the Commission's terms: undertakings that demonstrably offset subsidy-caused injury, per the 12 January 2026 guidance [S7], not the single government-to-government minimum price MOFCOM prefers. If a replacement comes, it will come slowly and in company-specific increments, following the Volkswagen (Anhui) template — which is why most partial outcomes fall into the residual bin rather than the full-replacement bin. Berlin's hosting of and engagement in successive negotiation rounds, as the projection of its agenda-setting role suggests, serves exactly this incremental pathway.

And it caps the escalation sub-branch of the 17% residual. The clearest falsifier for escalation remains a MOFCOM initiation notice on large-displacement EU cars — the step that would test whether Berlin's new protectionist consensus survives contact with German automotive losses. Until that notice appears, the coalition holds, and with it the modal outcome: duties in force, talks in progress, and the German government standing beside the French one, asking Brussels for more trade defence rather than less.

## The WTO Track (DS630) and the Hybrid Safeguard: Legal Clocks That Do Not Bind

Two legal clocks run alongside the Brussels–Beijing bargaining that will actually decide this dispute: China's WTO challenge in dispute DS630, and the EU's nascent hybrid safeguard with provisional measures advertised for December 2026. The central claim of this chapter is that neither clock can compel a change in the status quo before 30 June 2027. The DS630 panel's final report is not expected before Q2 2027, and even a panel win for China produces only a recommendation to bring measures into conformity — a recommendation whose adoption, appeal and implementation would straddle or follow the horizon, given the appeal route the parties pre-agreed. The hybrid safeguard, by contrast, is a clock that ticks *for* the European Commission: it enforces the cap negotiated in Beijing on 9 October 2026 and, in its most likely form, becomes a managed monitoring regime rather than a trade shock. Both processes therefore reinforce the modal outcome of this report — duties in force essentially unchanged at 50% — while leaving the negotiated-replacement path (22%) as the only realistic channel through which the instrument itself changes inside the window.

### DS630: A Litigation Track That Cannot Bind Before the Horizon

The procedural history is long and, for forecasting purposes, decisive. China requested consultations on 4 November 2024, days after the definitive duties took effect on 30 October 2024. Consultations in December 2024 failed to resolve the dispute; China requested panel establishment on 13 March 2025; the Dispute Settlement Body deferred on 24 March and established the panel on 25 April 2025, with 17 members reserving third-party rights — Australia, Brazil, Canada, India, Japan, Kazakhstan, Korea, Mexico, Norway, Russia, Singapore, Switzerland, Thailand, Türkiye, Ukraine, the UK and the US. The panel was composed only on 13 October 2025, and its timetable was subsequently extended at the parties' own request on 1 April 2026, with the final report not expected before Q2 2027.

Two structural features of this dispute make even an early panel report a weak lever for change inside the forecast window. First, the parties pre-committed on 31 July 2025 to MPIA-style arbitration under DSU Article 25 for any appeal, informing the Dispute Settlement Body jointly. This was a good-faith gesture that forecloses the "appeal into the void" strategy — but its practical effect is to lengthen, not shorten, the path to legal finality. A losing party that appeals now enters a functioning arbitration process that will take additional months to conclude, with compliance implementation coming only after that. Second, even a fully adopted ruling adverse to the EU would require the Commission to bring its measures into conformity — a process that runs through a reasonable-period-of-time negotiation and, in practice, through the same interim-review instrument the Commission already controls. WTO rulings do not self-execute into the Official Journal. The US as third party has been an active participant, with USTR filings documented from April through July 2026 — a reminder to Brussels that the systemic stakes of this case are watched well beyond the two parties, strengthening the Commission's incentive to litigate it out rather than settle under duress.

The counterargument deserves a fair hearing: could a panel report landing in, say, April 2027 pressure the Commission into a rapid negotiated settlement, effectively converting litigation risk into the undertaking scenario? This is conceivable in principle — a report finding the duties inconsistent with the SCM Agreement would strengthen MOFCOM's hand at exactly the moment undertaking talks are live. But three frictions blunt this path. The report is not expected *before* Q2 2027, meaning it may well arrive after 30 June 2027 entirely. The parties jointly requested the timetable extension, signalling mutual comfort with delay — both governments prefer to see what the negotiating track produces before litigating to the end, exactly as the December 2025 resumption of minimum-price talks running parallel to the litigation suggests. And the Commission has every institutional incentive to defend its instrument: its credibility rests on duties persisting or undertakings demonstrably offsetting injury, and a negotiated capitulation under legal duress would be read across the 192 definitive measures in force at end-2024 as evidence that litigation pays.

The report therefore treats DS630 as a leading indicator and a post-horizon decision point, not a within-window driver. WTO-driven change to Regulation (EU) 2024/2754 before 30 June 2027 would require an unusually early report plus unusually rapid Commission action — a compound tail event, not a base case. This is precisely why the interim-review scenario is assigned only 11%: the review channel exists and was used once (the partial review initiated 4 December 2025 and concluded 9–10 February 2026, accepting the Volkswagen (Anhui) undertaking), but nothing in the DS630 timetable forces the Commission to open another one inside the window.

One subtle interaction deserves emphasis: DS630 and the negotiating track are not independent. A negotiated undertaking accepted by both sides would partially moot the panel, just as the 2013 solar undertaking defused that episode's confrontation before adjudication could run its course. Both parties know this, which is one reason neither is pressing for acceleration. The litigation is, in effect, a hedge each side holds while bargaining — a card that neither needs to play before mid-2027.

### The Hybrid Safeguard: Enforcement of the Beijing Cap, Not a New Shock

The second clock ticks on the Commission's side. Under the EU–China understanding of 9 October 2026, Chinese hybrid exports to the EU are capped at roughly 350,000 vehicles per year over four years, implemented via an EU safeguard with provisional measures advertised for December 2026, alongside market access worth about €4 billion for EU products. The economic backdrop explains why Brussels secured this cap: Chinese customs data cited by Reuters show hybrid exports to the EU-27 reaching $7.5 billion in the first half of 2026, tripled from $2.5 billion a year earlier, while Eurostat records EU plug-in-hybrid imports rising 48% in 2025 (€8.8 billion to €13.0 billion) even as battery-electric import value fell 8%. The duty wall on BEVs channelled volume into the duty-free hybrid lane; the safeguard puts a ceiling on that lane.

The forecast here is that the December 2026 safeguard decision becomes a managed monitoring regime rather than a shock, for three reasons. First, it is not an escalation but the *implementation* of a jointly negotiated understanding — MOFCOM accepted the framework and committed to "immediately begin negotiations" on EV price undertakings, and Chinese exporters have had months to anticipate the cap. Second, the mechanics of a safeguard with an agreed annual quota favour quota administration over prohibitive duties: the instrument's purpose is to hold hybrid volumes near the agreed level, and front-loaded PHEV shipments in late 2026 will consume quota headroom that subsequent quarters must respect — a self-correcting dynamic that reduces the need for sharp provisional rate spikes. Third, the Commission's own interest is in a working precedent for quantity-based management: a proof of concept that could later be transposed to the BEV file as an alternative to ad valorem duties. A botched, confrontational safeguard launch would damage the credibility of exactly the toolkit Šefčovič needs for the undertaking talks. It should also be stressed that the October 2026 understanding is a framework, not a signed text — as of 20 October 2026 the safeguard investigation itself had not yet been launched, and it is unknown whether quota allocation will be linked to BEV-undertaking progress. This is an explicit evidence gap the report flags rather than fills.

The December decision is nonetheless the single nearest dated discriminator between scenarios. If provisional measures arrive cleanly, with quota administration and no explicit linkage to the BEV undertaking negotiations, that reads as the two files being kept separate — favouring duties holding unchanged. If, by contrast, the safeguard design embeds a minimum-price term or explicitly conditions quota allocation on BEV-undertaking progress, the two tracks are being merged into a single package — favouring the negotiated-replacement scenario (22%).

A steelman for the shock view: the legal commentary on the hybrid safeguards deal asks pointedly whether it is a win-win "for whom?", suggesting the agreement's enforcement architecture could prove contested. If December 2026 provisionals are set at genuinely restrictive rates, or if Chinese exporters' front-loaded inventories breach the cap's spirit early, MOFCOM could read enforcement as bad faith, poisoning the EV undertaking talks the understanding was designed to launch. This chain would push probability toward the residual/escalation bin (17%), and it cannot be dismissed. But the empirical pattern of this dispute cuts against it: every Chinese measure since 2024 — brandy, pork, dairy — has landed at final rates far below provisionals, calibrated and reversible. Both governments have repeatedly chosen managed de-escalation over shock. Bruegel's reading of the understanding itself is that it "buys time," favouring managed outcomes over escalation, with EV trade continuing to be regulated by either countervailing duties or price undertakings [S25].

### Two Clocks, One Direction: Why the Legal Architecture Favours Inertia

Taken together, the two processes produce a distinctive asymmetry. The WTO clock protects the EU: China's legal lever cannot mature before the horizon, so Beijing's only routes to relief inside the window are negotiation or retaliation — and the retaliation record, as Chapter 5 details, is calibrated and has left automotive untouched. The safeguard clock disciplines China: it converts the October 2026 understanding into enforceable EU law and caps the escape route Chinese exporters had found in hybrids — a route particularly valuable to BYD, whose PHEV exports fall outside the duty's scope and thereby lower its incentive to seek a BEV undertaking. Notably, the safeguard narrows exactly the loophole that was keeping the strongest Chinese exporter content with the status quo, which in the medium term could push BYD toward the negotiating table even as it removes its incentive to do so unilaterally.

This is the structural foundation of the 50% modal scenario. Regulation (EU) 2024/2754 runs to 31 October 2029 with no expiry-review decision point before the horizon; the Commission's sole formal modification to date — the VW (Anhui) carve-out of 9–10 February 2026 — left the duty structure intact for every other exporter. For the duties to change before 30 June 2027, the Commission must act affirmatively through an Official Journal act: either a new undertaking decision naming BYD, SAIC, Geely or Tesla (moving mass toward the 22% scenario), or an interim-review notice (the 11% scenario). Absent those acts, inertia is not merely passive — it is what the two legal clocks, one stalled in Geneva and one ticking toward managed enforcement in Brussels, actively produce.

The verification markers follow directly. Watch for the DS630 report's actual circulation date against the Q2 2027 floor, and for whether any appeal is lodged into the pre-agreed Article 25 arbitration. Watch for the December 2026 safeguard instrument's design — quota administration versus hard rates, and any linkage to the BEV talks — and for whether the safeguard investigation is launched at all before year-end. Each observation will shift probability mass across the four scenarios, and none of them, on current timetables, compels a change in the duties themselves before 30 June 2027.

Trade data rarely settle a tariff dispute, but they determine what every actor can plausibly claim at the negotiating table. This chapter reads the official indicators available as of October 2026 and asks a narrow question: what do they imply for the durability of Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2754 through 30 June 2027? The answer is double-edged. The macro indicators show a China that can still afford to subsidise and still needs export markets, which supports continued EU defence of the instrument. The micro trade data, meanwhile, show a duty that has diverted rather than blocked Chinese vehicles — value down, units up, hybrids surging — an ambiguity that weakens the economic case for urgent replacement while giving both Brussels and Beijing reasons to keep talking rather than force a rupture. That ambiguity, not any decisive statistic, underpins this report's modal forecast of duties persisting alongside talks (50%), with a 22% probability of negotiated replacement.

### The Macro Snapshot: A China That Can Still Subsidise, and Still Must Export

Three official series frame Beijing's bargaining position. First, China's exports of goods and services stood at 21.071% of GDP in 2025, up 0.628 percentage points year-on-year [S19]. By this report's calculation from the stored history, that reading sits at the 57th percentile of the past thirty annual observations — neither an export-collapse economy nor an inward-turning one. The direction matters more than the level: Chinese exports as a share of GDP are rising again, which means the political economy of walking away from a market absorbing more than 900,000 Chinese electric cars a year remains unattractive to Beijing. A government managing a creeping-upward export share has every incentive to negotiate away a 35.3% duty on its flagship industry rather than abandon the market.

Second, US imports of goods from China reached $27.13 billion in August 2026, up 6.9% year-on-year [S20]. By this report's calculation from the stored history, that monthly reading sits at only the 16th percentile of the 118-month window — a structurally depressed level produced by the earlier American tariff wall, even though the year-on-year trend has turned positive. The implication for the EU dispute is indirect but real: with the US channel compressed relative to its 2022 peak, Chinese exporters treat Europe as a growth outlet they cannot casually cede. Brussels understands this, and it strengthens the Commission's hand — MOFCOM needs a deal more than DG TRADE does, which is one reason this report discounts full undertaking replacement to 22% despite the two-for-two historical precedent of EU-China duty episodes ending in undertakings.

Third, and most directly relevant to the legal core: credit to China's private non-financial sector reached 204.1% of GDP in the first quarter of 2026, up 2.2 percentage points year-on-year [S21]. By this report's calculation from the stored history, this is the highest reading in the entire 37-quarter window — the 100th percentile. The countervailing-duty finding rests on the premise that the Chinese BEV value chain benefits from unfair subsidies threatening injury to Union producers, codified under the basic anti-subsidy Regulation (EU) 2016/1037. A record credit stock is not proof of any specific subsidy programme, but it is consistent with the structural claim underlying the instrument: the fiscal-financial capacity to support exporters persists. Notably, BYD's behaviour in the EU market — including subsidy-plus-incentive discounting in Germany — continues against exactly this macro backdrop of record private credit. As long as that capacity remains observable, the Commission can defend the duties as addressing a live phenomenon rather than a historical one, and the 2024 expiry-review base rate (11 of 13 confirmed ) tells EU institutions that instruments anchored in subsidisation findings tend to survive.

### The Effectiveness Controversy: Value Fell, Units Rose, and the Counterfactual Is Disputed

The micro evidence on whether the duties "work" is genuinely contested, and honest forecasting requires presenting the conflict rather than averaging it away.

On one reading, the duties are biting. Eurostat reports that EU imports of battery-electric cars fell 8% in value in 2025, while China's share of extra-EU electric-car import value declined to 42% — still the largest origin, with China, Japan, South Korea and the UK together at 73% of hybrid and electric imports. A duty stack reaching up to 45% including the base tariff, collecting an estimated €500 million per quarter — roughly €2 billion a year — has visibly reshaped the import mix.

On the other reading, the duties have barely dented the flow. The IEA counts EU imports of electric cars from China growing about 35% in units in 2025 to more than 900,000, with Chinese brands' share of those imports rising from 50% to over 70% — while Tesla's Shanghai-built share of those imports fell from 30% in 2023 to 10% in 2025, confirming that the growth is indigenous Chinese brands, precisely the exporters the duties target. Rhodium Group finds Chinese-made cars at a record 9.3% of EU new-car sales, with Chinese imports into Europe accelerating overall a year and a half after the duties were applied [S16]. The resolution of the paradox is substitution on two margins: exporters cut prices (depressing import value while holding units) and pivoted toward plug-in hybrids, which sit outside the duty's product scope — EU plug-in-hybrid imports jumped 48% in 2025, from €8.8 billion to €13.0 billion, even as total hybrid and electric imports rose only 4% to €45.1 billion. Chinese hybrid exports to the EU reached $7.5 billion in the first half of 2026, tripled from $2.5 billion a year earlier, per Chinese customs data — a boom so large that the 9 October 2026 understanding had to cap it at roughly 350,000 vehicles per year.

The sharpest challenge to the duties' causal effect comes from CEPR's difference-in-differences analysis: China's value share fell from about 55% in early 2024 to about 42% by mid-2025, but by a similar or larger margin in tariff-free Norway, Switzerland and the UK — with the share decline in non-EU markets actually exceeding the EU decline — yielding a negative, small and statistically insignificant estimate of the duties' effect. If untreated control markets moved the same way, the duties may be doing little protective work. This sits in direct tension with the Regulation's recorded injury finding; the divergence is methodological (econometric identification versus a legal determination), but politically it is live, because settlement advocates can cite it to argue the instrument is revenue without protection.

### Why Ambiguous Data Stabilise Rather than Destabilise the Status Quo

The instinctive inference — "if the duties don't work, Brussels will scrap them" — gets the political logic backwards, and steelmanning the opposite view makes this clear. The strongest argument for rapid replacement is that a duty collecting €500 million a quarter while Chinese-made cars hit a record 9.3% of EU sales is economically wasteful and diplomatically corrosive; a minimum-price undertaking could cap discounting directly and remove the retaliation pretext. If the Commission fully internalised the CEPR reading, the instrument's rationale would erode — and indeed the record shows France and Germany jointly pressing for *stronger* trade defence by 2026, partly because of that record 9.3% penetration, the opposite political reaction.

Three mechanisms push against rapid replacement. First, the Commission's institutional incentive is instrument credibility: a measure repealed because econometricians found it ineffective would hand MOFCOM a precedent against every future EU trade-defence action, days into the DS630 panel process. Second, the unit-value divergence gives the Commission a usable counter-narrative — 900,000 units and a 70% Chinese-brand share is precisely the "threat of injury" picture the Regulation addresses, and the tripling of hybrid volumes outside its scope proves, in this reading, that the problem migrated rather than disappeared. The institutional response to the hybrid surge was not liberalisation but a new cap — the behaviour of an authority that doubles down on instruments, not one preparing to retire them. Third, data ambiguity lowers urgency on both sides: neither Brussels nor Beijing can demonstrate the duties are decisive, so neither faces pressure to settle on the other's terms before mid-2027. Ambiguity buys time, which is how Bruegel characterised the October 2026 understanding — a welcome buying of time.

For forecasting, this is why "duties hold unchanged" carries 50%: the data neither vindicate repeal nor demand escalation, and in trade policy that combination defaults to inertia. The 22% replacement probability is driven by the negotiation track opened on 9 October 2026, not by the data, which function as background conditioning rather than trigger.

### Observable Thresholds for Each Scenario

Each forecast branch has data-detectable signatures, specified for post-hoc verification:

- **Duties hold (50%)**: China's share of extra-EU electric-car import value stays in a 35–45% band around the 2025 reading of 42%, with a *sustained* fall in the 2026 data being the escalation indicator to watch; EU BEV import value remains within ±10% of its 2025 level; hybrid imports moderate toward the €13.0 billion plateau as the 350,000-unit cap binds; no Official Journal undertaking act naming BYD, SAIC, Geely or Tesla through May 2027.
- **Undertaking replacement (22%)**: an implementing act accepting undertakings covering a majority of Chinese BEV export share before 30 June 2027. Leading data indicator: a fall in China's value share well below 42% *combined with* unit volumes holding near 800,000–900,000, the signature of minimum prices raising unit values while capping volumes, before any formal act.
- **Interim-review modification (11%)**: a Commission review notice concerning Regulation 2024/2754. Data-wise, a sharp break in the value/unit divergence — import value rebounding while units fall — would constitute the changed circumstance that legally justifies rate adjustment.
- **Residual/mixed (17%)**: additional company-specific carve-outs on the VW (Anhui) model without majority coverage; December 2026 hybrid-safeguard provisionals shifting the trade-policy mix; or a steep fall in the 42% share attributable to new Chinese non-tariff friction rather than undertakings.

Two cross-cutting series deserve monitoring though they are not direct outcome measures: US imports from China [S20], where a renewed slump would raise Beijing's pressure to secure EU market access (raising replacement probability); and China's private-credit ratio, where sustained deleveraging would be the first macro hint that the subsidisation capacity underlying the EU's legal finding was eroding — relevant mainly to the 2029 expiry review, but colouring Commission testimony in DS630.

### The Bottom Line from the Data

The official indicators tell a consistent story about structure and an ambiguous story about effect. Structurally, China has the financial capacity (record private credit at 204.1% of GDP ), the export dependence (21.071% of GDP and rising ) and the market urgency (the US channel at the 16th percentile of its recent range ) to want a deal — but not the desperation to accept company-specific terms. On effect, the duties coincided with value falling 8% and share falling to 42% while units rose past 900,000 and hybrids tripled — a record that neither vindicates nor discredits the instrument, and that no econometric reading has settled. In trade disputes, unresolved evidence contests favour whoever controls the default. Through 30 June 2027 the default is Regulation 2024/2754 running to October 2029, and the data, read honestly, give the Commission no reason to abandon the instrument and MOFCOM no leverage to force its abandonment. That is the quantitative case behind this report's modal forecast.

### Calibration: Three Empirical Anchors and One Explicit Discount

The probability weights were not free-styled. They rest on three base rates drawn from the record, adjusted by case-specific structure.

**Anchor one: instruments persist.** The Commission's 43rd annual report records that of 13 expiry reviews concluded in 2024, 11 ended with duties confirmed and only 2 lapsed — roughly 85%. The EU ended 2024 with 192 definitive measures in force, launched only 33 new investigations, and initiated only four interim reviews across the entire year. Once definitive, EU trade-defence measures overwhelmingly survive to their natural horizon — and in this case the natural horizon is 31 October 2029 per the Commission's TRON case database, more than two years beyond this forecast's boundary. No expiry-review decision point, whose request window opens only in the measure's final months, binds before 30 June 2027. That structural fact alone would justify a duties-hold weight above half; the negotiating track is what caps it at 50%.

**Anchor two: EU-China duty disputes end in undertakings — two out of two.** The solar dispute of 2013 concluded with a Commission decision of 2 August 2013 accepting a price undertaking that displaced provisional duties of 11.8% for participating exporters; the VW (Anhui) carve-out of 9–10 February 2026 partially displaced the current duties. Two-for-two is a small sample with heterogeneous scope — one full replacement, one carve-out — but it establishes the settlement currency: both governments know the undertaking instrument intimately, and China itself used minimum-price exemptions to spare the major cognac houses in July 2025 [S11]. This anchor is why replacement carries 22% rather than a token weight.

**Anchor three: China's retaliation is calibrated and agriculturally confined.** Two of two prior retaliation episodes after EU trade measures produced investigations within months, but every implemented duty landed in agriculture and spirits, and every final settled far below provisionals: brandy up to 34.9% with cognac-house exemptions [S11]; pork finals of 4.9–19.8% against provisionals up to 62.4%; dairy finals reported at 7.4–11.7% after provisionals of 21.9–42.7%. Relief was explicitly conditioned on EV-talk progress. This pattern anchors the small escalation tail.

**The discount, stated openly.** This report's structured scenario deliberation — synthesising Commission, MOFCOM, German-government, Volkswagen and BYD perspectives — projected a substantially higher probability of undertaking replacement, on the order of three-quarters. This report rejects that number and holds 22%, and the reason is the observable record: as of 20 October 2026 there is one accepted undertaking, and the Commission itself stated that VW (Anhui) submitted the sole formal offer since imposition of the definitive measures — no primary record shows BYD, SAIC, Geely or Tesla filing one. The commitment to "immediately begin negotiations" made under the 9 October 2026 understanding remains untested; the safeguard investigation it presupposes had not yet been launched; and the December 2026 decision that would first reveal the framework's substance has not occurred. The deliberation priced negotiating goodwill; the primary record prices filings, and there are none. It also underweighted three obstacles the record makes plain: the Commission's stated doubt that a single minimum price would counter subsidy-caused injury [S7]; MOFCOM's insistence on a government-to-government model that rejects the company-specific architecture; and BYD's duty-free hybrid channel — $7.5 billion of EU-bound exports in H1 2026, tripled year-on-year [S43] — which keeps the largest exporter content without a deal. This divergence is flagged as the principal source of forecast uncertainty: if the deliberation's reading of Beijing's political will is correct, the true replacement probability is materially higher than 22%, and the December 2026 safeguard design plus the Official Journal through Q1 2027 are the observations that will adjudicate between the two readings fastest. The deliberation did yield one robust qualitative conclusion this report retains: across every projected negotiation dynamic, the Commission, MOFCOM, Volkswagen, the German government and Šefčovič dominated the agenda while the WTO panel was peripheral to each decisive moment — the dispute, if settled at all, is settled between Brussels and Beijing, not in Geneva.

### Decision Implications for Automakers

For Chinese exporters the forecast implies three distinct positions, and the 50/22 split should change how each is evaluated.

BYD faces the most consequential choice. Under the modal path, its 17.0% duty persists but so does its hybrid channel — until the cap of roughly 350,000 vehicles per year binds. The cap is enforced regardless of whether BYD files an undertaking, so the duty-free escape route narrows through 2027 while the BEV duty stays. The timing insight follows from the demonstrated machinery: the roughly nine-week review cycle from initiation on 4 December 2025 to acceptance on 9–10 February 2026 means an offer submitted in Q1 2027 could be accepted before mid-2027, converting a 17.0% duty into a quota-plus-minimum-price regime while competitors remain dutied — the first-mover advantage Volkswagen (Anhui) now enjoys, with its annual quota, minimum import price, sole-importer routing and monitoring obligations. Mechanically, the 22% replacement scenario is a world in which BYD files and others follow; BYD's own filing decision is therefore not merely an input to the forecast but its single largest swing variable.

SAIC, at the top rate of 35.3% and with no comparable hybrid valve, has the strongest economic case for an undertaking and the least to lose by accepting minimum prices; a substantial part of the residual bin is precisely "SAIC or Geely files, BYD does not" — a partial carve-out that changes trade flows without displacing the instrument. Tesla, at 7.8%, is correctly idle: the constraint of an undertaking exceeds the relief available at that rate.

For European manufacturers, the modal path preserves the current asymmetry. Renault and Stellantis keep their protection; Volkswagen keeps a carve-out its dutied rivals lack; BMW and Mercedes-Benz keep their retaliation exposure untriggered — which aligns them with the Franco-German "settlement without erosion" formula Berlin and Paris converged on by 2026. The one scenario that harms Volkswagen specifically is full replacement: a general minimum-price regime enjoyed by all erodes the exclusive cost position it secured in February 2026. Volkswagen's rational interest therefore runs toward the modal outcome — continued quiet support for the instrument its industry once lobbied against, now that it is the only company exempt from it. The cheapest real-time monitor of the retaliation risk the German majors still carry is the brandy exemptions: their quiet maintenance signals continued bargaining; their withdrawal marks the first step out of the calibrated playbook.

### Decision Implications for Investors

Two asset-relevant streams follow directly from the table. First, duty revenue: Gros et al. estimate collections of roughly €500 million per quarter, about €2 billion per year — an authors' estimate based on 2024–25 import patterns, not a customs statistic. Under the modal path this stream persists through the horizon; under replacement it is exchanged for a monitoring regime the same economists explicitly advised against. A fiscally visible €2 billion per year is a quiet constituency for the status quo that any settlement must buy out.

Second, the trade-data signature of each scenario is investable before it is official. The most valuable leading indicator is the divergence between value and units: China's value share of extra-EU electric-car imports stood at 42% in 2025, while unit imports grew about 35% to more than 900,000. A fall in the value share well below 42% combined with unit volumes holding near 800,000–900,000 would be the data fingerprint of minimum prices operating on the ground — the replacement scenario showing itself in customs statistics weeks or months before it shows itself in law. Conversely, share stability in the low-40s with continued unit growth confirms the modal path. Gros et al.'s finding that the value share fell by similar or larger margins in tariff-free Norway, Switzerland and the UK (Q2 2025: −40% in non-EU markets versus −33% in the EU), with a small, negative, statistically insignificant estimated duty effect, means investors should not read share movements naively as evidence of the instrument's force — the counterfactual is genuinely disputed, and that dispute is itself stabilising, since neither side can demonstrate the duties are decisive enough to force the other's capitulation.

### Decision Implications for Policymakers

For the Commission, the central lesson is that its own calendar discipline is its strongest asset. The December 2026 safeguard decision is the highest-leverage design choice inside the window: linking quota allocation to BEV-undertaking progress would convert an enforcement instrument into a negotiating instrument, raising replacement probability at the risk of inviting MOFCOM accusations of bad faith if enforcement bites — the very question legal commentary on the hybrid deal has already posed in asking whether it is a win-win "for whom?" [S48]. Keeping the files separate preserves the modal path. The DS630 timetable — a report not before Q2 2027, with appeal pre-routed to the Article 25 arbitration agreed on 31 July 2025 — means the litigation hedge needs no servicing before the horizon; the Commission's scarce resource is the acceptance pipeline, whose nine-week demonstrated cycle makes it procedurally capable of absorbing a wave of filings if Beijing ever authorises one. Preparing that capacity is the cheapest hedge against the 22% branch.

For Beijing, the forecast identifies a paradox worth internalising: the strategy that maximises near-term commercial gains — discount-driven BEV growth plus the hybrid surge — is the strategy that most entrenches the instrument, because it supplies Brussels with the threat-of-injury evidence and justifies successive caps. The path to removing the duties runs through the very company filings that MOFCOM's government-to-government doctrine currently prohibits. If Beijing's political will is as strong as the deliberation suggested, the resolution of this tension — MOFCOM orchestrating filings rather than blocking them — is the mechanism to watch.

For both sides, the horizon beyond this forecast matters more than the horizon within it. The expiry review, requestable roughly three months before 31 October 2029, is where the 85% confirmation base rate will next bite; the DS630 endgame will mature after that; and the safeguard's four-year cap expires in a similar frame. Everything forecast for 30 June 2027 is structurally an interim state negotiated in the shadow of 2029. The three dated observations that most efficiently update this forecast — the December 2026 safeguard design, the Official Journal through Q1 2027, and the status of the brandy exemptions — are successive readings of one question: whether Brussels and Beijing are managing this dispute toward a settlement, or merely managing its speed. On the evidence as of 20 October 2026, the modal answer is the latter — and the roughly one-in-five path to the former runs, mechanically and observably, through the filings that have not yet been made.
