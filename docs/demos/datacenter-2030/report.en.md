## Part Two · Framework & Synthesis (Part 2 — Framework & Synthesis)

### Master Analytical Framework: A Three-Parameter-Shaped Wide-Interval Forecast

This report's forecasting framework rests on a core judgment: by the end of 2030, the most likely shape of the global data center market is not a single number extrapolated by one institution, but a wide interval jointly shaped by three parameters: **pipeline realization rates, the form of power constraints, and financing-structure resilience**. A global cumulative installed IT capacity of 190–210 GW and single-year capital expenditure of $2.5–3.2 trillion are the highest-probability landing points, but hard power constraints and financing tightening together constitute 50% of downside tail, making this market one of the highest-variance infrastructure bets of this generation.

We choose five mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive scenarios as the skeleton of the probability distribution: baseline expansion (A, 35%), power-constrained (B, 30%), financial tightening (C, 20%), upside surprise (D, 5%), and other/hybrid paths (E, 10%). The overall confidence rating is moderate — this is not false modesty, but an honest pricing of the fact that at least two of the three parameters (regulatory politics and financing-ring resilience) cannot be observed with high confidence before 2027.

Why anchor primarily on installed IT capacity and pipeline-discounted delivery? Because the four authoritative institutions diverge by more than twofold on the same 2030 figure, and the root of that divergence lies in differing credit treatment of the "pipeline": metrics that treat signed RPOs, self-reported bookings, LOIs, and unleased leases as equivalent systematically overestimate deliveries. Our framework applies binding-strength-tiered discounts to demand signals, which places the primary anchor naturally below the most optimistic extrapolations and above the conservative estimates of a pure grid perspective — this position is itself a product of the framework, not a stance of eclecticism.

### The Demand-Side Engine and the First Link of the Causal Chain

The demand side is the first mover of this expansion cycle and also the most fragile link in the framework. Demand-visibility signals are real and enormous in magnitude, but their quality is systematically tiered: signed RPOs (such as the $638 billion RPO disclosed in Oracle's FY26 annual report, including roughly $75 billion in customer prepayments) carry the strongest binding force; vendor self-reported bookings come next; LOIs and unleased leases decay step by step in binding strength. This tiering directly determines the pipeline discount rate, and in turn the upper bound of the A-scenario range of 190–210 GW — we extend no full credit to unlocked pipeline.

The more critical causal variable is the structural gap of roughly 6–7x between AI revenue and capex. The sustainability of the expansion cycle is a bet on the 2027 revenue-verification milestone: if verification succeeds, the corridors to Scenarios A and D open; if it fails, the trigger conditions of Scenario C (US single-year capex falling below $1.5 trillion, a single-quarter renegotiation rate on unleased leases above 10%, or more than two hyperscalers cutting multi-year guidance by over 25%) are activated. The quality tiering of the demand-side evidence chain is the core reason we assign Scenario A 35% rather than the higher weight implied by market narratives — narratives price the pipeline at face value; we price it at realization rate.

### Hard Power Constraints: Regime Shift Determines the A/B Boundary

Power is the only input that cannot be circumvented with financial engineering. Our scenario analysis concludes: the constraint will not disappear, but it will change form — from 2026 to 2028 it manifests as a physical bottleneck (turbine slots, transformer and turbine lead times), and after 2028 it shifts to a regulatory-allocation constraint (colocation rules, state-level new-build moratoria, and the allocation politics of roughly 370 state bills).

This regime-shift mechanism is precisely the logical basis of the probability boundary between A (35%) and B (30%). The dividing line between the two scenarios is not whether constraints exist, but the race between the pace of marginal relief of physical bottlenecks and the pace of demand-pipeline realization: if behind-the-meter generation and colocation rules absorb the gap fast enough, the global landing point holds the baseline range of 190–210 GW; if not, and attributable evidence of power-related delays emerges — PJM 2028/29 or 2029/30 auction shortfalls exceeding 5,000 MW, or at least 3 states extending new-build moratoria beyond 2029 — then capacity slides into the B range of 150–185 GW. Scenario B's weighty 30% probability reflects our judgment that regulatory allocation politics will tighten after 2028: the political economy of grid queues and transmission approvals has already shifted from a technical problem to an allocation problem across multiple US load centers, and allocation problems historically resolve more slowly than equipment-supply problems.

### The Dark Side of the Financing Structure: Pricing the 20% of Scenario C

The funding structure of this expansion cycle has already shifted from profit reinvestment to a three-layer structure of "debt-first + off-balance-sheet leverage + vendor financing." The combined free cash flow of the four hyperscalers has fallen from $237 billion in 2024 to $200 billion in 2025, while capex guidance for 2026 continues to rise — the gap is filled by debt and off-balance-sheet instruments. The cost is that the entire financing ring's dependence on 2027 revenue verification is highly leveraged: construction-side financing precedes demand-side contract lock-in, so if revenue verification fails, the renegotiation cascade will unfold in the order "unleased leases → RPOs → SPV debt."

This structural judgment supports two pricing decisions. First, Scenario C's 20% probability: the financing ring's fragility is endogenous and structural, not the incidental result of external shocks, so its probability should not be discounted as a traditional "tail event" but priced as "the conditional rupture probability of accumulated leverage at a specific verification milestone." Second, the upper bound of the A-scenario capex range, $3.2 trillion, carries a discount relative to the Dell'Oro measure — financing constraints suppress full-throttle spending even within the baseline path.

### Why Upside Is Only 5%: Scenario D's Narrow Corridor

Scenario D (IT installations exceeding 230 GW, single-year capex above $3.5 trillion, and AI annual revenue ≥ $2 trillion all holding simultaneously) receives only 5%, because its trigger conditions require three independent evidence chains to break upward at once: an AI revenue explosion delivering, power bottlenecks clearing rapidly, and the financing ring not rupturing under high leverage. Scenario analysis shows the joint probability of the three chains is far below any single chain — especially since the regime-shift judgment on power constraints implies that even if demand explodes, regulatory-allocation constraints will suppress delivery pace before 2030. 5% is not a denial of upside possibility, but a pricing of the conjunctive structure requiring "three conditions satisfied simultaneously."

### Structural Divergence and Regional Contribution: Filling in the Skeleton's Space

The framework's probability distribution is jointly supported by segment-level and regional evidence. The four segments diverge along the fault line of "whether they serve AI training workloads": hyperscale (including Neocloud) and colocation form the twin cores of symbiotic expansion, together absorbing the vast majority of global incremental IT capacity from 2026 to 2030; we estimate the 2030 capacity split at roughly hyperscale 60–67%, colocation 22–28%, enterprise self-build 8–12%, and edge under 3%. The enterprise share has entered a long-cycle contraction, and the edge segment garners narrative attention but has an extremely low physical-scale ceiling — this structure caps the realization of the "soft components" within total pipeline volume, consistent with the discount logic of Scenario A.

At the regional level, the United States is the only market pushing all three parameters to extremes: the fiercest capex arms race, the earliest grid-politics detonation, and the heaviest pipeline inflation. It is therefore both the largest contributor to Scenario A and the largest risk source for the 50% downside tail — the 2030 global landing point's elasticity to the single parameter of "US pipeline realization rate" exceeds that of any other variable, with each notch of shift translating into a displacement on the order of 5–10 GW in the global interval. Under the LBNL measure, the reference case for US data center electricity consumption in 2030 is 649 TWh (range 521–843 TWh, or 11.8% of total US electricity), with the US and China combined accounting for nearly 80% of the global increment.

China is the mirror-image case of constraint form: capital and demand pose no constraint, policy is actively compressing total volume, the only hard constraint sits in chip supply, and its determinant is Beijing customs rather than Washington licenses. We estimate China's 2030 IT installations at roughly 50–65 GW (probability-weighted midpoint around 55 GW), one quarter to three tenths of the global baseline range — a contributor with restrained volume and a structure tilted toward intelligent computing. The key inference: China's principal downside trigger is endogenous chip-output ramping, not PJM auctions or a financing-ring rupture, so Chinese variance has limited influence on the probability allocation among A/B/C and mainly affects the absolute level of the global interval. This judgment is self-consistent with the skeleton: Scenario B's attribution conditions (evidence of power-related delays) are designed with the US grid signal as the observational anchor precisely because the US-side realization rate is the decisive variable of the probability distribution.

### How We Weigh Prediction-Market Anchors

Where price signals exist, we use prediction markets as calibrators of Bayesian priors, not as deciders. Three principles govern the treatment: first, markets are price-efficient on near-term questions settleable before 2027 (such as whether a specific year's capex guidance is met), and receive heavier weight; second, on long-horizon structural questions at the 2030 mark, market liquidity is thin, participant composition is skewed, and pricing often reflects pipeline face value rather than discounted measures, so these serve only as a reference floor; third, wherever market-implied probabilities diverge from our scenario analysis, the evidence chain prevails — this report's combined downside tail of 50% is significantly fatter than the distribution implied by most narratives, and this deviation is the result of the framework explicitly pricing the financing dark side and regulatory allocation politics. We own it and present it explicitly, rather than regressing toward market consensus.

### Final Defense of the Skeleton Probabilities

In sum, the internal logic of the probability distribution is: Scenario A 35%, because it is not the most optimistic scenario but the only one in which the two evidence chains — demand authenticity and supply constraints — point in opposite directions with comparable magnitude; the hardest demand anchor and the hardest constraint anchor offset each other, pressing the landing point into the upper-middle 190–210 GW; Scenario B 30%, because the regime shift of power constraints is a high-confidence judgment, and the only uncertainty is whether its suppression magnitude suffices to push capacity below 190 GW; Scenario C 20%, because financing fragility exists structurally, but its eruption requires failure of 2027 revenue verification as a trigger, making it a conditional rather than inevitable risk; Scenario D 5%, because the conjunctive conditions are too narrow; Scenario E 10%, absorbing all hybrid paths where interval boundaries and auxiliary conditions go unmet — including cases where capacity lands within the interval but lacks corresponding attribution evidence. These five probabilities are collectively exhaustive and mutually exclusive, and each can be traced directly back to the offsetting structure of the framework's three parameters and two hard evidence chains. ## I. Forecast Overview: A Morphological Portrait and Core Conclusions for the Global Data Center Market in 2030

Every subsequent chapter of this report is anchored to a single judgment laid out in this section: **by the end of 2030, the most likely shape of the global data center market is not a figure extrapolated by any single institution, but a wide range jointly shaped by pipeline conversion rates, power constraints, and financing structures—global cumulative IT installed capacity of 190–210 GW and single-year capital expenditure of $2.5–3.2 trillion are the highest-probability landing zone, but hard power constraints and financing tightening together form a 50% downside tail, making this market one of the highest-variance infrastructure bets of this generation**. This chapter first presents the complete scenario distribution and the capacity and spending paths, then addresses a question no serious forecast can avoid: why the four authoritative institutions differ by more than a factor of two on the same 2030 figures, and why this report chooses IT installed capacity and a pipeline-discounted delivery basis as its primary anchor.

### Scenario Distribution: A Moderate Baseline and a Heavy Downside Tail

This report's core forecasts for the December 31, 2030 point in time are presented as five mutually exclusive scenarios (all probabilities are this report's estimates):

- **A Baseline Expansion (35%)**: By the end of 2030, global IT installed capacity lands in the 190–210 GW range, and single-year global data center capex in 2029 or 2030 falls between $2.5–3.2 trillion—provided that an authoritative basis (Dell'Oro or LBNL) confirms both ranges hold simultaneously. The implicit mechanism of this scenario is that pipeline realization settles in the mid-to-high band between historical base rates and contracted pipelines: speculative announcements naturally decay, while contracted portions deliver steadily.
- **B Power-Constrained (30%)**: Capacity slides to 150–185 GW, attributable to observable power delays—PJM 2028/29 or 2029/30 auction shortfalls exceeding 5,000 MW (the 2027/28 auction already posted a record 6,623 MW reliability shortfall for the first time), or three or more states extending new-build moratoriums beyond 2029.
- **C Financial Tightening (20%)**: U.S. single-year capex falls below $1.5 trillion by or before the end of 2027, with the quarterly renegotiation rate of non-commenced leases exceeding 10%, or two or more hyperscalers cutting multi-year capex guidance by more than 25%—the trigger being a contract-renegotiation cascade following the failure of AI revenue validation.
- **D Upside Surprise (5%)**: Capacity exceeds 230 GW, single-year capex exceeds $3.5 trillion, and annual AI revenue reaches $2 trillion—all three conditions must hold simultaneously, which is why this can only be a thin tail.
- **E Other/Hybrid (10%)**: Paths that do not fall into any of the complete determination combinations above, including mutually offsetting negative feedback between the two downside scenarios and hybrid outcomes at the range's edges.

Three structural features of this distribution deserve to be stated up front. First, **the baseline scenario carries only 35%—meaning "linear extrapolation of the current narrative" is not this report's default answer**. The evidence along the two fault lines of power and financing is physical and institutional, not sentiment-driven: GE Vernova heavy-duty gas turbines are sold out before 2028, with only about 10 GW of remaining slots for 2029–30 and lead times of about 3 years; the combined free cash flow of the four major hyperscalers has fallen from $237 billion in 2024 to $200 billion in 2025, with Amazon's trailing-twelve-month FCF turning negative at −$7.6 billion. Second, **B and C together total 50% but cannot simply be priced additively**: power constraints physically defer spending and thus delay financing pressure, while financing contraction in turn relieves power queues—this negative feedback is the core reason this report assigns no higher weight to a "joint collapse" and retains a 10% Scenario E. Third, Scenario D's 5% is not disdain for the upside but reverence for joint conditions: the probability that the physical ceilings of capacity, spending, and annual AI revenue—TSMC CoWoS advanced packaging capacity (ramping from about 75,000–80,000 wafers/month to 120,000–140,000 wafers/month by the end of 2026, forming a physical ceiling on accelerator shipments), gas turbine slots, and revenue validation—are all simultaneously breached before 2030 is extremely low; TrendForce's 2030 grid gap of 268 GW is itself internal testimony to the unattainability of the unconstrained upper bound.

### Capacity Path: A Four-Year Climb from 122.9 GW

The starting point is firm: global data-center capacity stood at 2025 GW in 122.9 GW, with 2026 projected at 161 GW, a single-year increase of 31%; measured on a facility-capacity basis, JLL records that the doubling of the world from 103 GW to 200 GW is occurring between 2025 and 2030 (a CAGR of roughly 14%). This report's baseline range of 190–210 GW implies that the 2026–2030 period still requires an average compound annual growth of about 18–23%—a deceleration from 31% in 2026, but not a cliff-edge deceleration. This "decelerating yet still steep" trajectory shape is itself a forecasting judgment: the first half (2026–2028) is suppressed by physical bottlenecks such as gas-turbine slots and three-year transformer lead times, while in the second half (2028–2030) the pace is set by regulatory allocation as equipment supply capacity comes on stream—the constraint shifts from "whether there is power" to "who gets the power." The LBNL 2025 update revises U.S. data-center electricity use in 2030 upward to a reference case of 649 TWh (range 521–843 TWh, or 11.8% of total U.S. power), and its scenario sensitivity explicitly acknowledges the downside branch in which "actual IT equipment deployments fall short of projections"—meaning the upward revision embeds a realization-rate discount, consistent with this report's approach.

The magnitude shift in single-year capex is even more dramatic. In 2026, the four major U.S. hyperscalers' combined capex guidance was about $735–760 billion (Alphabet 195–205 billion, Amazon about 220 billion, Microsoft about 175 billion after reclassification, and Meta 130–145 billion); on a Gartner basis, global data center systems spending reached $822 billion in 2026. By 2030, this report's baseline range is $2.5–3.2 trillion, overlapping the lower half of Dell'Oro's latest extrapolation of "exceeding 3 trillion in 2030, with the four majors accounting for about half of the global total." In other words, this report expects global single-year capex to grow roughly threefold again within four years—every step-up in this leap is capped by the dual constraints of power and financing, and this report's slightly more conservative stance relative to Dell'Oro is essentially a discount for financing capacity: Moody's estimates that of $1.2 trillion in lease commitments, about $820 billion remain non-commenced, and the debt market's capacity to absorb incremental supply carries tightening risk beyond 2028 that demand extrapolations have not priced in.

Regionally, the United States and China account for nearly 80% of the global increment, but the two are on fundamentally different paths: the United States sustains arms-race-style expansion through debt-financed funding, while China executes "total volume down, computing power up"—China Mobile's total capex fell 9.5% in 2026 even as computing-network investment grew 62.4%, and the hard ceiling on its training-grade computing power lies not in power or money but in Huawei Ascend 910C's planned 600,000-unit output for 2026 and HBM supply. This means that within the global 190–210 GW landing zone, China's contribution is structurally "less but finer"—official policy bans new small-scale computing projects, while leading third-party IDCs (GDS utilization 77.5%) expand at full load even as regional intelligent-computing centers run at only about 30% utilization. As of the end of 2025, China's nominal intelligent-computing capacity was about 1.59 million PFLOPS (on the FP16 basis), which cannot be mixed with the MIIT system's official figures of 280→962 EFLOPS due to differences in precision conversion and statistical scope—the gap between nominal and effective computing power is itself the Chinese-side mirror of the global measurement problem.

### The Battle of Definitions: Why 490.7 GW, 200 GW, and 219 GW Are All Simultaneously Valid

Anyone who has read forecasts from multiple institutions has been troubled by an apparent contradiction: for global data center capacity in 2030, TrendForce gives 490.7 GW, JLL gives 200 GW, McKinsey gives 219 GW (of which 156 GW is AI load), and earlier figures even stop at 122 GW. These numbers differ by up to fourfold—but this report's conclusion after verification is: **most of the divergence is a difference in problem definition, not a numerical contradiction**. Taken apart:

- **TrendForce's 490.7 GW is an "unconstrained power demand ceiling"**: it implicitly assumes full realization of the interconnection queue and explicitly excludes behind-the-meter generation, while also projecting a grid supply-demand gap of 2030 268 GW (over 170 GW in the United States)—and this gap figure is itself an admission that the ceiling is unattainable. Measured against the LBNL-tracked interconnection queue (over 2,060 GW) with a historical realization base rate of only about 13%, the 490.7 GW figure should be treated as a stress-test ceiling rather than a forecast. FERC co-location rules allow behind-the-meter generation to partially offset the grid gap, but the scale of that offset is constrained by turbine scheduling and fuel supply, and cannot change the ceiling's nature.
- **JLL's 200 GW is a facility-capacity delivery metric**: it counts facility capacity that will actually be built and delivered by 2030, landing almost precisely at the midpoint of this report's baseline range. Its limitation is publication timing (January 2026), which did not fully capture the momentum if 2026's +31% growth rate were partially sustained through 2027–2028—which is why this report raises the upper bound of the range to 210 GW.
- **McKinsey's 219 GW is a demand-side estimate**: it back-derives capacity requirements from the compute demands of AI workloads, sitting between the delivery metric and the unconstrained metric [S22].
- **Low outliers were mostly published in early 2025 and were not updated through the upward revision cycle**: in an environment where Dell'Oro nearly doubled its single-year capex forecast for 2030 from 1.2 trillion to over 3 trillion within seven months, and Gartner revised upward three times in nine months, any extrapolation older than a year is already obsolete.

The evidence from caliber layering also holds on the pipeline side. Announced pipelines of roughly 410 GW at ERCOT and about 53.8 GW at Dominion versus LBNL's historical base rate of 13% imply a 5–10-fold caliber gap; Georgia Power's large-load pipeline net shrank by 6 GW in a single quarter to 50.9 GW, and regulatory testimony assessed that about 65% of announced MW are speculative [S18]—direct empirical evidence of pipeline padding. By contrast, AEP's contracted pipeline doubled to 56–69 GW, showing a significantly higher realization rate for the signed-contract portion. The expert estimate cited by Utility Dive—speculative interconnection applications running 5–10 times actual completed projects—together with the 13% base rate constitutes two independent layers of evidence. **Contracted pipelines and announced pipelines are two different kinds of assets; lumping them together in a single "pipeline" discussion is the single largest source of forecast error in this field.**

### Primary anchor selection: why IT installed capacity and the discounted delivery metric serve as the standard

Based on the metric dissection above, this report selects **IT installed capacity (the IT load a data center can accommodate) and pipeline-discounted actual delivery** as its primary anchor, for three reasons. First, IT installed capacity is the only metric that connects directly to the economics of compute—capex buys servers and racks, revenue is generated from the tokens running on them, and neither facility capacity nor unconstrained demand can close the "input-output" loop. Second, the delivery metric is the only one that can be factually verified: a facility is either energized or it is not, which makes this report's scenario determination criteria falsifiable, whereas "unconstrained demand" is never wrong and can never be verified. Third, and most importantly: **in an environment of systematically padded pipelines, discounting is not conservatism but fidelity to the empirical base rate**. The 13% historical base rate applies to announced pipelines, while contracted pipelines are clearly higher—Georgia's roughly 65% of announced MW assessed as speculative implies that after removing speculation, the realization rate of the contracted portion rises substantially—this report takes a mid-to-high band for the blended pipeline, a choice that itself carries a capacity uncertainty of about ±20 GW, which this report chooses to acknowledge explicitly rather than pretend to eliminate.

The opposing view that must be addressed head-on is this: over the past two years, every bearish signal—the Microsoft 2 GW order cancellation reported by TD Cowen (later judged, based on vacancy data, to be a rebalancing between owned and leased capacity rather than a demand collapse), and the Georgia pipeline contraction—was ultimately absorbed by upward spending revisions. Why should anyone believe that "calibration discipline" can outrun reflexivity this time? This report's answer: the revision cycle has always absorbed bad news on the supply side and the pipeline side; it has never been genuinely tested by bad news on the revenue side—the gap between roughly $60 billion of AI revenue against roughly $400 billion of capex in 2025 is precisely that unexploded test case. Physical constraints (gas turbine lead times, CoWoS capacity) do not expand just because extrapolations are revised upward—that is the basis for leaving only 5% for the upside.

This primary anchor also determines the report's explicit divergences from the baselines of the four major institutions: about 2.4 times below TrendForce (whose calibration is a theoretical ceiling, still used as the reference for the upper edge of Scenario D); nearly coincident with JLL but with the ceiling extended to 210 GW; the smallest divergence from McKinsey—the baseline range can be viewed as its demand estimates converted to a delivered-capacity basis after applying a realization discount of roughly 85–95%; and Dell'Oro's capex figures are given a discount for financing capacity. The near-doubling self-correction by forecasting institutions within nine months [S1][S2] is itself the most important calibration lesson: in this field, the width of the range carries more information than the midpoint.

### This chapter's conclusions and the roadmap for the full report

To sum up this chapter: the shape of the global data center market in 2030 is a probability structure of **"a moderate baseline + a heavy downside tail + a thin upside tail"**—35% probability toward 190–210 GW with $2.5–3.2 trillion of the pipeline realized at a discount; 50% probability forced into the lower band by power (30%) or financing (20%); only 5% probability of exceeding 230 GW under the demanding conditions of a simultaneous revenue breakout and bottleneck relief. The three parameters that determine the bifurcation, ranked by observability: pipeline realization rate (where the outcome lands between the 13% base rate and the contracted pipeline); the regime shift in the power constraint (physical before 2028, regulatory-allocation-based thereafter); and the accumulation of leverage in the financing loop (the commencement pace of $820 billion in not-yet-commenced leases). The hardest anchor on the demand side—Oracle RPO $638 billion—and the softest anchor—the "shelved" NVIDIA–OpenAI hundred-billion LOI—exist simultaneously, which is precisely the embodiment of the judgment that "demand is real but stratified in its pricing." Every forecast in this report was written to be tested by the data from those few quarters.

## II. The Demand-Side Engine: AI Compute Expansion, the Revenue Verification Gap, and the Sustainability of the Forecast-Revision Loop

The demand side is the primary driver of this round of global data center expansion, but it is also the most fragile link in the entire forecasting framework. This report's base judgment on the demand side is: **the "visibility signals" of demand are real and enormous in magnitude, but their quality exhibits systematic layering—from signed RPOs and self-reported bookings to LOIs and unleased leases, binding force decays step by step; and the structural gap of roughly 6–7x between AI revenue and capex means the sustainability of the entire expansion loop hinges on the revenue verification milestone in 2027**. This judgment directly underpins this report's assignment of a 35% probability to the baseline expansion scenario, rather than the higher weight implied by the market narrative.

### The Three-Layer Structure of Demand Visibility: Decay of Binding Force from RPO to LOI

The hardest evidence on the demand side comes from contractually binding commitments. Oracle's remaining performance obligations (RPO) disclosed as of its FY26 annual report reached $638 billion, including approximately $75 billion in customer prepayments, FY27 capex guidance $90 billion. RPO represents legally binding unfulfilled contracts under accounting standards—this is the highest-quality tier of demand signal. The second tier is supplier self-reported "visibility": NVIDIA recorded $215.9 billion in revenue in FY26, and management's publicly stated bookings have advanced from $500 billion in October 2025 toward "$1 trillion by 2027"—but bookings are not contracts; they mix confirmed orders, framework agreements, and intentions, making them a demand thermometer rather than demand itself. The third tier is the weakest: the NVIDIA–OpenAI $100 billion/10 GW agreement was from the outset an LOI (the September 2025 signing used "intent" language), and per a WSJ 2026 report it is already on hold—non-binding arrangements can vanish silently when the cycle turns, making this the first validated sample of soft-commitment failure in the current cycle.

This three-tier structure determines this report's precise formulation of "demand authenticity": the demand is real, but **a substantial portion of what the market prices with first-tier certainty is in fact second- and third-tier soft commitments**. The market widely points to OpenAI as Oracle's approximately $300 billion largest customer, but that figure comes from a single WSJ source and has never been publicly confirmed by either party—meaning that on Oracle's 638 billion RPO chain, the performance capacity of the largest single node depends on OpenAI's financing capacity rather than operating cash flow. A similar concentration problem exists on the Neocloud side: Microsoft alone once accounted for 62% of CoreWeave's 2024 revenue.

The spending guidance of the four hyperscalers constitutes the execution layer of demand, and it has continued to be revised upward rather than converging through 2026: Alphabet raised from its earlier $175–185 billion range to 1950–$205 billion, Amazon's cash capex basis was raised to approximately $220 billion, Microsoft stands at approximately $175 billion after depreciation/lease reclassification with CFO Amy Hood stating explicitly that "the plan is unchanged," and Meta guides to $130–145 billion—the four combined total approximately $735–760 billion, a year-over-year increase exceeding 60%. Notable is management's attribution style: Alphabet CEO Pichai attributes it to supply constraints, and Amazon's Jassy stated that capacity was insufficient in both 2026–2027—three companies with highly consistent framing: not "we want more," but "we cannot get enough." This supply-constrained narrative itself forms a psychological foundation: it defines continued escalation as a rational response rather than overinvestment.

### The Revenue Verification Gap: The 60 billion-versus-400 billion Arithmetic

The core tension on the demand side can be summarized in a simple piece of arithmetic: data compiled by Quinn Emanuel shows AI-related revenue of approximately $60 billion in 2025, against same-basis capex of approximately $400 billion—a gap of nearly 7x; sustaining expansion beyond 2027 requires a magnitude of approximately $2 trillion in annual revenue per Bain's estimate. It should be noted that another widely cited estimate of "LLM annualized revenue of approximately 50 billion versus 400 billion" has been downgraded to unverified because the original post cannot be traced; this report's gap argument is anchored primarily in the Quinn Emanuel figures and company-level disclosures. A capex-revenue mismatch is nothing new—telecom fiber and railroad history offer precedents—but this cycle's distinctiveness lies in **spending being front-loaded by debt and off-balance-sheet structures (see Chapter IV), while revenue verification is pushed back**; the time gap between the two is precisely the window in which risk accumulates.

But "revenue verification failure" is not a uniform event. Decisive bifurcation has already emerged on the monetization side: Anthropic's ARR rose from $9 billion to above $44 billion, with gross margin improving from 38% to above 70%—if this curve is extrapolated, it suggests the unit economics of frontier models are closing rapidly. User-base evidence on the demand side is also expanding: ChatGPT's weekly active users on February 2026 have exceeded 900 million. The counterpoint is OpenAI's discipline signal: on February 2026 it was reported to have cut its pre-2030 compute spending target from a previously committed basis of approximately $1.4 trillion down to approximately $600 billion. It must be emphasized that this is a single source, and commitments (including LOIs/frameworks) and spending targets are different bases; but even with a discounted reading, this is the clearest proactive contraction signal from the demand side to date—the largest single compute buyer is resetting the denominator of future spending. The implication for the financing loop is direct: the marginal increments of visibility indicators such as Oracle's RPO, CoreWeave's backlog, and NVIDIA bookings will all be repriced as OpenAI resets its budget.

### The Upward-Revision Loop: Why Bearish Signals Have All Failed Thus Far

This report considers the most important mechanistic feature of the demand side to be **the co-directional feedback loop formed by vendor guidance and third-party forecasts**. Dell'Oro's forecast for single-year capex in 2030 was revised upward within seven months from $1.2 trillion in 2029 to over $3 trillion in 2030—nearly doubling; Gartner revised upward three times within nine months to $822 billion for 2026. Third-party upward revisions provide "market consensus" cover for vendor escalation, and vendor escalation in turn supplies the data for the next round of revisions—the loop is self-reinforcing.

To test whether this loop has absorbed negative information, consider two cases. First, Microsoft reclassified certain leases, which was briefly read as a demand-weakening signal; its CY2026 capex subsequently landed at a magnitude of approximately $175 billion, and the signal was overwhelmed by spending. Second, Georgia Power's large-load pipeline net-shrank by 6 GW to 50.9 GW, with regulatory testimony assessing that approximately 65% of announced MW was speculative [S18]—direct empirical evidence of pipeline padding; yet over the same period AEP's contracted pipeline doubled from 56 GW to 69 GW, and expansion on the contracted basis once again overwhelmed contraction on the announced basis. **Thus far every bearish signal has been overwhelmed by stronger spending evidence—that is a fact; but inferring from this that "the loop will continue forever" is an inductive fallacy**—the loop has absorbed bad news from the supply side and the pipeline; it has never truly been tested by bad news from the revenue side. The 60 billion-versus-400 billion gap in 2025 is precisely that unexploded test case.

### The Loop's Boundary: Four Transmission Chains Before the 2027 Verification Milestone

This report judges that the upward-revision loop will most likely persist until the AI revenue verification milestone in 2027—this is one of the core underpinnings of the baseline scenario receiving the highest probability—but the transmission chains that could prematurely break the loop must be explicitly listed:

1. **Reverse transmission along the RPO chain**. Oracle's 638 billion RPO depends on large-customer commitments; if any key node (especially OpenAI) contracts, pressure will renegotiate along the sequence "unleased leases → RPO → SPV debt" (mechanism detailed in Chapters IV and IX). The observable falsification threshold is already clear: a single-quarter RPO decline exceeding $100 billion triggers a baseline-scenario revaluation.
2. **Leverage scrutiny of the vendor-financing loop**. Dallas Fed and Chicago Fed 2026 research notes named the circular pattern of NVIDIA injecting equity/warrants into OpenAI, xAI, and CoreWeave, with those customers then using the funds to purchase NVIDIA systems; the BIS annual report further flagged off-balance-sheet sale-leaseback structures, offering the benchmark that AI contributed approximately 1 percentage points to US GDP [S26]. Regulators' repeated flagging means "what share of revenue is nominal demand created by vendor financing" will become a central question in 2027 audits and ratings.
3. **Marginal deterioration of free cash flow**. Combined FCF of the four hyperscalers has already fallen from $237 billion in 2024 to $200 billion in 2025, and Amazon's TTM FCF turned negative at −$7.6 billion; Barclays forecasts Meta's FCF will turn negative in 2027–28. When on-balance-sheet cash flow and off-balance-sheet structures come under pressure simultaneously, the funding source of the upward-revision loop will be forcibly constrained.
4. **Accumulation of earnings-quality skepticism**. Burry alleged that hyperscalers artificially extended GPU depreciation schedules, under-provisioning depreciation by approximately $176 billion across 2026–28, with Meta's 2028 profit allegedly overstated by approximately 21% and Oracle's by approximately 27% (CNBC explicitly stated it could not independently verify). Whether the allegations are true or not, they direct market attention to one question: in years when capex far exceeds operating cash flow, how wide is the rift between reported profit and economic profit—precisely the variable that will be repriced first if revenue verification fails.

### Conclusion: Demand Is "Real Enough" but No Longer "Infinitely Real"

In sum, this report's characterization of the demand side is consistent with the probability framework: demand is real and of sufficient magnitude to support the baseline landing zone of 190–210 GW, but an unconstrained figure of 490.7 GW implicitly assumes full realization of soft commitments and does not constitute a forecasting anchor. That OpenAI's downward revision and Anthropic's breakout are occurring simultaneously indicates the industry is shifting from "wholesale betting" to "differentiated pricing"—which in effect **lowers** the probability of the upside-surprise scenario (D, 5%) (whose trigger requires three conditions, including AI annual revenue reaching $2 trillion, to be met simultaneously), while making the middle path—"revenue verification delivers a good-enough rather than perfect report card"—the most likely outcome. The demand-side engine is still running, but its fuel is shifting from narrative to the income statement; 2027 is the one reading moment on the dashboard that cannot be avoided.

## III. The Hard Power Constraint: A Transformation in Constraint Form from Physical Bottleneck to Regulatory Allocation (2026–2030)

III. The Hard Power Constraint: A Transformation in Constraint Form from Physical Bottleneck to Regulatory Allocation (2026–2030)

Electricity is the only input in this round of data center expansion that cannot be circumvented through financial engineering. This report's core judgment on the form of the power constraint during 2026–2030 is: **the constraint will not disappear, but it will transform in form—from 2026–2028 it manifests as a physical bottleneck (gas turbine slots and equipment lead times), and after 2028 it shifts to a regulatory-allocation constraint (colocation rules, state-level moratoriums, and the allocation politics formed by roughly 370 state bills)**. This transformation determines the dividing line between this report's Scenario B (power-constrained, 30% probability) and Scenario A (baseline expansion, 35% probability): if the marginal easing of the physical bottleneck is slower than the pace at which the demand pipeline is realized, global IT installed capacity in 2030 will slide into the B-range of 150–185 GW; conversely, behind-the-meter generation and colocation rules will absorb part of the gap, preserving the baseline range of 190–210 GW. The structure of this constraint is elaborated below across three layers.

### The Physical Layer: The First Hard Wall of Turbine Slots and Equipment Lead Times

The physical starting point of this round of power constraints lies in the equipment supply chain rather than the grid itself. GE Vernova's cumulative heavy-duty gas turbine contracts have reached approximately 100 GW (with 44 GW on hand and 56 GW in slot reservations), and only about 10 GW of production capacity for 2029–30 remains unbooked, with lead times of roughly three years. This means that a single monopolist supplier's production schedule effectively sets a mathematical ceiling on new gas-fired grid interconnections in the United States before 2028: projects ordered in 2026 can be energized at the earliest in 2029, and the 2029 slots are already essentially sold out. Transformers represent the second squeeze under the same logic—the IEA notes that transformer delivery lead times have doubled in three years and gas turbine delivery schedules run to several years, putting roughly 20% planned data center projects at risk of delay. It must be emphasized that this 20% is a delay mechanism of equipment supply chain constraints, mechanistically entirely distinct from delays caused by regulatory intervention (such as the roughly 20% of the U.S. pipeline covered by the Texas moratorium, per BNEF); the numerical coincidence of the two does not constitute mutual validation—this report treats them as two independent delay sources in its scenario weighting.

The macro-level quantification of the physical constraint comes from TrendForce: unconstrained global data center power demand capacity reaches 490.7 GW in 2030, of which the grid supply-demand gap is 268 GW, with the United States accounting for more than 170 GW. This gap figure must be interpreted with caution—it explicitly excludes behind-the-meter generation and assumes full realization of interconnection queues, and should therefore be regarded as "the ceiling pressure of unconstrained demand" rather than "the outage gap that will actually occur." But even after discounting, supply pressure is still accumulating rapidly: the LBNL 2025 update (published June 2026) revised upward U.S. data center electricity consumption in 2030 to 649 TWh (range 521–843 TWh, or 11.8% of total U.S. electricity), corroborating the roughly +240 TWh increment in the IEA's base case for the United States. Under the LBNL reference case, U.S. data center electricity consumption nearly triples within five years, while dispatchable new gas capacity over the same period can scarcely expand in parallel due to GE Vernova's slot constraints—this is the most important physical basis for the 30% probability of Scenario B.

Here the opposing view must be steelmanned: some argue that the sellout of turbine slots is itself a signal of market clearing—high prices will spur substitute technologies (fuel cells, small modular nuclear, storage-plus-renewables combinations), and GE Vernova is also expanding capacity. This report's response is: the scaling timelines of all substitute routes fall after 2029, whereas the energization window of the demand pipeline is concentrated in 2027–2029; this timing mismatch means substitute technologies can only ease constraints from 2030 onward and cannot rescue deliveries due before 2028. This is precisely the core of the "constraint form transformation" judgment—the physical bottleneck is not being lifted, but is being handed off, propelled by time, to the regulatory layer.

### The Market Pricing Layer: PJM Auctions Turn from Price Signal into Political Flashpoint

The second layer of constraint becomes explicit through the price mechanism, and PJM is its largest quantifiable vehicle. Its trajectory is clearly traceable: the 2026/27 capacity auction held July 2025 hit the price cap at 329.17/MW-day with a total cost of $16.1 billion [S15]; by the 2027/28 auction, the clearing price rose to roughly 333.44/MW-day with a total of $16.4 billion, and for the first time reliability requirements went unmet—a shortfall of 6,623 MW emerged. Utility Dive, citing PJM's own report, notes that if data center load growth persists, PJM reliability could begin to fail before 2030, with the reliability critical point projected to arrive as early as 2027. The first failure to meet reliability requirements is a qualitative signal: the auction has shifted from "allocating scarcity" to "scarcity itself being insufficient to allocate."

The political consequences of prices are generating opposing narratives. PJM officially claims its auctions raised retail electricity bills by only 1.5–5%; IEEFA and consumer groups attribute the retail price increases primarily to data center load—the experience of Pennsylvania customers facing price increases of +14.6% is translating into state-level political pressure, and both accounts must be presented side by side without this report adjudicating between them. What matters is the transmission path of the attribution dispute: regardless of the true attribution, voter-perceived price increases flow toward state legislatures and FERC hearing procedures, supplying political fuel for the third layer of regulatory constraint. If the 6,623 MW shortfall of the 2027/28 auction widens beyond 5,000 MW in the 2028/29 auction (compounding rather than replacing), this report will raise the probabilities of political tightening and Scenario B—this is the fourth indicator on Chapter Twelve's monitoring dashboard.

### The Regulatory Layer: The Fork Between Colocation Rules and the Freezing Path

The third layer of constraint is taking shape at the institutional level, and the United States and Europe have already taken two sharply divergent paths. The U.S. path is "pricing the constraint": on December 18, 2025 FERC issued its colocation order (193 FERC ¶61,217), directing PJM to establish four transmission service options, under which colocation loads may be curtailed during a transition period before network upgrades are completed. The symmetry of this order deserves note—it both opens an institutional door to colocating data centers with generation resources (FERC had previously rejected, on November 2024, the Talen–Amazon interconnection expansion agreement at the Susquehanna nuclear plant [S17]) and retains the authority to curtail colocation loads, i.e., trading interruptibility for access rights. The European path, by contrast, is "outright queue rejection": since January 2022 EirGrid has imposed a de facto freeze on new data center connections in Dublin, with no lifting for the foreseeable future. This precedent demonstrates that a leading market can survive for more than six years under an administrative freeze—it shows the end-state form of a power constraint hardening into a regulatory constraint, and provides a ready-made template for U.S. state policy. If more than three U.S. states maintain new-build moratoriums beyond 2029, one of this report's trigger conditions for Scenario B is met.

The FERC colocation order also has a frequently underestimated function: it legitimizes the behind-the-meter bypass route. Oracle's fuel cell solution is a textbook case—using off-balance-sheet assets plus on-site generation to bypass the grid queue and colocation curtailment risk; its RPO $638 billion-driven expansion pace partly depends on this physical bypass. Of the 268 GW grid gap estimated by TrendForce, a portion will be converted into behind-the-meter gas and fuel cell markets rather than actual capacity loss [S7]—but the scale of this diversion is subject to the triple constraints of fuel supply, permitting, and cost, and this report judges that it can absorb only a small fraction of the gap, insufficient to dislodge electricity's status as the dominant constraint. This also explains why Scenario D (upside surprise, 5%) lists FERC colocation rules and behind-the-meter solutions as one of the few upside amplifiers, yet assigns them so low a probability: the bypass route eases the constraint around 2030 and is powerless against the physical wall of 2027–2028.

### The Timing and Scenario Implications of the Constraint Form Transformation

Synthesizing the three-layer structure, this report offers the following timing judgment for 2026–2030:

- **2026–2028: physical constraints dominate.** Turbine slots (sold out before 2028) and three-year transformer lead times are the binding constraints; no policy can physically deliver a turbine ahead of schedule that has not yet been manufactured. At this stage, power-related delays manifest as project postponements and cost escalation, not yet as large-scale politicization.
- **After 2028: regulation-allocated constraints take over.** Equipment capacity gradually comes online (GE Vernova's 2029–30 has only about 10 GW of tight remaining slots ), but PJM's reliability gap, the dispute over electricity price attribution, and state-level legislation (roughly 370 state bills) will determine who gets power first — the constraint shifts from "whether power exists" to "who gets it." FERC's four transmission service options under its co-location order and state moratoria together form the two major testing grounds for allocation rules [S16].
- **Fully parallel: behind-the-meter generation siphons off demand.** Oracle-type fuel cells and on-site gas solutions continue to divert demand away from grid shortfalls, but their scale is limited by physics and economics; they are a moderating variable, not a determining one.

The implications for the scenario distribution are as follows. Scenario B (30%) is triggered by a compounding of the physical and pricing layers: scarcity of GE Vernova turbine slots in 2029–30 combined with widening PJM 2027/28 gaps jointly compress deliveries, or state-level moratoria spread; its confirmation requires evidence of attributable power-related delays, not merely weakening demand. Scenario A (35%), by contrast, requires power delays to be absorbed by behind-the-meter generation, co-location rules, and natural pipeline attrition (a Georgia pipeline exiting within a single quarter, roughly 65% of announced MW judged speculative ) — in other words, part of the "power constraint" is actually self-digested by pipeline padding, which is the implicit mechanism that allows the base case to hold. Counterintuitively, the power constraint and the financing crunch analyzed in the next chapter (Scenario C) exhibit negative feedback: physical delays defer capital expenditure, easing cash strain; financing contraction reduces new projects, in turn easing power pressure. This mutual-exclusion mechanism is precisely why the two probabilities (30%+20%) do not simply sum to a higher total downside probability.

On the monitoring front, three observable milestones will calibrate this chapter's judgment earliest: the on-time energization rate for 2026Q4–2027Q2 (if actual deliveries come in below the pipeline by 20%, Scenario B's probability is raised by 10 percentage points), whether the PJM 2028/29 auction gap breaches 5,000 MW, and whether the first batch of co-located load under FERC's co-location options is actually curtailed. The ultimate form of the power constraint — PJM-style pricing versus EirGrid-style freezes — will be the single most important policy variable determining whether the United States can hold the 190–210 GW global benchmark range.

## Four. The Dark Side of the Financing Structure: Debt-ification, Off-Balance-Sheet Leverage, and the Accumulation of Fragility in the Financing Loop

This chapter breaks down the funding structure of the current expansion cycle. The core judgment is: **global data center construction has shifted from 'profit reinvestment' to a three-layer structure of 'upfront debt + off-balance-sheet leverage + vendor financing,' and the cost is that the entire financing loop's dependence on AI revenue validation in 2027 is highly leveraged—construction-side financing now precedes demand-side contract lock-in, and if revenue validation fails, the renegotiation cascade will unfold in the order of 'unleased leases → RPO → SPV debt'**. This judgment is the core basis for pricing the financial tightening scenario at 20% in this report, and also the reason for applying a discount to the upper bound of the single-year capex range ($3.2 trillion) in the base-case scenario for 2030 relative to the Dell'Oro figure.

### The Cash Squeeze: From Profit-Driven to Debt-Driven Capital Expenditure

The starting point of the financing dark side is a set of cash flow figures often drowned out by the narrative. Combined free cash flow of the four hyperscalers has fallen from $237 billion in 2024 to $200 billion in 2025, while over the same period their combined capex guidance for 2026 rose to roughly $735–760 billion —a gap of nearly $550 billion with opposite signs. The individual signals are sharper: Amazon's trailing-twelve-month FCF turned negative at −$7.6 billion, yet it raised its 2026 capex guidance to roughly $220 billion; Meta's single-quarter capex in the second quarter of 2026 reached $31.08 billion with quarterly FCF of only $784 million —at Meta's scale, quarterly FCF below $1 billion means capital expenditure can no longer be supported at the margin by operating cash flow. Microsoft, meanwhile, cushioned the apparent pressure through accounting treatment: a reclassification adjusting depreciation/lease useful lives from 15 years to 25 years lowered its CY2026 capex measure to roughly $175 billion —a treatment directly tied to the depreciation useful-life dispute at the end of this chapter.

The opposing view must be stated fairly: this is not a financial crisis in the standard sense. Most of the four hyperscalers still post positive annual FCF, their core cloud businesses are deeply profitable, and the ramp-up is a deliberate choice. What has truly changed is the **structure of marginal funding sources**—when capex growth (over 60% year-over-year) persistently runs several times faster than FCF growth, the increment must be absorbed by debt, off-balance-sheet entities, and vendor credit. The key variable determining the 2030 expansion ceiling is therefore not demand (Chapter Two) or power (Chapter Three), but the debt market's willingness to keep absorbing incremental supply. Barclays projects Meta's FCF will turn negative in 2027–28—if that materializes, the timing would coincide exactly with the AI revenue validation milestone, forming a convergence of twin pressures.

### Three Paths of Financing Innovation: JV Bonds, Debt-Funded Expansion, and Off-Balance-Sheet SPVs

**The first is structured JV bonds.** The benchmark case is the Meta–Blue Owl Capital Hyperion transaction: a joint venture was formed for the Louisiana data center, issuing $27.3 billion of JV bonds with a coupon of 6.581%, maturing in 2049, rated A+, with PIMCO anchoring roughly $18 billion, and Meta retaining only a 20% equity stake so that the data center's capital expenditure does not appear on its consolidated statements [S11]. Search evidence further shows that the A+ rating and spread of roughly +225 bp in this structure are in fact premised on Meta's guarantee—legally the risk is off balance sheet, but economically it remains with the sponsor; and Meta has already replicated this model in projects such as El Paso, showing this is not a one-off arrangement but a scalable financing pipeline. The BIS Annual Economic Report singles out sale-and-leaseback structures with exit clauses (of which Hyperion is an exemplar) as candidates for financial fragility, and offers the macro comparison that AI contributes roughly 1 percentage points to US GDP [S26]—for a central bank settlement institution to flag a single industry's off-balance-sheet leverage alongside its macro contribution is itself a rare regulatory signal.

**The second is leveraged, debt-funded expansion.** Oracle is the extreme case: expanding on credit backed by $638 billion of RPO (including customer prepayments/self-supplied GPUs of roughly $75 billion)—search results confirm a single $18 billion bond issuance on September 2025, FY26 Q4 FCF of −$23.7 billion—while simultaneously issuing FY27 capex $90 billion guidance, and bypassing grid queues through asset off-balancing and fuel cells. The essence of the Oracle model is using the order book as a balance sheet: the legal binding force of RPO is converted into a basis for debt pricing. The problem sits at the top of the chain: the market widely points to OpenAI as its roughly $300 billion major customer, but that figure comes from a single WSJ source and has never been confirmed by either party—the ultimate repayment capacity of hundreds of billions in debt rests on the financing capacity of a client that has not yet proven it can generate matching cash flows, not on operating revenue. This report sets "an RPO single-quarter decline exceeding $100 billion" as the baseline-scenario falsification threshold precisely because Oracle is the node on the loop with the thinnest leverage and fastest transmission.

**The third is the systematic accumulation of off-balance-sheet SPV debt.** Quinn Emanuel's survey shows cumulative off-balance-sheet SPV debt has already exceeded $120 billion within two years. Our research adds the supply-side industry landscape: Blue Owl and JPMorgan each injected roughly $13 billion-scale funding into the Abilene (OpenAI-related) SPV—showing that a dedicated pool of capital for absorbing data center off-balance-sheet financing has formed, with bank capital and private credit entering simultaneously. The common feature of these structures is: **the decision point of construction-side financing precedes the lock-in point of demand-side contracts**—the SPV issues bonds and builds first, then waits for leases to commence. Moody's estimates provide the full-scale calibration: roughly $820 billion of $1.2 trillion in lease commitments have not yet commenced, with direct debt of roughly $460 billion—the uncommenced portion is the widest crack between commitments and cash flow.

### The Vendor Financing Loop: A Circular Structure Twice Flagged by the Fed

The third layer of the dark side is equipment vendors injecting procurement capital into their own customers via equity and warrants. Both the Dallas Fed and the Chicago Fed 2026 research notes named the circular model in which NVIDIA injects equity/warrants into OpenAI, xAI, and CoreWeave, and those customers then use the funds to purchase NVIDIA systems. Our research provides the loop's specific thickness: roughly 2 billion of xAI's $20 billion Series E came from NVIDIA; the NVIDIA–OpenAI $100 billion/10GW agreement was from the outset an LOI and is reportedly on hold; AMD's 6GW deployment with OpenAI came with warrants for 160 million shares—the second GPU source is replicating the same structure. CoreWeave is the amplifying node on the loop: roughly $104 billion of backlog supports its debt stack, of which $8.5 billion in DDTLs has already received investment-grade ratings —new cloud vendor financing is penetrating investment-grade markets; and Microsoft alone once accounted for 62% of its 2024 revenue [S35] —the divergence between the "investment-grade label" and "single-customer concentration" is the mismatch the credit market has yet to reprice.

The risk of the circular structure lies not in the size of any single transaction, but in the fact that **revenue, demand, and capital expenditure mutually vouch for one another around the loop**: part of NVIDIA's booked revenue comes from customers it finances itself, the customers' ability to pay comes partly from NVIDIA's injections, and the debt market lends to other nodes on the loop on the basis of NVIDIA's revenue growth. When all nodes on the loop expand simultaneously, each link looks healthy; the problem only surfaces when revenue validation fails—at which point the "demand" propped up by vendor injections gets cut first, while the SPV debt and lease commitments backed by that demand simultaneously lose their cash flow source. The 2025 gap of roughly $60 billion in AI revenue against roughly $400 billion in capex means the 2027 validation milestone is this loop's first genuine external test.

### The Sequence of the Renegotiation Cascade and the Two-Tier Thresholds

If the test fails, the transmission sequence of stress can be fairly precisely deduced, because different instruments depend on cash flow at different points in time. **The first order of priority is uncommenced leases**: $820 billion have not yet generated cash flow, and cancellation or renegotiation carries the lowest accounting and credit cost—OpenAI's downward revision of its pre-2030 spending target from roughly the prior $1.4 trillion measure to roughly $600 billion (single source) has already demonstrated how this repricing happens silently. **The second is RPO**: contract cancellations require penalty costs, but in Oracle-style structures the RPO is itself the borrowing base, so contract shrinkage directly hits refinancing—our research confirms the market already lists Oracle's RPO alongside Moody's uncommenced leases as the two monitorable falsification indicators. **Only the third is SPV debt**: bondholders rank last but absorb the most complete losses, and Hyperion's 2049 maturity means the renegotiation window closed long ago. This report's monitoring rule is a two-tier ladder: if the single-quarter renegotiation rate of uncommenced leases exceeds 5%, raise the probability of the financial tightening scenario; if it exceeds 10%, switch to a credit-contraction determination—at which point the gate for capital providers of the Blue Owl/PIMCO type will close first on newly issued SPVs, and the narrowing of the off-balance-sheet channel will push pressure back onto the four hyperscalers' on-balance-sheet books, precisely at the point when their FCF is thinnest.

### The Depreciation Dispute: Earnings Quality as a Time Fuse

Burry alleges that hyperscalers artificially stretched GPU depreciation useful lives, understating roughly $176 billion of depreciation in 2026–28, with Meta's 2028 profits allegedly overstated by roughly 21% and Oracle's by roughly 27%—CNBC explicitly stated it could not independently verify these, and this report does not treat them as fact. But the mechanism deserves to be taken seriously, because it points in the same direction as Microsoft's confirmed 15-year→25-year reclassification. If the true economic life of GPUs is 4–5 years while the accounting measure is longer, then a substantial portion of current reported profits is a product of deferred depreciation. This does not directly change cash flow, but it changes two things: the timing of the market's judgment on "whether AI makes money" gets pushed later; and once an inquiry or restatement is triggered, an upward shift in discount rates would simultaneously hit both the equity valuations of the four hyperscalers and SPV debt pricing, resonating with the renegotiation cascade—the earnings-quality fuse and the cash-flow fuse lead to the same ignition point.

To conclude this chapter: three layers of dark-side factors jointly determine that the capex ceiling in 2030 is not a question of demand but a question of durability. Along the path in which $820 billion unleased leases ramp up smoothly and 2027-year revenue delivers a "good enough" report card, the debt market can continue absorbing incremental supply, and the base-case scenario holds; conversely, the trigger for financial tightening is not any single default event, but the speed at which a cascade unfolds along the sequence described above. The financing loop has no single breaking point — it has a timetable.

## V. Breaking Down the Four Major Sub-Tracks: Structural Divergence Across Colocation, Hyperscale, Enterprise, and Edge

The previous chapter dissected the darker side of the financing structure; this chapter returns to the physical market itself: by 2030, the fates of the four major sub-tracks will no longer rise and fall together, but will structurally diverge along a single fault line—whether or not they serve AI training workloads. This report's overall judgment is: **hyperscale and colocation form a twin core of symbiotic expansion, together absorbing the vast majority of incremental global IT capacity in 2026–2030; the enterprise self-build share enters a long-cycle contraction; and the edge track gains narrative attention but has an extremely low physical scale ceiling**. Using this report's base-case scenario (global IT installed capacity of 190–210 GW in 2030) as the denominator, this report estimates that by 2030 the capacity share structure will be approximately: hyperscale (including Neocloud) 60–67%, colocation (independent third-party definition) 22–28%, enterprise self-build 8–12%, and edge under 3%. This structure is directionally consistent with Synergy's assessment—Synergy estimates that hyperscale's share of global data center capacity will rise from 48% in the fourth quarter of 2025 (1,360 facilities) to 67% in 2031.

### Hyperscale: Self-Build Expansion from 44% to 61% and the Symbiotic Structure of "Eating Into" Colocation

The hyperscale track is the absolute primary engine of this cycle. Its expansion logic was fully argued in earlier chapters: the four major operators' combined capex guidance for 2026 starts at around the $73.5–76 billion level, with global single-year capex leaping toward the $2.5–3.2 trillion range in 2030, and Dell'Oro estimating that the four major hyperscalers account for roughly half of global data center capital expenditure. In capacity terms, Synergy's quantification provides the precise structure within the track: the hyperscale self-build share is expected to rise from 44% to 61% (by 2030), while approximately 50% of hyperscale capacity sits in leased colocation facilities.

These figures reveal a mechanism that is often misread: **the rise in self-build share and the expansion of colocation leasing are not contradictory—they are two sides of the same coin**. The wholesale capacity on the balance sheets of colocation providers such as Digital Realty and Equinix has hyperscale customers as its end users—operators like Oracle, whose aggressive RPO-driven expansion places a considerable share of its capacity within leased colocation facilities [S43]. In other words, the wholesale layer of the colocation market is being "colonized" by hyperscale demand: the colocation provider supplies land, power, shell, and grid-interconnection queueing capability, while the hyperscaler fills in its own GPUs and networking equipment. This means that the traditional practice of dividing tracks by "end-customer type" will essentially fail before 2030—the economics of an Equinix wholesale cabinet are determined by Microsoft's or Meta's procurement cycles, not by local enterprise demand.

The hyperscale track's CAGR is therefore directly bound to this report's scenario fork: in the base case (35% probability), hyperscale capacity posts a CAGR of roughly 18–24% over 2026–2030; in the power-constrained scenario (30%), the physical constraints—GE Vernova gas turbines down to only about 10 GW of slots for 2029–30 and lead times of about 3 years—will first hit hyperscale self-build projects, because they are the largest single grid-interconnection applicants; the rise in self-build share would then slow, passively raising the leased colocation share. This is a counterintuitive second-order effect: **the tighter the power constraint, the more hyperscalers depend on colocation providers' existing capacity with already-locked power, and the stronger the colocation providers' bargaining position becomes**.

### Colocation: The Definition Maze and True Pricing Power Under a 1.4% Vacancy Rate

Colocation is the track with the richest evidence—and the most vulnerable to definitional contamination. Supply-side tightness is unequivocal: CBRE data shows North American colocation vacancy at just 1.4%, with Northern Virginia as low as 0.3–0.5% [S28]. In a market where vacancy approaches zero, marginal pricing power shifts entirely to the supply side.

But the rent data involves a genuine definitional dispute: CBRE reported that Northern Virginia rents "held steady" in the second half of 2025, while JLL's national measure showed +9%. After reviewing the evidence base, this report confirms that the two conclusions are directionally consistent but cover different market sets and scopes, and **neither supports the narrative circulating in the market of "Northern Virginia rents up +30–50% year over year—that claim was discarded during evidence review**. For forecasters, the lesson of this divergence is: colocation rents are not a single national price, but a dual-track structure of "existing power-locked stock (stable rents, locked long-term leases) vs. newly built power-attached capacity (significant premiums)". By 2030, with the PJM 2027/28 auction showing a reliability shortfall of 6,623 MW for the first time, and transitional arrangements under FERC's co-location order allowing co-located load to be curtailed, **"colocation capacity with confirmed power" will itself become an independently priced asset class, and the persistence of its premium may even exceed that of rents**.

The more serious definitional problem lies in "broad-definition" studies of market size: counting wholesale leases signed by hyperscalers as part of the colocation market inflates the colocation market size by roughly 3—since about 50% of hyperscale capacity is already located in leased colocation facilities, once this demand is statistically counted simultaneously as "hyperscale capex" and "colocation market revenue," it constitutes systematic double counting. This report therefore uses "independent third-party colocation provider revenue" as the definition of the colocation track. As anchors, research on the Chinese market provides a referenceable range: Kezhi Consulting estimates that China's third-party traditional IDC business revenue will grow from RMB 184.75 billion in 2025 (+16.7%) to about RMB 350 billion in 2030, landing in the middle of the CAGR forecast range between Arizton's roughly 8.94% and Goldman Sachs' roughly 20% (research dossier data, single-country definition). This report accordingly infers: in the base case, the global independent colocation track's revenue CAGR for 2026–2030 is 10–16%—significantly below hyperscale capacity growth, because colocation revenue is priced as power capacity × rent, and growth in the hyperscale self-build portion is not counted as colocation revenue. The Chinese market also provides a structural contrast: leading third-party IDCs are running at full load—GDS achieved an utilization rate of 77.5% and completed the listing of the first data center public REITs, while VNET posted RMB 9.95 billion in revenue in 2025 (+20.5%), delivered 404 MW, and secured about 500 MW in orders from ByteDance—while at the same time regional intelligent computing centers averaged only about 30% utilization. **Returns in the colocation track will be highly bimodal: third-party IDCs tied to leading cloud and AI customers will expand at full utilization, while speculative regional projects will sink into sunk costs**.

### Enterprise Self-Build: Not Shrinkage, but Being "Squeezed Out of the Fast Lane"

Enterprise data centers are the only long-cycle contraction item among the four tracks, but the contraction mechanism needs precise articulation. Direct evidence is limited; this report makes inferences based on the following three research materials, with uncertainty explicitly flagged: first, Uptime's 2025 annual survey shows the global average PUE of existing data centers at 1.54 and mainstream rack density at 10–30 kW, while newly built AI facilities reach a PUE of 1.1–1.25 and rack densities leaping toward 120 kW (GB200 NVL72-class)—**the existing enterprise fleet has fallen a full generation behind the AI-era technology baseline on both energy efficiency and density, and retrofit costs typically exceed migration costs**. Second, DRAM contract prices rising 45–50% in a single quarter and 232-clause 25% tariffs pushing up nominal server prices significantly worsened the unit cost of enterprise self-built AI capacity in 2026–2027, while hyperscalers, leveraging procurement scale and in-house ASICs (e.g., Meta's triple-sourcing from Broadcom/AMD/NVIDIA), hedged part of that inflation. Third, under the MIIT's statistical definition, carrier racks in China moved from 970,000 at the end of 2023 → 830,000 at the end of 2024 → 938,000 at the end of 2025 [S31 related research dossier], showing that even within the carrier system, traditional racks are being structurally replaced.

The counterargument that must be addressed head-on is that data sovereignty and regulatory compliance (finance, healthcare, government) will preserve a floor for enterprise self-build. This report partially accepts this thesis—**the correct forecast for the enterprise track is not a collapse to zero, but "structural freeze"**: existing facilities persist due to compliance and sunk costs, but incremental AI workloads flow almost entirely to cloud and colocation. This report estimates an enterprise self-build capacity CAGR of –2% to +2% over 2026–2030, with its share of global capacity falling from the current roughly 15–18% to 8–12% by 2030. The only upside exception is that once behind-the-meter generation technology matures, individual energy-intensive industries (oil and gas, mining) may self-build computing power to absorb curtailed electricity—but that is a tail branch of the upside scenario (5%).

### Edge: The Track with the Biggest Narrative and the Smallest Scale

Edge data centers receive the most conceptual attention, yet face the hardest scale ceiling. The core of the logic lies in the physical nature of the workloads: the bulk of this round of AI capex is training clusters, and training naturally tends toward centralization—power, liquid cooling (penetration already at 53% and now a high-end standard [S23]), CoWoS packaging supply, and network architecture all reward massive hyperscale concentrated deployment. Inference workloads could theoretically sink to the edge, but three constraints suppress their scale before 2030: rack density and cooling (mainstream existing racks of 10–30 kW cannot accommodate AI inference densities), single-site capacity economics (edge sites cannot amortize redundant power and network costs), and the substitute path of inference centralization (hyperscalers deploying inference clusters in regional hubs, trading backbone latency for economies of scale—which is precisely an extension of the distribution logic behind their 50% of capacity sitting in leased colocation facilities).

The research materials lack a reliable quantitative forecast for the edge track's scale in 2030, so this report offers only a directional judgment while explicitly flagging the information gap: **edge data centers will account for less than 3% of global IT installed capacity in 2030, and although their CAGR may reach 15–25%, the base is extremely small**. The real opportunity lies not in general-purpose inference but in two narrow niches: low-latency industrial scenarios (autonomous vehicle operations, factory visual inspection) and China's "East Data, West Computing"-style policy-driven layout—the latter essentially uses administrative directives to build concentrated capacity in western hubs rather than representing true decentralized edge.

### Scenario Sensitivity of Segment Structure and the Endgame of 2030

The sensitivity ranking of the four major segments to this report's scenario forks is clear-cut. **Colocation providers are a rare 'scenario-insensitive beneficiary'**: in the baseline scenario, volume and price both rise; in the power-constrained scenario, the premium on their locked-in stock of power capacity widens; and even in the financial tightening scenario (20%), hyperscalers cutting self-build capex would actually push more workloads toward leasing—the colocation segment's principal risk is not vanishing demand, but whether the repricing risk on unleased contracts, estimated by Moody's at $82 billion, spills over onto its own balance sheet [relevant S25 case files]. The hyperscale segment, by contrast, fully inherits all the variance of the scenario forks. Enterprise and edge remain supporting players across all scenarios.

The endgame picture for 2030 can thus be summarized in one sentence: **this is a dual-core market defined by hyperscalers, collecting rent by colocation providers, with enterprise retreating to a compliance foundation and edge filling the gaps**. The implication for investors and operators is that segment selection will, for the first time, matter more than the market's overall beta—the same 190–210 GW of global capacity lands on two entirely different asset destinies depending on whether it sits in 'colocation wholesale capacity tied to anchor tenants' or in 'speculative regional AI-compute projects'; the case of roughly 65% of announced MW in the Georgia pipeline being deemed speculative and removed has already rehearsed this divergence. The next chapter turns the lens to the single largest variable determining the overall picture: the pipeline realization rate in the U.S. market.

## VI. Deep Dive into the US Market: The Decisive Variables of the Arms Race, Grid Politics, and Pipeline Conversion Rates

The first five chapters of this report established the global framework: the baseline landing point of 190–210 GW is jointly determined by three parameters—pipeline conversion rate, power constraints, and financing structure. This chapter zooms in on the world's largest single market: the United States. The core judgment is this: **the US is the only market that simultaneously pushes all three parameters to their extremes—the capex arms race is the fiercest, grid politics detonated first, and pipeline inflation is the heaviest—which makes it both the largest contributor to the global baseline scenario (35%) and the largest source of risk for the 50% downside tail. The elasticity of the 2030 global capacity landing point to the single parameter of "US pipeline conversion rate" exceeds its elasticity to any other variable**. Under the LBNL accounting, US data-center electricity consumption in the reference case for 2030 is 649 TWh (range 521–843 TWh, or 11.8% of total US power), with the US and China together accounting for nearly 80% of the global increment—every notch of drift in the US-side conversion rate translates directly into a displacement of the global range on the order of 5–10 GW.

### Capex Detail for the Big Four: An Arms Race Sustained by Debt and Accounting Conventions

The spending side of the US market is dominated by four hyperscalers, whose 2026 guidance breaks down as follows: Amazon approximately $220 billion (about 200 billion under the February 2026 basis, revised upward during the year), with Jassy stating that capacity in 2026–2027 will be insufficient in both years; Alphabet $195–205 billion; Microsoft about $175 billion after reclassification; Meta $130–145 billion—for a combined total of approximately $735–760 billion, a year-over-year increase exceeding 60%. This figure alone exceeds the annual fixed-asset investment of most countries, but its financing implications must be read together with the cash-flow picture in Chapter Four: the combined FCF of the Big Four has fallen from $237 billion in 2024 to $200 billion in 2025, Amazon's TTM FCF is −$7.6 billion, Meta's FCF for a single quarter in the second quarter of 2026 was down to $784 million, and Barclays forecasts that Meta's FCF will turn negative in 2027–28. **The marginal fuel of the arms race has shifted from operating cash flow to debt, off-balance-sheet SPVs, and supplier credit**—Alphabet partially covers its capex with approximately $84.75 billion in equity financing plus $20.3 billion in bond issuances, making it the most explicit user of external financing among the four; under third-party aggregated accounting, hyperscaler capex for 2026 is approximately $697 billion, the same order of magnitude as the Big Four's guidance, cross-validating the robustness of total spending.

The strategic divergence among the Big Four is more informative than the aggregate. Alphabet is pursuing TPU self-sufficiency to reduce its dependence on NVIDIA, with over 80% of its fleet already liquid-cooled [S23]; Meta is executing a three-source procurement strategy (Broadcom ASICs, AMD MI450 up to 6 GW, and NVIDIA GPUs), and is industrializing its financing pipeline through Hyperion-type off-balance-sheet JV structures—by retaining only a 20% equity stake, Meta moves $27.3 billion of capital expenditure off its consolidated statements, and the model has already been replicated in projects such as El Paso [S11]; after FERC rejected the Talen nuclear co-location ISA [S17], Amazon pivoted to multiple power pathways; and Microsoft plays two roles simultaneously—a spender of roughly $175 billion of its own, and the indirect backer of OpenAI (a PBC in which it holds about a 27% stake) and its spending target of approximately $600 billion. This means that **the US arms race is not four parallel armies but a web of cross-holdings, mutual customer relationships, and mutual financing**: OpenAI is simultaneously betting on Oracle (RPO $638 billion), CoreWeave (about $22.4 billion in contracts), and AMD (6 GW plus warrants), while NVIDIA's circular pattern of injecting equity/warrants into OpenAI, xAI, and CoreWeave has already been called out in 2026 research notes from the Dallas and Chicago Feds. The intensity of the competition derives precisely from this mutual lock-in: no single player can unilaterally stand down without damaging the other nodes on its own financing loop—this is the structural reason US capex keeps getting revised upward even as FCF weakens across the board, and the same reason that any pivot would be unlikely to land gently.

### Accounting Distortion: How Microsoft's 15→25-Year Reclassification Contaminates Comparability

When interpreting this combined guidance of approximately $750 billion, one accounting treatment becomes an unavoidable definitional landmine: Microsoft adjusted its depreciation/lease terms from 15 to 25 years, which lowers its CY2026 capex from about $190 billion under the pre-restatement basis to about $175 billion, while its FY26 PP&E additions still reached $115.9 billion. This is not simply a case of "the number got smaller": **lengthening the depreciation schedule means the annual income-statement cost of the same physical investment is diluted, reported profit thickens accordingly, and the future depreciation base is pushed further out over subsequent years**. This aligns with the direction of Burry's allegations—that hyperscalers artificially extended GPU depreciation lives and under-provisioned depreciation by roughly $176 billion over 2026–28, with Meta's 2028 profit allegedly overstated by about 21% and Oracle's by about 27% (CNBC explicitly stated it could not independently verify). This report does not treat the allegations as fact, but two conclusions are firm. First, **the Big Four's capex "guidance" figures are no longer comparable numbers measured in the same units**—Amazon uses a cash basis, Microsoft has been reclassified, and Meta includes finance-lease principal; the bases must be unified before summing. Any third-party statistic that adds them directly (including the roughly 735–760 billion range itself) carries definitional noise on the order of tens of billions of dollars—a discount that must be applied before gauging the intensity of the "arms race." Second, if the true economic life of GPUs is 4–5 years while the leased portion has been stretched to 25 years, a meaningful share of current reported profit is a product of deferred depreciation—extending the judgment of "can the arms race be sustained" from a cash-flow question (analyzed in Chapter Four) to an earnings-quality question, with both fuses pointing to the same 2027 ignition point.

### Pipeline Conversion Rates: A 5–10x Basis Gap Between the 13% Base Rate and the Contracted Pipeline

If capex is the US spending engine, the pipeline conversion rate is the transmission that determines how much of that engine's horsepower reaches the wheels—it is the key parameter on which this report's baseline scenario (35%) stands or falls, and it is precisely where the US market's evidence is most tangled.

The root of the confusion is that the word "pipeline" is used to describe two entirely different classes of assets. **The first is the announced pipeline**: interconnection requests submitted to the grid by developers and speculative applicants. ERCOT's roughly 410 GW and Dominion's approximately 53.8 GW of announced figures fall into this category. Discounts applied to it must reference historical base rates: LBNL's tracking of interconnection queues from 2000–2020 shows that as of the end of 2025, only about 13% of queued capacity had actually entered service, with total queue volume exceeding 2,060 GW; the expert estimates cited by Utility Dive are more blunt—speculative interconnection applications outnumber actually built projects by a factor of 5–10. Georgia provides the most complete empirical anatomy: its large-load pipeline net-shrank by 6 GW in a single quarter to 50.9 GW, and regulatory testimony assessed that about 65% of announced MW were speculative and had been removed from the pipeline [S18]. **The second is the contracted pipeline**: projects backed by executed power contracts. AEP's signed large-load pipeline started at 56 GW and has doubled—this is the "signed" basis, whose conversion rate is logically far higher than the announced basis, but still subject to equipment supply-chain constraints: the IEA notes that about 20% of planned projects face delays because transformer lead times have doubled to three years and gas turbine delivery schedules run several years out.

The definitional gap between the two pipeline types is a factor of 5–10x, and market narratives (including some institutional extrapolations) systematically cite the announced pipeline as if it were the contracted pipeline—the single largest source of forecast error in this field, and the micro-foundation of the divergence between TrendForce's unconstrained ceiling of 490.7 GW and JLL's delivery-basis figure of 200 GW. This report's approach is therefore a tiered discount: **apply the 13–20% base-rate range to the announced pipeline, the 50–70% conversion-rate range to the contracted pipeline (less an additional haircut for equipment lead times), and the mid-to-upper band to mixed pipelines**—this choice itself carries roughly ±20 GW of US-side capacity error, which we choose to acknowledge explicitly rather than pretend to eliminate. In numbers: anchored on LBNL's US reference case of 649 TWh for 2030 and JLL's global 200 GW of deliveries, this report estimates US data-center IT installed capacity for 2030 at roughly 75–95 GW, about forty percent of the global 190–210 GW baseline; conversely, if ERCOT's and Dominion's announced pipelines were misread as deliveries, that figure would be inflated by more than twofold.

The opposing view must be confronted head-on here: is pipeline inflation really bad news? One forceful defense is that Georgia's 65% speculative share is precisely evidence that the screening mechanism works—regulators are actively squeezing out the froth, and the surviving contracted portion is of higher quality; the doubling of AEP's contracted pipeline [S18] likewise proves that real demand is expanding, not contracting. This report partially accepts that argument: **pipeline inflation is noise for forecasters but a buffer for the system itself**—it means a portion of the "power constraints" is in fact self-resolved through the natural exit of speculative projects, which is exactly the implicit mechanism identified in Chapter Three that allows the baseline scenario to hold. But the defense has a boundary, and it lies in timing: speculative exits occur at the interconnection-review stage (early in a project's life), while the physical wall of power constraints stands in the 2027–2029 energization window—GE Vernova's cumulative gas turbine contracts are about 100 GW, sold out before 2028, with only about 10 GW of slots remaining in 2029–30 and lead times of roughly 3 years. **The exit mechanism can clean up the queue, but it cannot conjure gas turbines for already-contracted projects.** The conversion-rate ceiling of the contracted pipeline is set by the equipment supply chain, not by contractual sincerity—this is the first reason this report does not assign a conversion rate above 70% to the US-side contracted pipeline.

### Grid Politics: Two Detonation Thresholds in the PJM Auctions and the Co-Location Discount

The institutional vehicle of US power constraints is highly concentrated in a single organization—PJM, whose capacity auctions have evolved from price signals into political detonation points. The timeline is clear: the 2026/27 auction hit the price cap at 329.17 dollars/MW-day for a total cost of $16.1 billion [S15]; the 2027/28 auction cleared at about 333.44 dollars/MW-day for a total of $16.4 billion, and **for the first time failed to meet reliability requirements—posting a 6,623 MW shortfall**. Utility Dive, citing PJM's own report, warns that reliability could begin to fail before 2030 if data-center load growth continues.

This report accordingly sets two detonation thresholds. **The first is a quantity threshold: the PJM 2028/29 auction shortfall breaches 5,000 MW and keeps widening.** Note that the 6,623 MW from 2027/28 already exceeds that number on its face, so the real test is not the single-year shortfall but the direction—is the shortfall converging (indicating that transmission expansion and the return of retired units are working, and the price mechanism is still self-correcting) or persistently widening (indicating that scarcity has become structural and political intervention has taken over)? If it widens, the price mechanism will give way to a political mechanism, and the probability of Scenario B (30%) moves up. **The second is an attribution threshold: the degree to which voters attribute retail electricity price increases to data centers.** PJM itself claims its auctions raise retail bills by only 1.5–5%, while IEEFA and consumer groups blame data-center load for most of the increase, and Pennsylvania customers' experience of +14.6% electricity price growth is converting into state-level political pressure—the two claims sit side by side and this report does not adjudicate between them, but the transmission path does not depend on adjudication: voter perception precedes attribution science, roughly 370 state bills are already pending in state legislatures, and once restrictive provisions move from the fringe to the mainstream, that becomes the concrete realization path for the Scenario B criterion of "≥3 states maintaining new-build moratoria beyond 2029."

The FERC co-location order (193 FERC ¶61,217) adds an institutional discount to this picture: it directs PJM to establish four transmission service options, but during the transition period co-located loads can be curtailed before network upgrades are completed. Combined with November 2024's precedent of vetoing the Talen–Amazon nuclear ISA expansion (300→480 MW), this shows that federal-level allocation instruments are already loaded. **The "curtailable" nature of co-location attributes imposes an institutional discount on the actual fulfillment rate of a substantial portion of the contracted pipeline in the PJM footprint — this is the second reason this report does not assign a fulfillment rate above 70% to the contracted pipeline** (the first being gas turbine scheduling). Conversely, the same order also opens an institutional door for behind-the-meter bypass: Oracle's fuel-cell scheme, with off-balance-sheet assets and on-site generation, bypasses grid queues and curtailment risk, so part of the 268 GW grid gap estimated by TrendForce (over 170 GW in the United States) will be converted into behind-the-meter gas and fuel-cell markets rather than real capacity loss — but the scale of this diversion is subject to the triple constraints of fuel supply, permitting, and cost, and can only absorb a small fraction of the gap.

### Conclusion: The Three Elasticity Transmissions by Which the United States Determines the Global Range

To summarize this chapter: the U.S. market determines the 2030 global landing point through three transmission chains. First, **capex elasticity** — if the $735–760 billion guidance of the Big Four bends in 2027 due to failed AI revenue validation (Scenario B trigger: U.S. single-year capex falling below $1.5 trillion), the baseline range for global $2.5–3.2 trillion would lose roughly half of its expenditure base; meanwhile, Microsoft's reclassification and the earnings-quality issues pointed to by the Burry allegations mean this validation will simultaneously test the income statement and the cash flow statement. Second, **pipeline elasticity** — for every 10 percentage-point shift in the U.S. mixed-pipeline fulfillment rate, the global capacity range shifts by approximately 7–9 GW; if Georgia-style speculative clean-up and AEP-style contracted expansion proceed in parallel, the U.S.-side fulfillment rate will land in the mid-to-high band, supporting the baseline scenario. Third, **political elasticity** — the PJM 2028/29 auction gap and state-level moratorium orders are the two most observable signals among the Scenario B trigger conditions; the next system recalibration point falls on the clearing day of that auction.

The reality of the arms race is beyond doubt — Oracle RPO $638 billion, NVIDIA FY26 revenue $215.9 billion, and the continuous upward revisions of Big Four guidance are all hard data. But the particularity of the United States lies in this: it is simultaneously the market with the most genuine demand, the heaviest padding, and the nearest constraints. From the interest structure laid out in this chapter, one can also read a deeper implication: the Big Four, equipment vendors, capital providers, and regulators are now highly coupled within the same interactive arena — the regulators (FERC, PJM) are both the imposers of constraints and the suppliers of co-location rules, and the capital providers (Blue Owl, PIMCO) are both the providers of financing and the bearers of curtailment risk — no single party can independently determine the outcome. When the next chapter turns to China, we will see an almost perfect mirror image: genuine demand, virtually no padding (policy directly prohibits speculative small-scale computing projects), but constraints of an entirely different form — not in the grid, but in chips.

## VII. Deep Dive into the Chinese Market: The Dual-Track Pattern of Falling Totals and Rising Compute, and Beijing's Decisive Hand over Chip Supply

The end of Chapter Six left us with a mirrored proposition: the United States is the market with "the most genuine demand, the most heavily inflated figures, and the nearest constraints," while China is nearly the opposite. The core judgment of this chapter is: **the Chinese market is the mirror-image case of the global constraint structure—capital and demand pose no constraint, policy actively compresses the total, and the only hard constraint sits in chip supply, whose decision-making power lies with Beijing Customs rather than Washington's licensing. This transforms the question of forecasting China's 2030 capacity landing point from a question of "pipeline conversion rate" into one of "chip output ramp-up"**. This report estimates China's 2030 data center IT installed capacity at roughly 50–65 GW (probability-weighted central estimate around 55 GW), accounting for a quarter to three-tenths of the 190–210 GW global baseline range—a contributor with restrained totals but a structure tilting toward intelligent computing, not a major source of global capex variance. This estimate is internally consistent with the report's scenario framework: the principal downside trigger on the China side is domestic chip supply, not PJM auctions or a broken financing chain.

### A Quantified Sample of the Dual-Track Pattern: "Falling Totals, Rising Compute" in a Single Financial Statement

The entry point for understanding the Chinese market is the carriers' capital expenditure tables. China Mobile's 2026 total capital expenditure was RMB 136.6 billion, down 9.5% year over year, yet its computing power network investment reached RMB 37.8 billion, up 62.4% year over year—within one and the same statement, "falling totals" and "rising compute" hold simultaneously; this is not cyclical fluctuation but policy-driven structural rebalancing. Industry evidence shows this is not an isolated case: China Unicom's 2026 capex was about RMB 50 billion, China Telecom's total capex contracted about 9.2% over the same period, while all three carriers' computing power investment expanded against the trend, forming an industry-wide dual-track structure. The policy driver is explicit: since 2025 the NDRC has banned new small-scale local computing projects and curbed excess capacity, while the East Data West Computing project (launched February 2022, establishing eight hubs and ten clusters) requires new computing capacity to concentrate at hub nodes. The overt policy goal is to squeeze out inefficient supply and concentrate resources in leading vehicles—in other words, **China is the only major market in the world where policy ex ante enforces a "pipeline haircut"**: compared with roughly 65% of announced MW on the Georgia pipeline in the U.S. Chapter Six judged speculative and ex post squeezed out by regulation to [S18], China's speculative supply is not screened out but directly prohibited, with rigidity of enforcement far exceeding that of the United States.

The corresponding structure on the private-enterprise side is Alibaba: February 2025 announced more than RMB 380 billion of AI-plus-cloud capex over three years, with management itself describing the figure as "conservative"; March 2026 further committed to annual AI-plus-cloud revenue exceeding $100 billion within five years. Tencent is joining this race as well—its 2024 fourth-quarter capex alone reached RMB 36.58 billion, a year-over-year surge of 386%. Carriers and private enterprises point in exactly the same direction—tilting toward computing power—but their intensities sit in two different magnitude classes: carriers are more tightly constrained by policy, while private-enterprise commitments carry an explicit commercial-return hurdle. This is precisely one of the core China–U.S. differences: America's big four sustain their arms race with debt-financed funding (Chapter Four), whereas in China the funding side poses virtually no constraint—no $820B of unleased-lease-style leverage accumulation, and no credit fragility from off-balance-sheet SPV debt.

The downstream beneficiary structure of the dual-track pattern is equally clear. Based on carrier procurement relationships, Inspur and Sugon, as domestic server suppliers, absorbed the counter-trend expansion of the three carriers' 2026 computing power investment, while Envicool benefited in parallel as a domestic liquid-cooling supplier—this is the profit redistribution of "falling totals, rising compute" along the supply chain: traditional IDC and general-purpose server demand shrinks, while incremental links such as compute servers and liquid cooling capture structural excess returns. Notably, this beneficiary chain is entirely domestic—the gas-turbine and transformer bottlenecks on the U.S. side pose no symmetric constraint in China; Chinese equipment makers' scarcity derives from the structural tilt of computing investment, not from a comprehensive physical supply shortage.

### Beijing's Decisive Hand over Chip Supply: The 48 Hours of H200 and the Two Sets of Ascend Numbers

The hard constraint on China's AI computing power lies not in electricity (China's power supply is relatively ample), nor in capital (carriers and Alibaba are well funded), but in training-grade AI chips. And the decision-making power over this supply has completed its transfer from Washington to Beijing.

The timeline evidence is decisive. H20 was banned from sale by the U.S. side on April 2025 (NVIDIA subsequently writing down $5.5 billion in impairment), conditionally restored on July 2025 (with a 15% revenue-share clause attached), yet from August 2025 Chinese customs effectively blocked entry. The most symbolic scene unfolded on January 2026: the U.S. side conditionally cleared NVIDIA's H200 on January 13, and Chinese firms promptly ordered more than 2 million units at about $27,000 each; **the very next day (January 14), Chinese customs restricted entry of H200 and discouraged purchases**. Within 48 hours, Washington's licensing decision was nullified by Beijing's customs stamp. The political-economy conclusion this report draws is this: whether U.S. controls loosen or tighten, the actual supply of AI chips to China is determined by the enforcement discretion of Beijing customs—U.S. policy retains only second-hand effects (setting the price of the compliant installed base and the smuggled gray market). Jensen Huang publicly called export controls on China a "failure" on May 2025 and praised the Trump administration for abolishing some restrictions, but the H200 episode proves: even if Washington grants full clearance, Beijing's industrial-autonomy policy will still shut the door. The result of dual-direction control is that controlled inventory and training-grade high-end cards enter a seller's market, and all domestic-substitution demand flows to Huawei Ascend.

From this, the question of China's training-grade compute ceiling concentrates on two divergent sets of numbers. Huawei plans to produce about 600,000 Ascend 910C chips in 2026, roughly double its 2025 output (about 300,000 units); Ascend-family wafer capacity rises from about 1 million in 2025 to 1.6 million in 2026, implying a theoretical finished-unit ceiling of about 800,000 at a 2:1 dual-die packaging ratio—this is the capacity-plan caliber. SemiAnalysis, by contrast, estimates that HBM supply will compress effective 2026 Ascend output to about 250,000–300,000—this is the available-HBM caliber. The divergence's core lies in the definitional gap between wafer capacity and available HBM plus yield losses, and the bottleneck's physical location can be pinpointed: Huawei depends directly on ChangXin Memory (CXMT)—CXMT's HBM capacity ramp and yields determine the HBM quota ceiling for Ascend; the manufacturing bottleneck is SMIC's advanced-node foundry capacity. In other words, the ceiling on China's training-grade compute is not Huawei's ambition but CXMT's yield curve and SMIC's process capacity. This report's treatment: **treat 600,000 as the capacity ceiling, 250,000–300,000 as the pessimistic effective output under HBM constraint, and set the monitoring threshold at 400,000**—if 2026 actual output falls below 400,000, China's intelligent-compute gap becomes explicit, and this report will cut its China-side capacity contribution by 5–10 GW; if fully delivered and the ramp continues into 2027, it becomes an amplifier on the China side of the upside D scenario (5%). This transmission also reaches the power side: if 910C is hard-constrained by HBM, China's estimated 2030 data center electricity increment of +175 TWh (+170%) would be haircut by 30–50%—the chip constraint transmitting upstream into electricity demand is the distinctive risk structure of the "rising compute" path.

The opposing view must be stated head-on. One forceful defense holds that the HBM bottleneck is a temporary engineering problem, not a structural ceiling—CXMT capacity is ramping, and Cambricon's revenue has surged roughly 453% year over year on domestic-substitution orders, showing that the substitution ecosystem's supply elasticity far exceeds what Huawei's standalone capacity table implies; NVIDIA's China share has slid from about 95% before the controls to about 50%, corresponding to annual revenue loss of about $15 billion, and this loss is being divided among multiple domestic vendors. This report partially accepts this argument—it is precisely the China-side basis for retaining rather than zeroing the upside probability in the D scenario—but the defense's boundary lies in time: training-grade chip yields and HBM capacity ramp in units of years, while China Mobile's +62.4% computing investment and Alibaba's three-year 380 billion plan are windowed on 2026–2028. The timing mismatch means that even if domestic substitution ultimately succeeds, intelligent-compute supply in 2027–2028 remains subject to the currently locked-in capacity table—capex can be revised upward; CXMT's yield cannot.

### Bimodal Utilization and the Caliber Gap: Nominal Compute Is Not Effective Compute

The third structural feature of the Chinese market is extreme bimodality of utilization. On one side, low utilization at regional intelligent-computing centers: average utilization only about 30%, with in-rack utilization of western thousand-GPU clusters below 30% [S31]. On the other side, top third-party IDCs running at full load: GDS's utilization rate reached 77.5% and it completed the listing of the first batch of data center public REITs, while VNET posted 2025 revenue of RMB 9.95 billion (+20.5%), delivered 404 MW, and secured an order of about 500 MW from ByteDance.

Beneath the bimodality lies a further layer of statistical fog. Under the MIIT's "Statistical Bulletin on the Communications Industry" caliber, carrier racks stood at 938,000 at the end of 2025, having swung through 970,000 at the end of 2023 → 830,000 at the end of 2024 (caliber narrowed) → 938,000 at the end of 2025, and forming a series break against the National Data Administration's nationwide figure of 13.73 million racks; official computing power figures (280 → 962 EFLOPS under the MIIT system) measure the nominal supply of all shipped equipment, while the 2025 year-end intelligent-computing scale of 1.59 million PFLOPS (FP16 caliber) reported by Sina Tech cannot be mixed with the EFLOPS figures due to precision conversion and statistical-scope differences. **The gap between nominal and effective compute is precisely the China-side mirror of the global caliber problem described in Chapter One**—and China adds a layer of policy response: the NDRC's ban on new small-compute projects and the MIIT's push to resell and reactivate idle compute together constitute official acknowledgment of, and a hedge against, the utilization problem. The policy direction is not to expand the denominator but to make the numerator real—which means capacity forecasts for China must be anchored to effective compute rather than nominal capacity; this report's 50–65 GW estimate already embeds this haircut.

The allocation implications of this structure are clear: scarce compute supply flows to the vehicles with the highest bids and utilization. The top third-party IDCs' model of "high utilization on existing stock plus binding to head clients" (GDS at 77.5%, VNET bound to ByteDance for 500 MW) is systematically superior to newly built regional intelligent-computing projects. If the HBM constraint materializes (effective Ascend output falling below the 400,000 threshold), the bimodality will widen further—top IDC utilization trending full with firm rents, while regional projects continue to sleep as sunk costs. It should be noted that ByteDance's own capex figure (about $20 billion in 2025) comes from anonymous sources and is denied by the company; per this report's evidence-grading discipline, it is permanently excluded from any baseline scenario, and VNET's order is its only verifiable indirect trace in the sample.

### Three Transmission Elasticities for the Global Framework

To summarize this chapter, China embeds itself into this report's global scenario framework through three chains. First, **capacity elasticity**: taking Rystad Energy's figure of approximately 60 GW of China 2030 (32 GW@2025→40 GW@2026→60 GW@2030, corresponding to 289 TWh of electricity consumption) as a reference — a figure that forms a weak cross-validation against the approximately 275 TWh for China implied by the IEA baseline scenario in 2030, though it comes from a single institution's methodology and confidence is limited — combined with the policy rigidity of operators' 'aggregate reduction,' this report estimates China's IT installed capacity in 2030 at 50–65 GW. The revenue-side anchor is Kezhi Consulting's figures for China's third-party traditional IDC business: RMB 184.75 billion in 2025 (+16.7%) to approximately RMB 350 billion in 2030, landing in the middle of the divergence range between Arizton's roughly 8.94% and Goldman Sachs' roughly 20% CAGR forecasts — a divergence that itself partly hinges on whether the 600,000 Ascend units of capacity are treated as a hard ceiling on compute supply, meaning the variance in China market revenue forecasts is already pricing in the chip constraint. Second, **chip elasticity**: Huawei Ascend production is the most independent of the five monitored indicators — because supply is determined by Beijing Customs [S14], its readings are unaffected by the oscillation of Washington's licensing or American electricity politics, making it the cleanest China-side leading variable. Third, **decoupling elasticity**: the main trigger mechanisms of Scenario B (power-constrained) and Scenario C (financial tightening) — the PJM auction gap and the renegotiation of unleased leases — do not directly act on China; the channel through which the China side is affected by global downside scenarios is mainly the export-oriented liquid cooling and server supply chains (the overseas exposure of Envicool, Inspur, and Sugon), rather than domestic compute investment itself. The policy-driven capital rebalancing is far less sensitive to external financing cycles than the dependence of the four major US hyperscalers on debt markets.

The implications for the 2027 validation window are therefore bidirectional: if AI revenue validation on the US side fails and triggers a global capex bend, the China market will become the relatively most stable piece of the global picture — demand has a policy floor, supply has domestic substitution, and financing has no leverage vulnerability; conversely, if the global baseline scenario materializes while Ascend production falls below 400,000 units, China's gap will become independently visible. The endgame shape of the China market can be summarized in one sentence: **restrained in aggregate, aggressive in structure, autonomous in constraints — its contribution to the global 2030 capacity landing point is a certain floor, and its source of variance lies not in power, not in capital, but in the stamp of Beijing Customs and the yield curve of CXMT**. Chapters Nine and Ten, when developing the downside and tail scenarios, will treat the branch of insufficient Ascend production as the concrete path through which the China-side gap becomes visible; the monitoring dashboard in Chapter Twelve has already set 2026 actual production as a sentinel indicator — it is the earliest readable, least US-noise-contaminated China-side signal.

**VIII. Scenario-by-Scenario Forecast (I): Baseline Expansion (Probability 35%) — A Steady Realization Path After Pipeline Haircuts**

This chapter converges, for the first time, the chain of evidence accumulated over the preceding seven chapters onto a concrete landing point: what the world looks like in 2030 if things develop "normally." The core judgment can be stated in one sentence: **Scenario A receives the highest probability in this report (estimated here at 35%), not because it is the most optimistic, but because it is the only scenario in which the two evidence chains — demand authenticity and supply constraints — point in opposite directions with comparable magnitudes: the hardest anchor on the demand side and the hardest anchor on the constraint side offset each other, pressing the most likely landing zone to the upper-middle range of 190–210 GW and $2.5–3.2 trillion**. The following sections successively argue Scenario A's three mechanisms of validity, its four-stage realization path, its probability defense, and its falsification signals.

### Mechanism One: Pipeline realization lands in the upper-middle band — the correct use of the 13% base rate

The first condition for Scenario A is that pipeline realization falls in the 30–40% upper-middle band, which first requires clarifying a commonly confused question: what is the relationship between this figure and LBNL's 13% historical base rate? The answer is that they measure different assets. 13% applies to the renewable-project-dominated interconnection queues of 2000–2020 (with a cumulative total exceeding 2,060 GW), whose applicants were mostly speculative developers without power purchase agreement backing; whereas the core of this round's data center pipeline is contracted capacity underwritten by hyperscaler credit — AEP's contracted large-load pipeline doubling to more than 56 GW is a direct reading of this layer. Georgia's regulatory anatomy provides the relative thickness of the two layers: roughly 65% of announced MW was rated speculative and removed, while the remaining 35% contracted portion logically realizes at a far higher rate than the historical base rate. Scenario A's parameter combination is therefore: 13–20% for announced pipelines, 50–70% realization for contracted pipelines (after further deducting equipment lead-time attrition), with the blended total landing at 30–40%.

This upper-middle-band realization has an often-underestimated implicit mechanism: **power constraints and pipeline inflation act as mutual antidotes within Scenario A**. TrendForce's estimate of a 2030 grid gap of 268 GW (over 170 GW in the United States) is calculated on the assumption of full queue realization — but those 65% speculative Georgia MW were never going to be built in the first place; their natural exit precisely frees up equipment slots and grid interconnection windows for contracted projects. In other words, Scenario A does not require anyone to "solve" the power constraint; it only requires the screening mechanism to operate normally: GE Vernova's gas turbine production schedule — sold out before 2028, with only about 10 GW of slots remaining for 2029–30 — gets allocated first to the highest contract-quality projects, while the speculative portion evaporates silently. What Chapter One called "a portion of the power constraint being self-digested by pipeline inflation" is precisely this process. JPMorgan's third-party compilation of hyperscaler capex of about $697 billion for 2026 is of the same order of magnitude as the four largest players' guidance, cross-validating the robustness of committed spending — Scenario A's increment is built precisely on such hard commitments, not on announced pipelines.

### Mechanism Two: Power delays are absorbed by three layers of cushioning

The second condition for Scenario A is that power delays do not breach the 190 GW lower bound, and there are three absorption layers of decreasing strength.

The first layer is behind-the-meter generation diversion. TrendForce's 268 GW gap explicitly excludes behind-the-meter generation [S7], which means part of the gap will convert into a behind-the-meter gas and fuel cell market rather than a true capacity loss. Oracle's fuel cell solution has demonstrated a viable path: taking assets off balance sheet and generating power on site to bypass the grid queue, with its $638 billion RPO-driven expansion pace partially relying on this physical workaround. FERC's co-location order has opened the institutional door for this path—the December 18, 2025 193 FERC ¶61,217 directs PJM to establish four transmission service options, bringing co-location arrangements from case-by-case negotiation (November 2024 the precedent of the Talen–Amazon nuclear ISA being rejected) into codified rules. Its limits must be honestly noted: behind-the-meter diversion is subject to the triple constraints of fuel supply, permitting, and cost, and can only absorb a small portion of the gap—it is a moderating variable, not a cure.

The second layer is the temporal conversion of constraint forms. Chapter Three already argued: the physical bottlenecks of 2026–2028 (gas turbine slots, transformer lead times doubling in three years) gradually give way after 2028 to regulation-driven allocation constraints. What Scenario A requires is that this transition occur smoothly — the PJM 2028/29 auction shortfall does not persistently breach the 5,000 MW political threshold (2027/28's 6,623 MW already exceeded it numerically; the real test lies in direction: is the shortfall converging or structurally widening), and the restrictive provisions among the roughly 370 state bills remain on the margins. EirGrid's de facto freeze on Dublin since January 2022 provides a cautionary counterexample — a top-tier market can survive more than six years under an administrative freeze — and Scenario A requires the United States to take PJM's price-based path rather than EirGrid's freeze path; the co-location rules are precisely that watershed.

The third layer is the most hidden: demand-side self-discipline. OpenAI's downward revision of its pre-2030 spending target to about $600 billion (single source) and the shelving of the NVIDIA–OpenAI hundred-billion LOI are, in Scenario A's narrative, not precursors of collapse but the system's pressure-release valve — the most aggressive buyer proactively pruning the softest commitments precisely protects the credibility of the contracted portion. This links to Chapter Two's "differentiated pricing" conclusion: the orderly failure of soft commitments spares hard commitments from collective revaluation.

### Mechanism Three: 2027 revenue validation delivers a "good enough rather than perfect" report card

This is the least measurable of the three mechanisms, and the core reason Scenario A's probability was not set higher. The 2025 gap of AI revenue of about $60 billion against capex of about $400 billion needs to converge significantly by 2027 but need not close — Bain's estimate of $2 trillion in revenue is the magnitude that supports post-2030 expansion, whereas Scenario A only requires that the 2027 reading suffice for debt markets to keep absorbing the increment.

Evidence supporting a "good enough" outcome already exists. First, the monetization curve at the top is steep: Anthropic's ARR rose from 9 billion to above $44 billion, with gross margin improving from 38% to above 70% — if this curve diffuses to the second tier by 2027, the slope problem of the revenue gap is answered. Second, cloud revenue recognition is independent of model labs' monetization: global cloud infrastructure services revenue recorded by Synergy has reached an annualized run rate of about $476 billion with nine consecutive quarters of acceleration, and Alphabet's cloud backlog of $514 billion — cloud contracts are signed revenue that does not depend on end users' ultimate willingness to pay for AI applications. Third, the GPU rental market provides a marginal signal of demand tightness: on SemiAnalysis's measure, the H100 one-year contract price rebounded from 1.70 dollars/GPU-hour in October 2025 to 2.35 dollars in March 2026 (+40%) — the earlier crash from late 2024 to mid-2025 (global average prices at one point fell to 2.85–3.50 dollars/GPU-hour) had been read as evidence of oversupply, and the rebound indicates that inference demand growth is once again consuming existing compute capacity. **The V-shaped reversal in compute rental prices is Scenario A's most sensitive thermometer: it translates "revenue validation" from an income-statement problem into a spot-price problem** — if built capacity is being rented, the financing loop's $820 billion of unleased leases has a cash-flow source for commencing leases.

The threshold for "good enough" can therefore be made concrete: AI-related revenue in 2027 (model-lab ARR + cloud AI services + API) reaching the $150–300 billion magnitude — two to five times the 60 billion of 2025, yet without touching Bain's 2 trillion line. Under this reading, uncommenced leases come online steadily, Oracle RPO is consumed moderately or grows slightly, and the debt market's gate for incremental SPVs stays half open. In Scenario A, revenue validation is not passing the exam, but scraping a pass and being granted a makeup test.

### A four-stage unfolding of the implementation path

Placing Scenario A on a timeline, the period 2026–2030 splits into four stages, each with a different dominant variable.

**2026 H2 through mid-2027: passing the validation window.** The three leading indicators on dashboard twelve read as follows: on-time energized deliveries of no less than eighty percent of the pipeline figure; RPO showing no single-quarter retrenchment on the order of hundreds of billions from the 638 billion base; and US single-year capex continuing to climb from the four majors' 735–760 billion in 2026. The key qualitative shift in this stage is that the upward-revision cycle (Dell'Oro raising its 2030 capex forecast from 1.2 trillion to over 3 trillion — nearly doubling it — within seven months, and Gartner revising upward three times in nine months) is confirmed for the first time by revenue-side data rather than being merely self-reinforced by the spending side — the cycle switches from a reflexivity-driven to a fundamentals-confirmation-driven regime.

**End of 2027 through 2028: deceleration against the physical wall.** Sold-out gas turbines and transformer lead times suppress deliveries, and the global growth rate falls from +31% in 2026 to 15–20% — but the absolute increment is still climbing, because the pipeline signed in 2024–2025 comes online in a concentrated fashion precisely within this window. Alphabet's self-description as "supply constrained" and Jassy's statement that capacity was insufficient in both 2026 and 2027 prove, in Scenario A, to be honest descriptions: complaints about insufficient supply imply full utilization of built-out capacity, which is the strongest endogenous justification for continued investment.

**2028–2029: the constraint regime transition.** GE Vernova's roughly 10 GW of remaining slots in 2029–30 are fulfilled on a priority basis, CoWoS advanced-packaging capacity ramps from roughly 75–80k wpm to 120–140k wpm [S12 related dossier], lifting the accelerator supply ceiling, while PJM's price mechanism and colocation options govern power allocation. This stage is the watershed between A and B: if electricity-price attribution politics detonates in 2028 and moratoria form in three or more states, the path slides toward B; A requires the price-based path to win the political race.

**2029–2030: completion of the capex step-change.** The single-year capex leap toward 2.5–3.2 trillion occurs mainly here: the four majors, accounting for roughly half of global spending, complete the crossing, and Dell'Oro's over-3 trillion figure most likely materializes in the first half of the A range. The discount by which this report caps the ceiling at 3.2 trillion comes from financing capacity — the commencement pace of 820 billion uncommenced leases determines the debt market's willingness to absorb incremental supply; Scenario A requires it to be steady, but not booming.

### A defense of the probability: why 35%, and objections from both directions must be answered

**Bulls will ask: why not higher?** Over the past two years every bearish signal has been overridden by upward spending revisions, and the cautious have been consistently wrong. The response has three layers. First, A's probability ceiling is structurally capped by B: the strength of the physical-constraint evidence (turbine scheduling slots, PJM's first 6,623 MW reliability shortfall, transformer lead times ) is sufficient for "power delays overwhelming 190 GW" to independently merit 30% probability — this is production-schedule arithmetic, not a sentiment call. Second, C's 20% likewise carves share away from A: SPV off-balance-sheet debt exceeding $120 billion over two years, plus the vendor-financing loop (both the Dallas Fed's and the Chicago Fed's 2026 research notes flag the circular pattern of NVIDIA injecting equity/warrants into OpenAI, xAI, and CoreWeave ) means the transmission of a revenue-verification failure is cascading, not gradual. Third, A's determination requires joint satisfaction of a dual band — capacity meeting the bar while capex overshoots (above 3.2 trillion) or undershoots (below 2.5 trillion) both slide into E's "within the band but auxiliary conditions unmet." With these three discounts stacked, 35% is the honest result after they have all been deducted.

**Bears will ask: why this high?** A market built on debt-financed funding, inflated pipelines, and unverified revenue — why should the middle path be the most frequent outcome? The response again has three layers. First, the hardest evidence chain on the demand side shows no sign of cracking: RPO of 638 billion including 75 billion in customer prepayments, NVIDIA FY26 revenue of 215.9 billion, and the big four's guidance being revised up continuously through the year — the bears have yet to produce any hard counter-data of comparable magnitude, only the chronological faith that "something will break eventually." Second, B and C exhibit the negative-feedback mutual cancellation argued in Chapter One: power delays postponing spending actually ease the cash squeeze, financing contraction reducing new projects actually eases the power queue — a "joint collapse" cannot occupy the whole of B+C's 50%, and the probability mass squeezed out by mutual cancellation flows back to A and E. Third, and most importantly: **Scenario A's mechanism does not require any party to "win" — it only requires every party to "not lose completely"** — pipelines discounted but not zeroed out, power delayed but backfilled, revenue failing the grade but getting a makeup exam, debt strained but not cut off. In a system where multiple independent forces check one another, the intermediate state with no single dominant force is the statistical default resting point. This is precisely the methodological reason this report sets A, rather than any extreme scenario, as the mode: rather than betting on some fault line erupting, bet that all fault lines are absorbed halfway by their respective buffers.

### Falsification Signals and Boundary Conditions

Scenario A's falsification conditions are written into the determination criteria; here we give the operationalized sequence (linking to Chapter Twelve). First priority: Oracle RPO falling more than $100 billion in a single quarter (currently 638 billion ) — a contraction in the order book of the thinnest node on the loop (its largest-customer contract is WSJ single-sourced and never confirmed by either party) would simultaneously strike at both the borrowing foundation and market confidence, the fastest single falsification path for A. Second priority: U.S. single-year capex in 2027 falling below $1.5 trillion — a confirmation signal for C, and simultaneously a declaration that A's capex band is unreachable. Third priority: on-time energization rates in 2026Q4–2027Q2 falling below the pipeline-basis 20%, requiring attribution by delay agent into B (equipment scheduling) or C (erosion of capital discipline — any softening of Amy Hood's "plans unchanged" framing is an early sign of the latter). Boundary conditions matter equally: if 2030 capacity lands at 190–210 GW but capex exceeds 3.2 trillion, the landing belongs to E rather than A — overspend itself means the financing-discount assumption has failed, which is a separate outcome requiring independent explanation; conversely, if capacity hugs the lower edge of 190 GW while capex meets the bar, it is within A's band, but already one step from B's gravitational boundary.

To sum up this chapter: Scenario A is the pricing of the default state in which "the system functions normally under multiple constraints." Each of its conditions for holding has an independent falsification signal, and every parameter comes from empirical base rates rather than optimistic extrapolation — the 13% queue base rate means it does not trust announcements, 820 billion in unleased leases means it leaves a discount on the capex ceiling, and the turbine production schedule means it holds reverence for the 2028 wall. The way it earns the highest probability is humble: it bets on no miracles, only that the buffers are slightly thicker than the fault lines. The next chapter turns to the 50% downside tail in aggregate — those two scenarios share the same set of facts as A; the disagreement lies only in which of the three mechanisms fails first, and in the probability that the failure outruns the buffers' absorptive capacity.

## Nine. Scenario-by-Scenario Forecast (Part Two): Power Constraints and Financial Contraction — The Downside Tail Structure Totaling 50%

Chapter Eight argued why the baseline scenario is the mode; this chapter argues why the other half of the probability mass — the downside tail totaling 50% — is not a pile of "collapse narratives" but rather two pathways, each with its own independent trigger mechanism and independent chain of evidence, and with negative feedback between them. Core judgment first: **Scenario B, power constraints (estimated at 30% probability in this report), and Scenario C, financial contraction (20% probability), together account for half the probability mass, but they are not two sides of the same story: B is the compression of the supply side by physical and regulatory constraints, with demand not disappearing; C is the liquidation of the financing side triggered by failed revenue validation, under which power actually loosens. The mutually offsetting negative feedback between the two scenarios is the most important structural reason this report declines to assign an independent probability to a "joint collapse" and retains Scenario E at 10%**. The following sections proceed in turn through B's trigger mechanism and realization path, C's cascade sequence and determination thresholds, and a formalized analysis of their mutual exclusivity.

### Scenario B (30%): Power Constraints — When Production Schedules and Auction Calendars Defeat Capital Expenditure

The determination criteria for Scenario B must first restate their strictness: global IT installed capacity at the end of 2030 falls within 150–185 GW, and this shortfall must be attributable to observable power delays — the PJM 2028/29 or 2029/30 auction gap exceeding 5,000 MW, or more than three states maintaining new-construction moratoriums beyond 2029. **The determination criteria exclude "capacity shortfall caused by weakening demand"**: if capacity in 2030 is below 190 GW but the cause is tenant cancellations rather than power delays, the outcome is classified as C or E, not B. This attribution discipline is the precondition for B receiving 30% rather than a lower probability: it prices the physical evidence on the constraint side, not sentiment.

B's chain of evidence was established in Chapter Three; this chapter distills it into the specific way the trigger mechanisms stack. The first layer is equipment production schedules: GE Vernova's heavy-duty gas turbine contracted backlog totals roughly 100 GW (44 GW in firm orders, 56 GW in slot reservations), with only about 10 GW of slot capacity unbooked for 2029–30 and delivery times of roughly three years. This means that as of September 2026 today, any project slated to come online after 2029 that has not yet locked in a turbine slot is already at the back of the queue for generation equipment supply. The second layer is the quantified grid-side gap: the PJM 2027/28 auction cleared at roughly 333.44 dollars/MW-day for a total of $16.4 billion, and for the first time failed to meet reliability requirements — a shortfall of 6,623 MW; Utility Dive, citing PJM's own report, warned that if load growth persists, reliability could begin to fail before 2030 and reach critical levels by 2027. The arithmetic implication of the two layers combined is: **even if every signed project is fully funded and every chip arrives on time, the 2028–2030 energization window cannot accommodate the entire signed pipeline**, and the overflow either gets pushed past 2030 (this report's classification as B) or diverts to behind-the-meter generation bypass routes (partially flowing back to A).

**The market configuration under Scenario B requires concrete depiction, not mere GW figures.** First, the capacity structure tilts toward "existing stock with locked-in power": North American colocation vacancy is already as low as 1.4%, with Northern Virginia at 0.3–0.5% [S28]; tightening power constraints will further widen the premium on colocation capacity with confirmed power, and hyperscalers will cut self-builds in favor of leasing already-energized stock (the colocation providers' "scenario-insensitive net beneficiary" logic argued in Chapter Five reaches its strongest form under B). Second, bypass routes become the primary growth pole: Oracle bypassing the grid queue with fuel-cell behind-the-meter generation, and Crusoe using on-site gas generation in Abilene — these routes shift from fringe options to the mainline in Scenario B, but their scale is constrained by the triple limits of fuel supply, permitting, and cost; moreover, the Crusoe-style bypass itself depends on GE Vernova turbine capacity, and with only about 10 GW [S19] remaining in turbine slots for 2029–30, bypassers and grid-interconnected projects compete for slots on the same production schedule. Third, of the 268 GW grid gap TrendForce estimates for 2030 (over 170 GW in the United States), a larger proportion will convert into undelivered capacity rather than the behind-the-meter market — this is precisely the origin of the 5 GW gap between the upper bound of the 150–185 GW range (185 GW) and the lower edge of the A range (190 GW).

**The political pathway is B's second trigger leg, and it is harder to predict than the physical pathway.** The spread of state-level moratoriums requires no new physical evidence — only the politicization of the electricity-price attribution dispute: PJM claims the auction raised retail bills by only 1.5–5%, while IEEFA and consumer groups attribute the increases primarily to data center load; Pennsylvania customers' experience of +14.6% electricity prices is converting into state-level political pressure. The two accounts stand side by side and this report does not adjudicate between them, but transmission does not depend on adjudication. Roughly 370 state bills are pending in legislatures across the states; once restrictive provisions move from the fringe to the mainstream, the B determination condition — "≥3 states maintaining moratoriums beyond 2029" — is met. The de facto freeze EirGrid has imposed on Dublin since January 2022 provides the endgame template: a top-tier market can survive more than six years under an administrative freeze [see S16 for related background]. **Scenario B's America is the parallel version in which PJM-style pricingization loses the political race to an EirGrid-style freeze.**

The strongest rebuttal to B must be stated head-on: why only 30% and not higher? The rebuttal has three layers. First, pipeline padding is a built-in shock absorber for power constraints: about 65% of Georgia's announced MW were judged speculative and removed [S18]; the natural exit of speculative projects frees up slots and interconnection windows for signed projects — power constraints kill first the projects that were never going to be built anyway. Second, the FERC co-location order (193 FERC ¶61,217) provides a codified institutional channel for behind-the-meter and co-location arrangements [S16], channeling constraints toward pricing rather than freezes. Third, historically, every warning that "power will become the bottleneck" has ultimately been diluted by grid response and demand-side rebalancing. This report's response: these three buffers absorb marginal pressure, whereas B's trigger requires only the directional fact that the gap keeps widening — the 6,623 MW gap in the 2027/28 auction already numerically exceeds the 5,000 MW threshold; if the 2028/29 auction continues to widen the gap and the political response takes shape, B is confirmed. 30% prices the proposition that "the shock absorbers and the fault lines are evenly matched": any lower would understate the arithmetic of the production schedules, and any higher would understate the system's track record of self-repair.

### Scenario C (20%): Financial Contraction — The Renegotiation Moment for $820 billion in Not-Yet-Commenced Leases

C's determination criteria are equally strict: by the end of 2027 or earlier, U.S. single-year data center capex falls below $1.5 trillion, AND the single-quarter renegotiation rate of not-yet-commenced leases exceeds 10%, OR two or more hyperscalers cut multi-year capex guidance by more than 25%. The three conditions form an "AND" logic, which is why C's 20% is lower than B's 30%: **C is not the gradual narrative of "slowing capex growth" but a sharp-turn event in which failed revenue validation detonates a cascade of contract renegotiations**, and a sharp turn requires the trigger to be detonated within a specific time window.

The starting point of the cascade is the revenue gap argued in Chapter Four: 2025 AI revenue of roughly $60 billion corresponds to roughly $400 billion of capex, while Bain's estimated support line is about $2 trillion in annual revenue. Scenario C assumes the 2027 reading comes in far below that, after which pressure unfolds in a set order. First in line are the $820 billion not-yet-commenced leases (Moody's estimates the not-yet-commenced portion of $1.2 trillion in lease commitments): this portion of commitments has not yet generated cash flow, so the accounting and credit costs of renegotiation are lowest. OpenAI's downward revision of its pre-2030 spending target from roughly $1.4 trillion to about $600 billion (single source) has already demonstrated how this repricing happens silently, and the "shelving" of the NVIDIA–OpenAI hundred-billion LOI (reported by WSJ, contested) is a verified specimen of soft-commitment failure. Second in line is RPO: Oracle borrows against $638 billion of RPO to fund expansion (FY26 borrowing on the order of $4.3 billion, FCF −$23.7 billion), and its largest single-customer contract is single-sourced to the WSJ with neither party ever publicly confirming it — meaning that once the contract book at the thinnest-leveraged node on the loop shrinks, both the borrowing base and market confidence are hit simultaneously. The falsification threshold this report sets — RPO declining more than $100 billion in a single quarter — is not a risk but a confirmation signal in Scenario C. Third in line, only then, is SPV debt: off-balance-sheet debt has accumulated beyond $120 billion over two years, and the A+ rating structure of Hyperion-type JVs depends on continuous validation of lease commencement rates; a renegotiation rate breaking 10% means the SPV cash flow model fails.

**The cascade's amplification node sits at the intersection of the Neocloud and vendor-financing loops.** CoreWeave's roughly $104 billion order backlog (August 2026 measure) underpins its debt stack, of which $8.5 billion of DDTL has already received investment-grade ratings — yet Microsoft alone once accounted for 62% of its 2024 revenue, and the divergence between the "investment-grade label" and "single-customer concentration" is a mismatch the credit market has not yet repriced. The vendor-financing loop compounds the amplification further: 2026 research notes from both the Dallas Fed and the Chicago Fed named the circular pattern of NVIDIA injecting equity/warrants into OpenAI, xAI, and CoreWeave, with those customers then using the funds to purchase NVIDIA systems. When every node on the loop expands simultaneously, each link looks healthy; when revenue validation fails, the "demand" backstopped by vendor injections is cut first, and the SPV debt and lease commitments endorsed by that demand simultaneously lose their cash flow sources — **the structure in which revenue, demand, and capital expenditure mutually endorse one another around the loop becomes, on the way down, a structure of mutual drag.**

The market configuration under Scenario C: U.S. single-year capex, from the trillion-scale of $735–760 billion across the four major players plus Neocloud and enterprise self-build in 2026, bends to below $1.5 trillion in 2027; capital providers of the Blue Owl/PIMCO type close the gate on new SPV issuance first, and the narrowing of off-balance-sheet channels pushes pressure back onto the four majors' balance sheets — precisely at the moment of their thinnest FCF (Amazon TTM −$7.6 billion, Meta single quarter $784 million, Barclays forecasting Meta turning negative in 2027–28); the capacity-side outcome is the 120–150 GW range — note this is below B's 150–185 GW; C is the deeper downside because C destroys both demand and financing simultaneously, whereas B merely delays delivery.

The strongest rebuttal to C likewise requires head-on treatment: why 20% and not higher? The bears would say that a market built on debt-financed funding and unvalidated revenue ought to carry a higher probability of liquidation, and that in 2026 several central banks and international institutions had already flagged the risk. This report's response has three layers. First, C requires a "sharp turn," not a "deceleration": the determination criteria demand that capex falling below 1.5 trillion and a renegotiation rate above 10% occur simultaneously, while historical base rates show that stress in financing loops typically releases gradually — leases are extended before being renegotiated, guidance slows before being cut — and most deceleration paths fall into E's mixed zone rather than C's full determination. Second, the "good enough" outcome for revenue validation has real evidentiary support: Anthropic's ARR rose from 9 billion to above $44 billion with gross margins above 70%; cloud contracts are signed revenue (Alphabet's cloud backlog $514 billion) that does not depend on the ultimate willingness to pay for AI applications. Third, mutual lock-in is itself a stabilizer: the four majors, OpenAI, NVIDIA, and the capital providers are highly coupled within the same interactive arena, and no single party can unilaterally pull back without damaging the other nodes on its own loop — which delays the fall of the first domino. But by the same token, that coupling means that once the fall begins, a soft landing is difficult — which is precisely why C's range (120–150 GW) is deeper than B's.

### Negative-Feedback Mutual Offset: Why B+C Does Not Equal a Joint Collapse of 50%

The most important analytical contribution of this chapter is to clarify the negative feedback mechanism between B and C, because it directly determines the probability structure. **First direction of negative feedback: power constraints delay financial tightening.** Physical deferral pushes the actual payment timing of capital expenditure further out; insufficient gas turbine slot availability means part of the 2028–2029 capex is pushed beyond 2030, flattening the FCF pressure curves of the four hyperscalers; meanwhile, power scarcity enhances the economics of already-built capacity — the vacancy rate of North American 1.4%, [S28], trends toward zero in Scenario B, and the resilience of GPU and colocation rents provides an unexpected revenue-side prop for "built capacity that someone pays for," which precisely eases the pressure of revenue validation. In other words, **B pushes back C's ignition time.**

**Second direction: financial tightening relieves power stress.** Financing contraction curbs new project applications; interconnection queues shorten, auction pressure eases, and the electricity price attribution dispute loses fuel — the political momentum behind the widening PJM gap is undercut at its foundation. A market with a bent capex curve no longer has a narrative of load growth crushing reliability, so B's political trigger leg automatically becomes inert once C occurs.

The combined conclusion of both directions is that B and C compete on the timeline: whichever occurs first compresses the probability of the other. This explains three things. First, why B+C totaling 50% does not require a standalone "joint downside" scenario: joint downside would require both fault lines to detonate synchronously within a short window too brief for negative feedback to operate — an AI revenue validation failure in 2027 combined with a widening PJM 2028/29 auction gap is possible, but its probability has already been apportioned into the individual judgments of B and C (for example, C's sharp turn itself implicitly assumes power constraints are no longer the principal contradiction), and listing it separately would constitute double counting. Second, why Scenario E must retain 10%: the product of mutually cancelling negative feedback — capacity landing within the range but auxiliary conditions failing to match, or a hybrid outcome where the two major scenarios each deliver half — needs a home. Third, why this report emphasizes in the monitoring dashboard that synchronous resonance is a risk "not fully priced into the probability distribution": negative feedback needs time to operate; if both fault lines detonate within the same quarter, the cancellation mechanism has no time to engage — this is the tail of the tail, and the ultimate reason Chapter Twelve exists.

### Summary of Judgment Boundaries and Falsification Signals

The falsification signals for B and C point in opposite directions and must not be confused when monitoring. **Falsification of B**: on-schedule energization rates returning to the baseline trajectory (2026Q4–2027Q2 deliveries no lower than eighty percent of the pipeline measure), unexpected expansion of gas turbine slots (GE Vernova capacity released above expectations), or convergence rather than widening of the PJM 2028/29 auction gap — any one of these holding true shifts probability back toward A. **Falsification of C**: unleased leases renting out smoothly (re-negotiation rates staying low), Oracle RPO being consumed gradually rather than retreating by hundreds of billions in a single quarter, and 2027 AI revenue readings landing in $150–300 billion's "good enough" range. **The confusion trap between the two**: if 2027 energization rates fall short while capex simultaneously weakens, one must distinguish by the deferring entity — utility and EPC scheduling problems belong to B, while hyperscaler capital discipline signals (such as a softening of Amy Hood's "plans unchanged" framing) belong to C; this distinction was established in Chapter Six, and here we emphasize that it is the operational pivot for the probability redistribution between B and C.

To conclude this chapter: the 50% downside tail is not a quantification of pessimism, but a probabilization of two paths each grounded in physics and institutions. B prices the production schedule and the auction calendar, with 30% reflecting the near-equilibrium between the cushion and the fault lines; C prices the race between $820 billion unleased leases and the 2027 validation node, with 20% reflecting how demanding the sharp turn is on the trigger's time window. The mutual cancellation of negative feedback rewrites the two from "compounding risk" into "a contest" — this is the core of what distinguishes this report's probability structure from simple bearish narratives. The next chapter will address both tails of the distribution: what stringent conditions must be simultaneously satisfied for 5% upside surprise, and what unnamed outcomes lie hidden in 10%'s "other paths."

## Ten. Scenario-by-Scenario Forecast (III): Upside Surprise (5%) and Other Paths (10%) — Tail Pricing and Trigger Conditions

The two ends of a probability distribution are often the sternest test of a forecast framework's honesty. Reserving 50% for the downside scenario takes courage, but reserving only 5% for the upside and a further 10% for the "unnamed endings" requires something rarer still: a precise distinction between "what conditions upside requires" and "what endings remain statistically." This chapter handles both tails of the distribution — D upside surprise (estimated in this report at 5%) and E other/hybrid paths (10%). Core judgment up front: **D is not a "more optimistic baseline" but a triple joint event — global IT installed capacity exceeding 230 GW by end-2030, single-year capex above $3.5 trillion, and AI annual revenue reaching $2 trillion; missing any one of the three conditions disqualifies it. E, meanwhile, is not a residual bucket but a functional container carrying three classes of hybrid endings: regional divergence, definitional drift, and mutual cancellation of negative feedbacks. Together they account for 15% of probability mass — precisely the component that distinguishes this report from single-point extrapolative forecasts**.

### Anatomy of the D Upside Scenario: Three Independent Gates

The stringency of D's criteria deserves item-by-item dissection, because each gate corresponds to an independent physical or economic constraint, and all of them must open simultaneously.

**The first gate is capacity: global IT installed capacity exceeding 230 GW by end-2030.** This figure sits above the upper bound of this report's baseline range by roughly 10–20%, and above JLL's facility-capacity-delivery figure of 200 GW by about 15%. It is not fantasy — TrendForce's unconstrained pathway gives a theoretical ceiling of 490.7 GW for 2030, leaving 230 GW with nearly double the headroom to the ceiling. But 490.7 GW assumes full realization of the interconnection queue, while LBNL's tracked historical base rate of queue realization is only about 13%, and Georgia regulatory testimony estimated that about 65% of announced MW were speculative and have been removed. D requires the blended pipeline realization rate to jump from the 13% historical base rate to near contracted-pipeline levels — and to do so within four years. The only structural support for this jump is the dominance of the contracted pipeline: AEP's signed large-load pipeline has doubled to over 56 GW, proving that real demand is expanding. Yet even the contracted portion runs into the equipment supply wall: GE Vernova's gas turbines are sold out before 2028, with only about 10 GW of slots remaining for 2029–30 and lead times of roughly three years, while transformer delivery times have doubled in three years, leading the IEA to estimate that about 20% of planned projects face delays.

**The second gate is capex: single-year spending above $3.5 trillion, roughly half a trillion above Dell'Oro's "over 3 trillion" figure.** The binding constraint at this gate is not demand but financing capacity. The four hyperscalers' combined FCF has fallen from $237 billion in 2024 to $200 billion in 2025, with Amazon's TTM FCF at −$7.6 billion; Moody's estimates that of $1.2 trillion in lease commitments, about $820 billion are not yet commenced; SPV off-balance-sheet debt has exceeded $120 billion over two years. Sustaining $3.5 trillion in single-year spending requires debt markets to keep expanding — rather than tightening — their absorption of incremental supply through 2028–2030, which is only possible if 2027 revenue validation delivers an explosive report card.

**The third gate is the hardest: AI annual revenue reaching $2 trillion.** The current baseline is 2025 AI revenue of about $60 billion against capex of about $400 billion — going from 60 billion to 2 trillion is a four-year 33-fold leap. Supportive evidence amounts to a single curve: Anthropic's ARR rising from 9 billion to above $44 billion, with gross margin improving from 38% to above 70%; the breadth of the user base (ChatGPT February 2026 weekly active users above 900 million) and the contracted nature of cloud revenue (Alphabet's cloud backlog of $514 billion) provide an additional revenue floor. But even under the most aggressive extrapolation — top-lab ARR tripling every 18 months — reaching $2 trillion requires monetization to spread comprehensively from model labs to the entire economy: enterprise AI spending, the API economy, and the wholesale monetization of agentic services. This is the most important reason D is suppressed to 5%: **the first two gates are supply-side constraints that capital can force open; the third gate is demand-side validation, and no financial engineering can conjure 33-fold revenue out of thin air in four years**. Conversely, if revenue truly reaches 2 trillion, the two major downside triggers for B/C (renegotiation of non-commenced leases, revenue validation failure) are cut off at the root — which is why the three conditions must be bound by "AND" logic: they are not three independent bets but three facets of one self-consistent world.

### The Only Two Upside Amplifiers: Oracle Fuel Cells and Huawei Capacity

Under such stringent joint conditions, this report identifies only two structural amplifiers capable of lifting the probability from near zero to 5% — their common feature is that each bypasses one gate, yet both are capped by the same class of physical limits.

**The first amplifier is the institutionalization of behind-the-meter generation.** Oracle's fuel-cell behind-the-meter scheme bypasses the grid queue, and its $638 billion RPO-driven expansion does not depend on PJM auction clearing. FERC's co-location order (193 FERC ¶61,217) provides a codified institutional foundation for this path: four transmission-service options move co-location arrangements from case-by-case negotiation (with the precedent of the rejected November 2024 Talen–Amazon nuclear ISA ) into a rules-based channel, although co-located load may be curtailed during the transition period. The key lies in the definitional implication: TrendForce's estimated 268 GW grid gap for 2030 explicitly excludes behind-the-meter generation — if behind-the-meter schemes scale up, this portion of the "gap" would convert into real delivered capacity rather than capacity loss, directly raising the capacity reading of the D scenario. But this report has already quantified its limits in Chapter Three: behind-the-meter diversion is triply constrained by fuel supply, permitting, and cost, and Crusoe-style on-site gas workarounds themselves depend on GE Vernova turbine production — the bypassers and the grid-connected are competing for the same batch of turbines [S19]. **The behind-the-meter route is a necessary amplifier for D, but its ceiling is sealed shut by the turbine production schedule.**

**The second amplifier sits on the other side of the Pacific: Huawei Ascend capacity delivering above expectations.** The planning figure is about 600,000 units 910C for 2026 (1.6 million dies, 2:1 packaging), while SemiAnalysis estimates HBM supply will effectively cap output at 250,000–300,000 units — with the bottleneck precisely located in CXMT's HBM capacity ramp and yields rather than SMIC's die capacity. If the 600,000 units are fully delivered and the ramp continues in 2027, China's training-grade compute constraint would be substantively lifted — demand-side funding is already in place (China Mobile compute investment of RMB 37.8 billion, +62.4%; Alibaba's over RMB 380 billion across three years, self-described as "on the conservative side"), in which case China's capacity contribution could shift meaningfully upward from this report's estimate of 50–65 GW, providing the key Chinese increment for the global total to break through 230 GW. This amplifier also has a unique independence: Chinese chip supply is determined by Beijing's customs rather than Washington's licenses (the day after the U.S. cleared H200, customs restricted entry [S14]), and its readings are immune to the noise of U.S. power politics and financing. The 400,000-unit monitoring threshold set in Chapter Seven is thus simultaneously the shared branch point for the B/C downside scenarios (a visible Chinese shortfall) and the D upside scenario (a released Chinese increment) — **one variable, the switch for two scenarios**.

The strongest bull rebuttal must be stated head-on; this report calls it the "reflexivity argument": every constraint warning over the past two years — turbines sold out, the PJM gap, depreciation accusations — has been swallowed by spending upgrades; Dell'Oro raised its 2030 capex forecast from 1.2 trillion to over 3 trillion, nearly doubling it, within seven months, and Gartner raised its forecast three times in nine months; why should we conclude that this time physics and revenue constraints will not likewise give way? This report responds on two levels. First, the upgrade cycle has always absorbed bad news on the supply side and the pipeline side; it has never truly been tested by bad news on the revenue side — the gap of 60 billion against 400 billion is the unexploded test case. The D scenario demands precisely that the revenue side deliver its best performance in history — not something already in the cycle's track record, but an action the cycle has never completed. Second, the constraint evidence is production-schedule arithmetic, not sentiment: GE Vernova's slot table [S19], CXMT's yield curves, and the three-year transformer lead time [S3] are all physical facts — capital cannot order a gas turbine that has not yet been manufactured to deliver early. The 5% pricing is therefore a faithful compromise on "reflexivity might win, but physics sets a hard ceiling on the winning margin": to admit it and to deny it are equally arbitrary.

### The E Scenario (10%): Not a Residual Bucket but Three Named Endings

E's criterion is "not falling within any complete determination combination of A/B/C/D." Assigning it 10% is not laziness but a probability container for three identifiable classes of paths — each with specific carriers and mechanisms.

**The first is a regional divergence path: the US pulls back while the Gulf and Asia absorb the slack.** This report's scenario framework judges global aggregate volumes, but reality may produce a rebalancing in which "the US tightens while elsewhere compensates." The research materials identify the carriers of this path: the HUMAIN program led by Saudi Arabia's PIF, which plans sovereign AI capacity of 2030 years before $77 billion/1.9 GW, and Google's Gulf AI data center cooperation with PIF, of $10 billion (second-hand aggregated information, mostly framework agreements, with limited confidence, used only as a directional cue); on the Chinese side, the policy backstop structure (Chapter Seven) is far less sensitive to external financing cycles than the dependence of the four major US hyperscalers on debt markets — China Mobile's capital rebalancing directly executes NDRC's compute infrastructure policy rather than quarterly revenue validation. Under this path, US power politics detonate and capex bends, yet the global aggregate could still land within the 190–210 GW range — the criterion for classifying it as A is "steady delivery after pipeline discounting," whereas the landing mechanism driven by sovereign-fund absorption is entirely different, so by discipline it goes to E. **The absorption capacity of sovereign capital is the systematically underweighted variable within E: it is unconstrained by quarterly revenue validation, operates on a political horizon measured in a decade, and the Gulf market has no PJM-style grid-queue politics.**

**The second is landing outside the range due to definitional drift.** Chapter One already argued that the single largest source of forecast error in this field is definitional confusion: the 5–10-fold gap between the 13% base rate and the contracted pipeline; CBRE and JLL giving "flat" versus "+9%" readings of rents in the same market; and the official compute figures of 280→962 EFLOPS within the MIIT system that cannot be mixed due to differences in precision conversion and statistical scope. If the authoritative definitions themselves migrate in 2030 — Dell'Oro or LBNL adjusting statistical scope, behind-the-meter capacity being included or excluded — the actual landing point may physically sit within some range while falling outside it under the report's definitions. This is the uncertainty principle of forecast frameworks: it must be explicitly priced rather than pretended away.

**Third is a hybrid product of mutually offsetting negative feedbacks, which likely accounts for the largest share of E.** Chapter Nine argues that B and C compete with each other for trigger timing: power delays push spending outward and thereby ease the cash squeeze, while financing contraction reduces new projects and thereby eases the grid queue. If each of the two fault lines releases half of its stress — 2027 sees some leases renegotiated but no breach of the 10% threshold, the PJM gap widens but no tri-state suspension order is issued, and US capex decelerates but does not fall below $1.5 trillion — the outcome is an intermediate state of "partial downside that satisfies no full verdict." Such an outcome is not a weakened version of any single scenario, but the natural product of a system partially releasing stress along multiple fault lines simultaneously. E's falsification signal therefore differs from the other scenarios: E is ruled out by means of "one of the other four scenarios fully materializing." By the end of 2028, if leading indicators have clearly pointed to a single scenario, E's 10% should be reallocated in its entirety.

### Tail-end monitoring timetable and operational implications

Although D and E are tail scenarios, their probabilities are not beyond dynamic monitoring. **Three leading signals for D**: First, if Huawei Ascend's actual 2026 output fully delivers 600,000 units and the 2027 plan continues ramping, the China-side amplifier is activated; second, the actual delivered volume of the first batch of behind-the-meter/co-located loads under the FERC co-location option — if behind-the-meter route deliveries in 2027–2028 exceed twenty percent of grid-delivered capacity, the probability of passing the capacity gate shifts materially upward; third, and decisively, the 2027 AI revenue reading — if an Anthropic-style curve diffuses to the second and third tiers, pushing industry revenue to $300 billion or above with no decay in slope, only then does $2 trillion's 2030 endpoint become mathematically reachable again. The joint probability of all three lights turning green simultaneously is the entire content of 5%; if any one goes dark, D is falsified and the probability mass flows back to A or E.

The operational implications for readers can be compressed into two sentences. **First, D is a tradable option, not a base-case assumption** — its value is concentrated in a handful of instruments: providers of behind-the-meter generation and co-location solutions, the domestic HBM supply chain exemplified by CXMT, and equity exposure to frontier labs; its trigger window coincides exactly with the 2027 validation window in Chapter Twelve — it is the call leg of the same lottery ticket. **Second, E is a warning to every investor positioned on a single point estimate**: in a domain where a forecasting institution doubled its own figures within nine months [S1][S2], any position without fault tolerance is paying tuition for definition drift and hybrid outcomes. By mid-2027, the dashboard's first three indicators (on-schedule energization rate, Oracle RPO quarter-over-quarter, and the magnitude of US capex) will suffice to render final judgment on D — most likely falsifying it — and to complete the first triage of E. At that point, this chapter's 15% probability mass will be almost entirely redistributed to A, B, or C — and that will be the true moment of reckoning for this century's infrastructure gamble: the two tails of the distribution are executed by time first, and the middle ground that remains is the reality capital must live with for the long haul.

## XI. Technology Trends and Supply Chains: Liquid-Cooling Standardization, the Rise of ASICs, and the Server Shipment Landscape

The first five chapters answered "how much will be built, who pays, and where the power comes from"; this chapter answers a more physical question: **by 2030, what will the technology stack inside data centers look like, and who defines that form**. The core judgments of this report have three layers. First, after liquid cooling crossed the 2026 penetration rate of 53%, it is degrading from a "technology selection" into a "mechanical interface"—the real new variable is not liquid cooling itself, but the fact that the cooling-component supply chain is beginning to replicate the single-point bottleneck structure of advanced packaging, and that insurance costs (global premiums rising from 10.6 billion to $24.2 billion, with liquid-cooling-related losses accounting for 24% ) are becoming an explicit input into siting and design. Second, the chip competitive landscape is shifting from "NVIDIA vs. nothing" to "NVIDIA-dominant vs. an ASIC camp eroding the edges": NVIDIA maintains an order-of-magnitude scale advantage with FY26 revenue of $215.9 billion, but Broadcom's FY25 AI revenue exceeded $20 billion and it guided to roughly $100 billion/year for 2027, with the object of erosion being margins rather than share. Third, the nominal economics of server shipments are being simultaneously distorted by three forces—tariffs [S40], memory inflation [S24], and the depreciation-accounting dispute [S27]—none of the three changes physical shipment volumes, yet each systematically changes "what a server is on the balance sheet".

### Liquid cooling as interface: heat dissipation is no longer anyone's decision variable

The trajectory of liquid-cooling penetration can already be precisely charted: 14% (2024) → 33% (2025) → roughly 53% (2026E) → roughly 60% (2027E). The driving force behind this curve is not an energy-efficiency preference but NVIDIA's forced generational transition executed through rack-level thermal specifications—GB200 NVL72 ships at roughly 120 kW/rack and mandates direct-to-chip liquid cooling, leaving any buyer of that generation's systems with no air-cooled option physically available. This is the precise meaning of "interfacing": **when an accelerator's performance roadmap is bundled with the cooling solution at delivery, liquid cooling is no longer the operator's decision variable but part of the chip vendor's product definition**. Google's TPU fleet is already more than 80% liquid-cooled [S23], showing that vertically integrated players moved earlier—the coupling of in-house chips with self-built cooling infrastructure is the only path by which hyperscalers can replicate NVIDIA's standard-setting power; Uptime surveys show mainstream rack density across the global installed base remains 10–30 kW [S47], and the divide between the old and new technology generations is no longer a gap but a difference of species.

The next-layer implication of interfacing is often overlooked: **the cooling supply chain is replaying the bottleneck structure of advanced packaging**. Search evidence shows that the vapor chambers for NVIDIA's next-generation Rubin platform are supplied by a single exclusive vendor (Jentech), so the cold-plate/CDU supply chain now replicates the GPU/CoWoS-style single-point dependency; meanwhile the physical ceiling on total accelerator supply still hinges on whether TSMC's CoWoS advanced-packaging capacity can expand from roughly 75–80k wpm to 120–140k wpm by the end of 2026. This constitutes a risk chain parallel to the power constraint (Chapter Three): gas turbines, packaging, and cooling components are three independent production schedules, and any one of them jamming compresses deliveries. Dell'Oro has already published an analysis titled "Is the CDU market heading toward saturation," which indirectly confirms that the upstream capacity race and downstream demand volatility may push this segment from shortage to surplus in 2027–2028—**the certainty in the liquid-cooling track belongs to the demand curve, not to every supplier within it**.

Rising insurance costs are pushing liquid cooling's risks into actuarial language. Swiss Re's sigma research shows global data-center premiums have already risen from 10.6 billion to $24.2 billion, with liquid-cooling-related losses accounting for 24% —the physical coexistence of water and electronics has entered operating budgets through policy terms for the first time. This report judges that by 2030 insurance premiums will be upgraded from "a line item in operating expenses" to a siting input variable: high-density liquid-cooled facilities at sites with high flood and leak risk will face coverage refusal or premium penalties, forming the mirror image of Chapter Five's "capacity with confirmed power priced independently"—**risk pricing is expanding from a single power variable to a dual power-plus-insurance variable**. The structure is parallel on the China side: domestic liquid-cooling suppliers such as Envicool directly absorb the counter-cyclical expansion of the three carriers' computing-power investment (China Mobile's computing-network investment of RMB 37.8 billion, +62.4% ), and liquid cooling is a certainty-increment link within the dual-track regime of "total capex down, computing power up".

### The ASIC camp: what is being eroded is the pricing structure, not the share table

The fundamental landscape of competition is extremely asymmetric: NVIDIA FY26 revenue of $215.9 billion, Broadcom's AI revenue FY25 just exceeded $20 billion with a single quarter of roughly 8.4 billion (+106%), and AMD's MI-series annual revenue of about $7–8 billion—the second and third sources still trail the dominant player by an order of magnitude. Therefore, any narrative that "ASICs will replace NVIDIA" lacks a quantitative basis before 2030. This report's judgment is more precise: **what the ASIC camp changes is not the share table but the pricing structure**, through three mechanisms.

First, Broadcom's AI revenue guidance of roughly $100 billion/year for 2027 comes from foundry design of hyperscalers' in-house chips—Alphabet's TPU track (fleet more than 80% liquid-cooled ) is the most mature exemplar, and Meta executes three-source procurement across Broadcom/AMD/NVIDIA. For NVIDIA, every in-house ASIC substitution within the top customer base costs not just revenue but its pricing anchor in the general-purpose GPU market: when Meta holds 6 GW-scale MI450 orders as leverage, NVIDIA's margins on remaining orders inevitably come under pressure. Second, AMD binds OpenAI (6 GW of deployments plus warrants) and Meta through warrant arrangements—essentially trading equity subsidies for a secondary-supplier position—which is isomorphic to Chapter Four's vendor-financing loop, meaning the second source's expansion likewise depends on capital markets rather than product strength alone; this is also why its significance before 2030 is "bargining power rather than supply". Third, there is a permanently closed gap in the China market: NVIDIA's pre-control share of roughly 95% in China slid toward roughly 50%, corresponding to roughly $15 billion in lost annual revenue, and January 2026 the day after the U.S. side cleared H200 it was restricted from entry by Chinese customs —NVIDIA's China variable no longer sits on its product roadmap; the gap is being split between Ascend 910C and Cambricon, whose revenue surged roughly 453%, with the ceiling determined by CXMT's HBM ramp (Chapter Seven).

The opposing view deserves to be stated squarely: some argue ASIC erosion is overstated—the development cost and iteration risk of in-house chips mean only extremely large players can afford them, and NVIDIA's annual architecture cadence (GB200→GB300→Rubin) sustains a performance lead that keeps general-purpose GPUs the default option before 2028. This report partially accepts this: precisely for that reason, the trigger conditions in every scenario exclude an "NVIDIA share collapse". But the boundary of the rebuttal lies in distinguishing timing from magnitude—by 2030, the real threat from ASICs to NVIDIA is not replacement but **converting the accelerator market from single-seller pricing to two-sided bargaining**, which is enough to compress NVIDIA's gross margin by several percentage points even as its revenue keeps growing. This variable, via NVIDIA's quarterly gross margin, will become the fastest reading for monitoring the migration of bargaining power across the entire AI supply chain.

### The nominal economics of server shipments: a triple distortion

At the physical shipment level, this report's baseline scenario (35% probability) implies the following judgment: AI server shipments expand in 2026–2029 in step with CoWoS capacity ramping from roughly 75–80k wpm to 120–140k wpm; after 2028, if the power constraint (Scenario B) or financing contraction (Scenario C) materializes, the bottleneck on shipment growth switches from packaging capacity to energized capacity and lease commencement rates—**server shipments are the aggregate reading of all downstream constraints and are no longer an independent variable in themselves**. What truly merits separate analysis is the triple distortion of nominal prices.

**The first distortion is tariffs pushing prices, not cutting supply.** Section 232 was announced on January 14, 2026 and took effect the next day, imposing a 25% ad valorem tariff on semiconductors and derivatives, covering data-center products exported to the U.S., with the effect of raising server nominal prices rather than severing supply. Costs are shared along the supply chain: hyperscalers absorb most of it through procurement scale, while the enterprise self-build buyers "squeezed out of the acceleration queue" argued in Chapter Five bear the fullest price increase—tariffs thus become yet another structural driver of market concentration toward hyperscalers, not the reverse.

**The second distortion is memory inflation, and it is harder to circumvent.** DRAM contract prices rose 45–50% in a single quarter at the start of 2026, driven by AI servers genuinely crowding out HBM and high-capacity DRAM, and cannot be avoided by shifting production locations. Its transmission is twofold: directly raising server cost per GW of capacity (this explains why a substantial portion of the upper bound of this report's baseline capex range $3.2 trillion is a price effect rather than a volume effect); and indirectly squeezing the general-purpose server budgets of enterprise and edge scenarios—when the memory cost of a general-purpose server nearly doubles within a year, deferring the replacement cycle becomes the only rational response. **By 2030, the "compute purchased per dollar of capex" curve will be jointly suppressed by CoWoS capacity (the volume ceiling) and DRAM prices (the price floor)**.

**The third distortion is the most hidden: depreciation accounting's distortion of apparent equipment economics.** Burry alleged that hyperscalers artificially extended GPU depreciation lives and under-provisioned roughly $176 billion of depreciation in 2026–28, with Meta's 2028 profit allegedly overstated by about 21% and Oracle's by about 27%—CNBC explicitly stated it could not independently verify this, and this report does not use it as fact. But one confirmed fact provides a directional reference: Microsoft has adjusted its depreciation/lease life from 15 years to 25 years, bringing its CY2026 capex measure down to roughly $175 billion. If GPUs' true economic life is 4–5 years, then part of the "AI profit" in current income statements is a product of deferred depreciation—this does not change cash flow, but it delays the market's judgment on "whether AI makes money". The implication for the supply chain is direct: the gap between the reported presentation of equipment replacement cycles (over 6 years) and the physical reality (4–5 years) means a wave of replacement demand—deferred by accounting conventions but physically impossible to defer—will arrive in 2028–2030; if DRAM inflation persists at that point, the capital intensity of the replacement wave will exceed every current extrapolation's assumptions. **The depreciation dispute is not merely an earnings-quality issue; it is also a hidden accelerator on the demand side of server shipments**.

### Three embedding points into the full report framework

The technical judgments of this chapter ultimately converge into three embedded points. First, liquid cooling and insurance constitute the "cost escalator" of the base-case scenario: neither the deterministic penetration path from 53%→60%+ nor the doubling of premiums depends on AI revenue validation, making them the least scenario-sensitive demand in the entire supply chain—consistent with Chapter Fourteen's thesis that "equipment makers profit from scarcity," though the potential crowding in the CDU segment signals that the marginal return on this position diminishes after 2028. Second, ASIC encroachment and memory inflation jointly compress "compute output per dollar of capex," which is this report's supply-side supplementary rationale for capping 2030 capex at $3.2 trillion and applying a discount to Dell'Oro—extrapolating institutions have priced in volume growth but underweight structural price changes. Third, the single-point bottleneck in cooling components stands alongside CoWoS and gas turbine scheduling as a triple physical ceiling—in the determination of the bull scenario (5%), all three must expand capacity simultaneously, which further explains why the upside is a thin tail: **every layer of the technology stack has its own production schedule, and production schedules do not revise upward with narrative**. By the 2027 verification window, the fastest readings this chapter offers are NVIDIA's quarterly gross margin (bargaining-power migration) and DRAM contract prices (the slope of cost inflation)—together with energization rates and RPOs, they form the complete dashboard for observing whether this capacity expansion collapses from within or continues to build pressure.

## Twelve. Map of Hidden Risks and Inflection Points: Monitoring Dashboard for 2026–2030

This section consolidates the risk threads scattered throughout the report into a single time-ordered monitoring dashboard. The core judgment is that the period from the end of 2026 to mid-2027 constitutes the "verification window" for the entire expansion — on-schedule energization rates, quarter-over-quarter Oracle RPO, and the magnitude of US single-year capex will largely lock in the direction of probability re-estimation across the report's five scenarios within roughly four quarters; meanwhile, the PJM auction shortfall, Huawei Ascend output, and the renegotiation rate of non-commenced leases serve as sentinel indicators on three independent fault lines: power, chips, and financing. The purpose of monitoring is not to predict individual events, but to identify which number moves first, and by how much, enough to change the overall trajectory.

### The 2026–2027 Verification Window: Three Leading Indicators Decide Whether the Base Case Survives

**On-schedule energization rate is the first-priority indicator.** The premise of this report's base case (A, probability 35%) is that the pipeline delivers at the mid-to-high range between historical base rates and the signed pipeline — LBNL's tracking of the 2000–2020 interconnection queues shows a historical realization base rate of only about 13%, while the IEA notes that roughly 20% of planned projects face delays due to equipment supply chain constraints; Georgia's single-quarter exit of 14.3 GW from the pipeline and the classification of about 65% of announced MW as speculative set the upper bound on the speculative end. The monitoring rule is therefore set as follows: if actual on-schedule energized deliveries in 2026Q4–2027Q2 fall below 20% of the pipeline figure, the base-case probability is cut by 10 percentage points, with the corresponding shortfall absorbed mainly by the B power-constrained scenario — because if under-delivery stems from gas turbine slots (GE Vernova has only about 10 GW of remaining capacity for 2029–30, with lead times of about 3 years) and transformer lead times, it is a physical constraint; if it results from demand-side deferral, it migrates toward the C financial-tightening scenario. The observable clue distinguishing the two is who is deferring: whether it is a scheduling problem for utilities and EPC contractors, or a signal of capital discipline from hyperscalers (e.g., any softening of Amy Hood's "plans unchanged" formulation [S39]).

**Quarter-over-quarter Oracle RPO is the fastest read on demand visibility.** The current baseline value is $638B. What makes this figure distinctive is its extremely leveraged structure: Oracle drives debt-funded expansion off RPO (FY26 borrowing $43B, FCF −$23.7B), moves assets off balance sheet, and bypasses grid queues with fuel cells. Monitoring rule: a single-quarter sequential decline exceeding $100B triggers falsification of the base case — because RPO is the thinnest link in the financing loop; construction-side financing was locked in ahead of demand-side contracts, and once the largest single contract book starts to shrink, renegotiation of the $820B non-commenced leases will begin in parallel. A caveat on definitional noise: RPO declines mechanically as contracts naturally commence, and this is difficult to distinguish in real time from declines caused by contract cancellations; it should therefore be cross-validated against whether customer prepayments (currently $75B) and the FY27 capex $90B guidance are revised down in tandem.

**Whether US single-year capex falls below $1.5T by the end of 2027 is the core determinant for the C scenario.** In 2026, the four major players' combined guidance is about $735–760B; adding Gartner's measure of data center systems spending $822B plus Neocloud and enterprise self-builds, the total US single-year pool remains below the trillion scale; Dell'Oro expects global single-year capex to exceed $3T in 2030 [S1]. The base path from the current magnitude to $2.5–3.2T in 2030 implies a compound annual growth rate of about 30% — meaning 2027 is an inflection point of "either keep steepening or begin to bend." The AI revenue verification gap is the driver of the bend: AI revenue of roughly $60B against capex of roughly $400B in 2025; if AI revenue in 2027 remains far below the support line, OpenAI-style spending discipline (the 2030 target revised down from about $1.4T to about $600B, single source) will evolve from an isolated case into industry practice.

### The 2028–2030 Constraint Fault Lines: Two Thresholds — Power Politics and Chip Capacity

**The PJM 2028/29 auction shortfall is the switch that politicizes the power constraint.** The timeline already points the way: the 2026/27 auction hit the cap of $329.17/MW-day $16.1B [S15], and the 2027/28 auction showed a reliability shortfall of 6,623 MW for the first time. This report sets the threshold at a shortfall expanding beyond 5,000 MW — note that 2027/28 already exceeds it, so the real test is whether 2028/29 **continues** to widen and triggers a political response (≥3 states maintaining new-build moratoria beyond 2029). There is a counterargument that must be addressed head-on: some hold that PJM auction price signals are precisely the market's mechanism for solving the problem — prices high enough will reactivate retired units and demand response, so a widening shortfall does not necessarily lead to regulatory tightening. This report partially accepts that argument — it is precisely why the B scenario probability (30%) is lower than A's — but the principle established by the FERC co-location order (193 FERC ¶61,217) that transitional co-located load can be curtailed shows the regulatory allocation tool is already loaded; there is a race between the price mechanism's self-correction and political intervention. The combination of roughly 370 state bills and the ambiguity of co-location rules means that after 2028 the power constraint shifts from a physical form to a regulatory-allocation form, and the focus of monitoring should shift from equipment lead times to the legislative and adjudication calendar.

**The actual 910C output of Huawei Ascend in 2026 determines the China-side bifurcation.** The plan calls for roughly 600,000 units (1.6 million wafers, 2:1 packaging), while SemiAnalysis estimates that HBM supply will effectively cap output at 250,000–300,000 units. The monitoring threshold is set at 400,000 units: below that level, China's intelligent-computing shortage becomes explicit—against a backdrop of China Mobile computing-power investment up +62.4% but total capex down 9.5% and Alibaba's three-year program exceeding ¥380 billion, demand-side funding is already in place, so the shortfall will show up entirely on the supply side, manifesting as a widening bimodal pattern in which top-tier third-party IDC rack utilization (GDS currently 77.5%) tightens further while regional intelligent-computing center utilization (around 30%) stays dormant. Conversely, if the 600,000 units are fully delivered and output doubles again in 2027, that becomes an amplifier of the bullish D-side scenario on the China side. The timeline evidence that chip supply is determined by Beijing Customs rather than Washington's licenses (customs restricted inbound shipments the day after the U.S. cleared H200 [S14]) means this indicator is almost immune to U.S. policy variables, making it the most independent of the five key monitoring indicators.

**The renegotiation rate of non-commenced leases is the last line of defense in the financing loop.** Current baseline: Moody's estimates that of $1.2T in lease commitments, $820B has not yet commenced, and the renegotiation rate is low. The monitoring rule is a two-step ladder: a single quarter above 5% raises the C scenario probability; above 10% triggers a switch to the credit-contraction scenario — because SPV off-balance-sheet debt has exceeded $120B over two years, and the A+ rating structure of the Meta–Blue Owl Hyperion $27.3B JV [S11] depends on continuous validation of lease commencement rates; a renegotiation rate breaking 10% means the SPV cash flow model fails, the refinancing gates of capital providers like Blue Owl/PIMCO close first, and the shock then transmits to CoreWeave-type Neoclouds (debt of about $14.0B, 2026 capex $35–39B).

### The Systemic Risk Dark Side: Four Slow Variables Not on the Timeline

**The depreciation accounting dispute is a ticking time bomb for earnings quality.** Burry alleges that hyperscalers artificially extended GPU depreciation lives, understating depreciation by roughly $1,760B in 2026–28 — the figure is unverified, but the mechanism deserves to be taken seriously: if the actual economic life of GPUs is 4–5 years while accounting treatment stretches it beyond 6 years, a substantial portion of current reported profits is a product of deferred depreciation. This does not directly change cash flows, but it changes the timing of the market's judgment on "whether AI makes money" — once an SEC inquiry or audit adjustment triggers a restatement, the upward shift in discount rates would hit both the four majors' equity financing and SPV debt pricing simultaneously.

**Exit clauses are an underestimated contingent liability within off-balance-sheet structures.** The BIS annual report singles out off-balance-sheet sale-leaseback leverage and highlights, as a macro counterpoint, AI's contribution of about 1pp to US GDP [S26] — the core concern within the regulatory field of view is that exit clauses in JV/SPV contracts will bounce risk back onto sponsors' balance sheets in a nonlinear fashion in a downturn. Meta's retention of a 20% stake in Hyperion [S11] means off balance sheet does not mean off the risk sheet.

**Revenue concentration makes Neoclouds an amplifier of cascades rather than a buffer.** Microsoft accounts for 62% of CoreWeave's 2024 revenue — if Microsoft pulls back on external procurement due to power or capital-discipline reasons, the credit discount on CoreWeave's roughly $104B order backlog would be immediately re-marked, transmitting through its $14B debt stack to the financing costs of the entire Neocloud sector.

**The politicization of electricity prices could close the loop of regulatory backlash.** PJM claims the auction only raises retail bills by 1.5–5%, but this framing and the attribution disputes championed by IEEFA-type groups have already been politicized at the state level. If retail electricity price increases in 2027–2028 are attributed by voters to data centers, the restrictive provisions among the roughly 370 state bills will move from the fringe to the mainstream — this is the concrete path in the B scenario by which "regulatory-allocation-type constraints" replace physical constraints, and the core reason this report sets the B scenario probability at 30% rather than lower.

## Thirteen. Master Forecast Table and Calibration: Probability Summary, Confidence Intervals, and External Benchmark Comparison

This chapter closes out the report: it compresses the analysis of the preceding twelve chapters into a single calibratable, falsifiable master table, and explains where the probabilities come from, where we diverge from external institutions, and when the forecast should be re-evaluated. The value of a forecast lies not in point accuracy, but in stating up front "which signals would expose it if it were wrong"—especially in a domain where the forecasting institution itself nearly doubled its estimate within nine months. An honest calibration discipline is more useful than a pretty median.

### Probability Sources and Confidence Statement: Why Only "Medium"

The probabilities in this report are not an extrapolation of any single institution's compound annual growth rate (CAGR), but a combined judgment from two evidence chains. On the capacity side, three sources are triangulated: the JLL facilities-capacity series of 103→200 GW (2025→2030, CAGR roughly 14%, ) as the delivery-side reference, McKinsey's demand-side 219 GW (of which AI is 156 GW, ) as the demand-side midpoint, and TrendForce's unconstrained ceiling of 490.7 GW () as the theoretical cap if the queue is fully realized—the baseline range of 190–210 GW is essentially a discounted range obtained by layering the 2026–2030 incremental momentum implied by the demand-side series (2026 alone grew +31% in a single year, 122.9→161 GW, ) on top of the delivery basis. On the capex side, Dell'Oro's 2030 >$3T () serves as the upper bound and the four hyperscalers' actual spending inertia as the lower bound; this report's range of $2.5–3.2T covers the lower half of Dell'Oro's figure.

Even so, overall confidence is still rated **medium**, for three layered reasons.

First, the decisive parameter—"pipeline realization rate"—itself carries a 5–10x basis discrepancy. LBNL's historical base rate for interconnection queues is roughly 13% ([S5]); expert estimates cited by Utility Dive put speculative interconnection applications at 5–10x actual built projects; in the Georgia case, about 65% of announced MW were assessed as speculative ([S18])—but signed pipelines (e.g., AEP's large-load pipeline doubling) show that the contracted portion realizes at a significantly higher rate. This report's judgment: 13% applies to announced pipelines, 30–40% to signed pipelines, with the mid-to-high band for mixed pipelines—this choice alone carries at least ±20 GW of capacity error, which we choose to acknowledge explicitly rather than pretend to eliminate.

Second, AI revenue forecasting lacks a reliable historical base rate. 2025 AI revenue of roughly $60B corresponds to about $400B of capex (); whether the gap can converge to "good enough" by 2027 has no comparable precedent—Anthropic's ARR rising from $9B to $44B+ with gross margins of 70%+ () proves top companies can monetize quickly, but a single company's trajectory cannot be extrapolated into an industry revenue curve. This is precisely why Scenario D (which requires AI annual revenue of $2T) is given only 5%: the joint probability of three conditions being met simultaneously is extremely low, and each is subject to hard constraints—the physical ceiling on accelerator shipments ultimately hinges on whether TSMC's CoWoS advanced packaging capacity can expand from roughly 75–80k wpm to 120–140k wpm by end-2026.

Third, several load-bearing data points come from single sources, and this report applies an explicit tiered treatment principle. OpenAI cutting its pre-2030 compute spending target to about $600B rests on CNBC as the sole source ([S10]); ByteDance's 2025 capex of roughly $20B comes from anonymous sourcing and is denied by the company ([S33]). The treatment rule: directional signals from single sources (such as the spending discipline implied by OpenAI's cut) may enter the mechanism analysis, but their figures never enter the baseline parameters of any scenario; ByteDance's capex is permanently downgraded and excluded from the baseline entirely. Likewise, Burry's allegation of roughly $176B in understated depreciation across 2026–28—which CNBC explicitly stated it could not independently verify ()—is used only as a lead on earnings-quality risk, not as fact; also of single origin is the rumored $300B contract between Oracle and OpenAI—never publicly confirmed by either party after the WSJ report, meaning the largest single node on the demand side itself rests on an unverified anchor.

### Explicit Disagreements with External Benchmarks and the Rationale

The comparison with four authoritative institutions, and this report's positions, is as follows:

- **Versus TrendForce (490.7 GW)**: our baseline range is about 2.4x lower. The reason is that the series implicitly assumes full queue realization, contradicting the 13% historical base rate ([S5]); TrendForce's own estimate of a 2030 grid shortfall of 268 GW () is itself internal testimony to the unattainability of its own ceiling. But since TrendForce's basis is an unconstrained demand ceiling, the upper-edge pricing of this report's Scenario D (>230 GW) still uses it as the theoretical cap reference.
- **Versus JLL (200 GW)**: JLL is a facilities-capacity delivery basis, landing almost exactly at the midpoint of this report's baseline range; however, the JLL series does not fully reflect the momentum if the 2026 growth of +31% is partially sustained into 2027–2028, so this report's range top is set at 210 GW.
- **Versus Dell'Oro (2030 >$3T)**: this report's capex range ceiling of $3.2T covers Dell'Oro's figure, but the probability mass sits in the $2.5–3.0T band—that is, slightly more conservative than Dell'Oro. The reason is that the cumulative constraint of financing capacity only becomes fully visible after 2028: the four hyperscalers' combined free cash flow (FCF) has already fallen from $237B (2024) to $200B (2025), Amazon's trailing-twelve-month FCF is −$7.6B (), Barclays projects Meta's FCF turning negative in 2027–28, and with Moody's-basis $820B in not-yet-commenced leases plus the off-balance-sheet leverage flagged by the BIS (), the debt market's capacity to absorb incremental supply carries a tightening risk in 2028–2030 that Dell'Oro's extrapolation does not incorporate. That is the substance of our disagreement with Dell'Oro: Dell'Oro extrapolates demand; this report applies a discount for financing capacity.
- **Versus McKinsey (219 GW demand side)**: the smallest disagreement—this report's baseline range can be viewed as the delivery-basis result of applying roughly a 85–95% realization discount to McKinsey's demand estimate.

The contrary view deserves to be taken seriously. One forceful critique holds that this report underestimates the upside: over the past two years, every bearish signal—Microsoft's lease reclassification, the Georgia pipeline contraction—was ultimately overwhelmed by spending upgrades; the reflexivity of the demand side has not yet lost, and Gartner revising upward three times in nine months to 2026 $822B () shows institutional forecasts systematically lag reality—so why believe that "physical constraints" can stop the money this time? This report's response: that very "lag behind reality" proves current market pricing already embeds the most optimistic path, leaving no free option on the upside; and the constraint-side evidence is physical, not narrative—GE Vernova having only about 10 GW of remaining 2029–30 turbine slots with lead times of about 3 years (), PJM posting its first 6,623 MW reliability shortfall (), transformer lead times doubling in three years ()—none of which expands when capex is revised upward. Assigning Scenario D 5% rather than higher is precisely an acknowledgment that reflexivity may win, but physical constraints set a hard ceiling on how much it can win.

### Historical Calibration Lessons and Reassessment Timing

The magnitude of forecast variance in this field has direct evidence: Dell'Oro's forecast for 2030 single-year capex rose from $1.2T (2025-08 edition, ) to >$3T (2026-08 edition, ) within seven months, nearly doubling; Gartner revised upward three times in nine months ([S21]). This yields two disciplines: first, any institution's single-point extrapolation—including this report's—should be treated as a high-variance estimate, where the width of the range matters more than the midpoint; second, this report's probability distribution is not a static commitment but a prior that rolls forward with each verification node.

The first clearly defined major re-rating window is **around mid-2027**, with triggers ranked by strength of evidence:

1. **2026Q4–2027Q2 on-schedule energization rate**: actual deliveries fall below the pipeline 20%, so the probability of Scenario A is cut by 10 percentage points, with B or C raised accordingly;
2. **Oracle RPO quarter-over-quarter** (currently $638B,): a single-quarter decline exceeding $100B triggers a Scenario A falsification check;
3. **Whether 2027 U.S. single-year capital expenditure falls below $1.5T**: if it falls below, Scenario C enters the primary assessment path;
4. **PJM 2028/29 auctions**: if the shortfall widens beyond 5,000 MW, the probability of political tightening rises significantly and Scenario B's probability is raised;
5. **Huawei Ascend 2026 actual output** (planned at roughly 600,000 units 910C,): below 400,000 units, the China-side capacity contribution is revised down and the center of the global range shifts lower by 5–10 GW;
6. **Renegotiation rate of un-commenced leases** (Moody's methodology, $820B base): >5% in a single quarter raises the weight of Scenario C; >10% switches to the credit-contraction assessment.

One-sentence calibration principle: the width of this report's confidence intervals (capacity ±30 GW, capital expenditure roughly ±$350B) is on the same order of magnitude as the self-corrections external institutions have made within nine months — this is both an honest compromise and the most practically useful risk warning for readers: before the 2027 verification milestones, any more precise point estimate should be treated as narrative rather than forecast.

## Fourteen. Decision Implications: An Action Framework for Operators, Capital Providers, Equipment Vendors, and Policymakers

The preceding thirteen chapters answered "what will happen"; this chapter answers "what should be done about it." The overall judgment is: this report's probability structure — base expansion scenario 35%, with power-constrained 30% and financial-crunch 20% together forming a 50% downside tail (30%+20%, verified to sum exactly to 50%) — means that virtually every decision-maker should allocate resources along the lines of "middle path as baseline, tail as hedging object," while the direction of tail exposure differs sharply across actors: the biggest risk for operators and capital providers is a broken financing loop, the biggest risk for equipment vendors is instead demand-side falsification, and policymakers face a hard-to-reverse institutional choice between two modes of constraint governance. The following discussion proceeds across five categories of actors.

### One. Hyperscalers and AI Labs: Treat Power Slots as Options and Disclosure Metrics as Leverage

For the Big Four and AI labs, the highest-priority action within the 2026–2028 window is not to keep raising capex guidance but to lock in physical supply. GE Vernova's heavy-duty gas turbine backlog totals roughly 100 GW, sold out before 2028, with only about 10 GW of slots remaining for 2029–30 and lead times of roughly 3 years; the IEA likewise notes that about 20% of planned projects face delay risk due to transformer lead times doubling over three years and gas turbine backlogs stretching years out. This means any US project requiring power-on after 2029 must make its generation-equipment decision by 2026–2027. Under the base expansion scenario (which this report estimates at 35%), locking in slots early amounts to a multibillion-dollar physical option; under the power-constrained scenario (30%), that option becomes the single variable separating "deliverers" from "delayers." Oracle's behind-the-meter fuel-cell route can bypass the grid queue under FERC's co-location rules framework and is worth replicating among load-dense East Coast operators — November 2024the precedent of FERC rejecting the Talen–Amazon interconnection agreement [S17] shows that the compliance certainty of grid-bypass paths is now determined by co-location rules rather than case-by-case negotiation.

The second priority is diversifying chip supply. Meta's three-source procurement structure (Broadcom ASICs, AMD MI series, NVIDIA GPUs) is a proven hedging template: Broadcom's FY25 AI revenue exceeded $20 billion with guidance of about $100 billion per year for 2027, and AMD used 6GW deployments plus warrant arrangements to bind OpenAI and Meta. But be clear-eyed about its limits: the AMD MI series generates only about $7–8 billion in annual revenue, an order of magnitude below NVIDIA, and the physical ceiling on overall accelerator supply sits at TSMC's CoWoS advanced packaging capacity (expanding to about 120,000–140,000 wafers per month by end-2026) — the benefits of diversified procurement lie mainly in bargaining power after 2028, not in actual supply increments before 2027. Operators should not expect near-term price relief, and still less should they use cost inflation on top of 25% tariffs under 232 terms and single-quarter DRAM contract price increases of 45–50% as grounds to delay locking in supply.

Third, and most subtly: disclosure-metric management. Oracle's RPO stands at $638 billion, and Moody's estimates that about $820 billion of $1.2 trillion in lease commitments remain uncommenced — these two numbers anchor market confidence in the financing loop. This report's setting of "an RPO quarterly decline exceeding $100 billion" as a falsification condition for the base scenario is not arbitrary: the first visible indicator to be renegotiated on the loop is RPO, followed by uncommenced leases and SPV debt. If management proactively discloses commencement progress ahead of the 2027 AI revenue verification milestone, and presents LOIs and signed contracts in separate layers, it can actually reduce the probability of a collective repricing — the case of the NVIDIA–OpenAI $100 billion/10GW LOI being reported by the WSJ as already "shelved" demonstrates that non-binding letters of intent quickly lose force and get repriced by the market when the cycle turns. By contrast, Microsoft's reclassification of depreciation/lease lives from 15 years to 25 years, combined with Burry's allegation of roughly $176 billion in understated depreciation over 2026–28 (unverified; CNBC explicitly stated it could not independently verify), has already been eroding the credibility of earnings quality. Candid disclosure is insurance in tail scenarios, not a cost.

### Two. Capital Providers: Duration Exposure Before 2027 Is the Core Pricing Variable

For Blue Owl- and PIMCO-type SPV investors, this chapter's decision implications can be compressed into one sentence: the rating feasibility of Hyperion-type structures ($27.3 billion JV, 6.581% coupon, maturing 2049, A+ rating, PIMCO anchoring roughly $18 billion [S11]) rests entirely on lease commencement rates, and the commencement pace of $820 billion in uncommenced leases depends on an exogenous variable capital providers cannot control — 2027 AI revenue verification. The gap between roughly $60 billion in AI revenue against about $400 billion in capex in 2025 means current coupon pricing embeds full confidence in the base scenario; if the financial-crunch scenario (20%) materializes, the renegotiation sequence runs uncommenced leases → RPO → SPV debt, with off-balance-sheet investors last in line but bearing the most complete losses — the BIS annual report has already called out the fragility of off-balance-sheet sale-leaseback leverage.

Concrete action proceeds on three levels. First, for newly issued SPV debt, require lease-up-rate-triggered coupon step-up clauses so that the risk of unleased capacity is explicitly priced rather than dressed up through ratings packaging. Meta's retention of only a 20% stake in the JV—which keeps Hyperion's capital expenditure off its consolidated statements—together with the structural fact that this template is being replicated in projects such as El Paso, shows that such structures will keep supplying the market, so pricing discipline must be front-loaded. Second, for existing exposure, the monitoring thresholds set in this report—raising the probability of financial tightening when the quarterly renegotiation rate of unleased leases exceeds 5%, and switching to a credit-contraction scenario above 10%—should serve as internal de-risking trigger lines for capital providers. Third, note one contrarian hedge: in the power-constrained scenario (30%), physical delays postpone spending but typically do not constitute default, so duration exposure in Scenario B carries far less risk than in Scenario C; what capital providers truly need to hedge is "revenue falsification," not "grid-connection delay." CoreWeave's debt stack (DDTL carrying an investment-grade rating, with roughly $104 billion in backlog orders) shows that new cloud vendors' financing is penetrating the investment-grade market, but its revenue is highly concentrated—Microsoft once accounted for 62% of its 2024 annual revenue—and the divergence between the investment-grade label and credit quality in such structures warrants renewed scrutiny from every allocator. Barclays' forecast that Meta's free cash flow turns negative in 2027–28 should also be incorporated into pricing the hyperscalers' own credit curves.

### Three. Equipment and Supply-Chain Companies: A Rare "Scenario-Insensitive" Position and Its Limits

The three supply chains of gas turbines, transformers, and liquid cooling benefit under nearly all five of this report's scenarios: demand materializes in the base scenario, scarcity lifts prices in the power-constrained scenario, and even in the financial-crunch scenario the gas turbine capacity already locked in before 2028 is hard to cancel. This is the only position category across the entire industry chain that requires no bet on scenario probabilities. Liquid cooling is especially certain: NVIDIA has set rack-level thermal specifications with GB200 NVL72 (roughly 120 kW per rack), driving liquid-cooling penetration from 14% in 2024 to about 53% in 2026 and about 60% by 2027, while Google's TPU fleet exceeding 80% liquid cooling establishes the vertically integrated first-mover path — this trend does not depend on the spending persistence of any single cloud vendor. The associated insurance costs (global data center premiums rising from 10.6 billion to $24.2 billion, liquid-cooling-related losses at 24% of the total) are themselves a new revenue pool for equipment vendors' services and design consulting. On the China side, Envicool, Inspur, and Sugon as domestic suppliers directly absorb the counter-cyclical expansion of compute investment by the three carriers (China Mobile compute investment of RMB 37.8 billion, +62.4%), making them a certainty-benefiting direction within the "falling totals, rising compute" structure.

But "scenario insensitivity" has limits, and the flip side must be faced squarely: if the financial-crunch scenario materializes and spreads to equipment financing — the circular financing model of NVIDIA injecting equity/warrants into OpenAI, xAI, and CoreWeave has already been flagged in a Dallas Fed research note — new equipment orders after 2028 could fall off a cliff, at which point the scarcity of gas turbine slots would turn from a seller's bargaining chip into an execution problem of buyers cancelling contracts. The vendors' countermeasure is to convert 2026–2028 excess profits into post-2029 service and operations contracts, rather than expanding capacity to levels tail scenarios cannot absorb. For the liquid-cooling segment, Dell'Oro has published an analysis titled "Is the CDU market heading toward saturation," warning that crowding risk within this niche is accumulating — a certain segment does not mean certain individual stocks.

### Four. Chinese Market Actors: The Gap Flows to Leading Third-Party IDCs, Not New Regional Projects

The decision logic in China's market differs fundamentally from the US: the constraint is not power or financing but chips. Huawei Ascend 910C plans about 600,000 units for 2026 (1.6 million in dies, 2:1 packaging), but SemiAnalysis estimates HBM supply will compress effective output to about 250,000–300,000 units — if actual output falls below 400,000 units (one of this report's monitoring thresholds), the training-grade compute gap will become explicit, and the HBM quota is both Huawei's constraint and a commercial opportunity for CXMT's capacity ramp, making the domestic memory chain the preferred position for hedging this bottleneck. Under this constraint, scarce compute supply will flow to the vehicles with the highest bids and utilization: GDS's rack utilization is 77.5% and it has landed the first batch of data center public REITs, while VNET posted RMB 9.95 billion revenue in 2025 (+20.5%), delivered 404 MW, and took on about 500 MW of ByteDance orders — the leading third-party IDC model of "high-utilization existing stock plus top-customer binding" is systematically superior to building new regional AI-compute projects (regional AI compute centers average only about 30% utilization). NDRC's 2025 ban on new small-scale compute projects and the MIIT's push to resell idle compute have further institutionally sealed off the expansion path for regional projects.

Specific recommendations for Chinese actors: carriers and cloud vendors (Alibaba's AI-plus-cloud investment exceeding RMB 380 billion over three years) should treat HBM and SMIC advanced-node foundry capacity as a harder bottleneck than power when scheduling production, locking in chip procurement lead times in step with domestic liquid-cooling localization; capital providers participating in Chinese IDC assets should focus on REIT-ized leading existing stock; and the policy side must face up to the gap between official nominal compute figures (the MIIT system's 280→962 EFLOPS) and effective compute, avoiding supply planning based on nominal capacity.

### Five. Policymakers: The Price-Based Path Beats the Freeze Path, and Co-Location Rules Are America's Institutional Watershed

Globally, there are two empirically demonstrated governance paths for managing constraints. The U.S. path: FERC's December 2025 co-location order (193 FERC ¶61,217) directs PJM to establish four transmission service options, with transitional co-located load subject to curtailment before network upgrades are completed, complemented by capacity auction price signals (2026/27 at 329.17 USD/MW-day [S15], and 2027/28 auctions at roughly 333.44 USD/MW-day with the first appearance of a 6623 MW reliability shortfall) — monetizing the constraint through prices, so that behind-the-meter generation and co-location arrangements can find a way out through price discovery. The European path: since January 2022, EirGrid has imposed a de facto freeze on new data center connections in Dublin, with no timeline for lifting it — direct queue refusal, sustained for more than six years. The cost of the former is the politicization of electricity prices: IEEFA and consumer groups attribute rising retail bills primarily to data center load, in opposition to PJM's own claim of only a 1.5–5% increase, and Pennsylvania customers' experience of +14.6% electricity price growth is turning into political pressure; the cost of the latter is the permanent loss of market. This report judges that the PJM-style price-based path is overall superior to the EirGrid-style freeze: the 268 GW grid shortfall estimated by TrendForce for 2030 (excluding behind-the-meter resources) can only be partially absorbed if co-location and behind-the-meter generation frameworks are clearly defined, whereas the freeze path converts the shortfall directly into undelivered capacity — which is precisely the definition of the power-constrained scenario (30%).

A three-sentence action framework for policymakers. First, hold the political bottom line on repricing: if the gap in the PJM 2028/29 auction widens beyond 5000 MW (the key threshold set in this report), electricity-price politics will very likely overwhelm the repricing path and trigger a proliferation of state-level moratoriums — the main institutional channel through which the U.S. slides into a power-constrained scenario. Second, establish disclosure requirements for off-balance-sheet SPV financing early rather than waiting for a credit event: the BIS has already flagged the contrast between off-balance-sheet sale-leaseback leverage and AI contributing roughly 1 percentage points to U.S. GDP [S26]; the cost of ex-ante rules is far lower than that of ex-post bailouts or disorderly deleveraging — off-balance-sheet SPV debt exceeding $120 billion within two years is already large enough to warrant systemic attention. Third, under the LBNL accounting, U.S. data-center electricity consumption in 2030 was 649 TWh (with a range of 521–843, or 11.8% of all U.S. power) — the policy goal should not be to suppress this figure, but to ensure it materializes in a dispatchable, priceable form.

To sum up: operators are buying time (equipment slots and disclosure buffers), capital providers manage duration (lease-up rates ahead of the 2027 validation milestone), equipment makers profit from scarcity (gas turbines, transformers, liquid cooling), Chinese players bet on existing assets (leading IDC operators and domestic memory), and policymakers choose the institutional setup (repricing versus freeze). The optimal actions of these five types of actors differ, but they share one common premise: acknowledging the same set of facts — pipeline numbers are systematically inflated, AI revenues remain unvalidated, and the dual constraints of power and financing are real. Acting on the basis of acknowledging these facts is the only universal strategy for navigating the scenario fork.

## How to verify this forecast (decision criteria and observation indicators)
This section provides **falsifiable, trackable** decision criteria and expiry/trigger-based observation indicators for each scenario, for later verification and calibration.

### Decision criteria for each scenario
- **[35%] A: Baseline expansion (pipeline steadily materializes after discounting)**: Authoritative figures (Dell'Oro or LBNL) published before 2030-12-31 show global IT installed capacity in the 190–210 GW range, and global single-year data center capex in 2029 or 2030 falls between $2.5T–$3.2T
- **[30%] B: Power-constrained (physical/regulatory constraints compress delivery)**: By end of 2030, global IT installed capacity reaches 150–185 GW (below 190 GW), and this is attributable to observable power delays: PJM 2028/29 or 2029/30 auction shortfall >5,000 MW, or ≥3 states maintaining new-build moratoria beyond 2029
- **[20%] C: Financial tightening / credit contraction (AI revenue validation failure triggers renegotiation)**: By the end of 2027 or earlier: US single-year data center capex falls below $1.5T, and the single-quarter renegotiation rate for unleased leases exceeds 10%, or more than two hyperscalers cut multi-year capex guidance by more than 25%
- **[5%] D: Upside surprise (revenue explosion + rapid clearing of supply bottlenecks)**: Global IT installed capacity exceeds 230 GW at the end of 2030, and global single-year data center capex in 2029 or 2030 exceeds $3.5T (Dell'Oro basis), while AI annual revenue reaches at least $2T
- **[10%] E: Other / hybrid paths (including inertia-driven maintenance of the status quo)**: At the end of 2030, global IT installed capacity falls outside every range — A (190–210), B (150–185), C (120–150), D (>230) — or, while within a range, fails to meet that scenario's auxiliary conditions (e.g., B requires evidence of attributable power delays)
