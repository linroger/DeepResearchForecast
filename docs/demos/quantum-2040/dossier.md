# Forecast the development of quantum computing in the United States, China, and the European Union from 2026 through 2040, and its impact on the economy.…

## Executive Summary

This report's central thesis is that quantum computing crosses from the noisy intermediate-scale era into early fault tolerance within the 2026–2040 horizon, that the United States enters that era with the strongest combination of public funding, private capital and hardware leadership, and that the economic payoff arrives late and unevenly — modest by 2030, compounding by 2035, material by 2040 — while the security consequences, above all post-quantum migration, arrive before the commercial ones because "harvest now, decrypt later" makes the risk window effectively open today.

The first inflection point has already been passed in the laboratory. Google's Willow chip (105 qubits, December 2024) demonstrated below-threshold surface-code error correction, halving error rates with each code-distance step [S1], and in October 2025 Google claimed the first verifiable quantum advantage on the same chip [S2]. Vendor roadmaps now cluster the first fault-tolerant machines at 2028–2030 — IBM's Starling in 2029 with 200 logical qubits running 100 million gates [S3], QuEra's Libra in 2028 with 256 logical qubits [S4] — but the definitions differ more than the dates, and independent judgment sits later: the Global Risk Institute's 2025 survey of 26 experts puts a cryptographically relevant quantum computer at only 28–49% probability within ten years and 51–70% within fifteen [S5], while BCG expects broad quantum advantage between 2030 and 2040 and full fault tolerance only after 2040 [S6]. That gap between vendor claims and calibrated expert opinion is the single largest source of uncertainty in this forecast.

Regionally, the US leads on hardware milestones and private capital, anchored by roughly $1 billion per year of federal research spending [S7], though the lapse of NQI reauthorization leaves its policy baseline fragile. China's state-directed funding sustains catch-up capacity even under export controls, and the EU bets on coordinated deployment through the Flagship, EuroHPC systems and national programs. Economically, the market remains small — global quantum-computing revenue was $1.4 billion in 2025 [S8] — against large value pools: McKinsey projects $1.3–2.7 trillion of economic value for companies worldwide by 2035 [S9], while BCG's more conservative model yields $450–850 billion of value creation by 2040 [S6]. NIST's draft transition plan would deprecate RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography after 2030 and disallow it after 2035 [S10], making migration a near-term cost regardless of when a code-breaking machine exists.

The report's scenario frame distributes probability as follows: Steady climb — 40% — incremental roadmap progress delivers early fault-tolerant machines around 2030–2033 with the US leading; Quantum leap — 15% — error-correction breakthroughs in superconducting or neutral-atom systems deliver utility-scale computing before 2030; Long winter — 30% — scaling stalls, commercial value stays niche through 2035 and China's state funding sustains catch-up; Fragmented multipolarity — 15% — regions lead different modalities and no single one dominates by 2035.

The leading indicators to watch are: IBM's claim of user-demonstrated quantum advantage by end-2026 on Nighthawk [S3]; DARPA QBI stage-C verdicts toward verifying utility-scale operation by 2033 [S11][S12]; logical-qubit counts and logical error rates on Starling-class machines; finalization of NIST IR 8547 and federal migration deadlines [S10]; expansion of quantum export controls and allied alignment [S13]; helium-3 supply for dilution refrigeration [S14]; and market revenue tested against the $3 billion 2028 projection [S8].

## Baseline: hardware state of the art by modality

### Superconducting
IBM's 2025-2026 fleet comprises Heron r1 with 133 qubits, Heron r2/r3 with 156, and Nighthawk with 120 programmable qubits [S15]; Nighthawk adds a square lattice with 218 tunable couplers, per secondary reporting [S16]. A community source citing IBM's cloud dashboards reports median two-qubit gate fidelity of about 99.76% on the best Nighthawk device — not confirmed on any primary page [S17]. In 2026 Nighthawk is to run 7,500-gate circuits across up to three 120-qubit modules while Kookaburra prototypes a real-time error-correction decoder [S18]. IBM's qLDPC "gross code" [[144,12,12]] encodes 12 logical qubits into 288 physical qubits, roughly 10x fewer than the surface code, demonstrated as a fault-tolerant memory in Nature in 2024 [S3]. Google's Willow (December 2024), a 105-qubit processor, was the first to demonstrate below-threshold surface-code error correction, halving the error rate at each code-distance step; Google says an RCS benchmark ran in under five minutes versus a claimed ~10^25 years on Frontier [S1]. Google reports the first verifiable quantum advantage on Willow in October 2025, with its next milestone — a long-lived logical qubit requiring millions of components — undated [S2][S19]. China's Zuchongzhi 3.0 (March 2025) has 105 qubits, 182 couplers, 72 μs coherence and parallel fidelities of 99.90% single-qubit, 99.62% two-qubit and 99.13% readout [S20][S21]; per Quantum Computing Report's summary of a Physical Review Letters paper, Zuchongzhi 3.2 (107 qubits) reached below-threshold correction with a distance-7 code and suppression factor Λ = 1.40(6), published December 22, 2025 [S22]. In the EU, IQM reported in June 2026 that directional tile codes cut per-logical, per-round error by up to 1,000x versus the surface code at about 30 physical qubits per logical qubit, targeting fault tolerance by 2030 [S23].

### Trapped-ion
Quantinuum's Helios, released November 2025 (benchmarking paper arXiv:2511.05465), provides 98 fully connected qubits at 99.9975% single-qubit and 99.921% two-qubit fidelity across all pairs, described by the company as the most accurate commercial quantum computer; it reports 48 fully error-corrected logical qubits at roughly 2:1 encoding ("Iceberg" code), 94 logical qubits in a record GHZ state, and 50 error-detected logical qubits running the largest encoded quantum-magnetism simulation to date, better than break-even [S24]. Its H2 product page claims >99.9% two-qubit fidelity [S25]. Microsoft and Quantinuum reported 12 logical qubits with an end-to-end chemistry workflow in September 2024 [S26]. Oxford Ionics, an IonQ company, claims a 99.99% two-qubit fidelity world record (October 2025), while another tracker cites 99.97% — selected-pair versus all-pairs conventions differ [S27][S28][S29].

### Neutral-atom
QuEra's Gemini, launched 2025 and first deployed at AIST in Japan, offers 260 physical qubits at 99.5% physical gate fidelity as a roughly 100-logical-qubit QEC testbed [S30]. The Harvard/QuEra line demonstrated 99.5% two-qubit fidelity in parallel on 60 atoms (October 2023), 48 logical qubits from 250+ physical (December 2023), and an end-to-end fault-tolerant architecture with up to 96 logical qubits (November 2025) [S31][S30]; QuEra cites two-qubit gate fidelities of 99.77% [S4]. Pasqal reports loading over 1,000 atoms in a single shot (June 2024) and logical qubits outperforming physical qubits on differential equations [S32][S33]. Secondary reporting says Google added neutral atoms as a second modality in March 2026 [S34].

### Photonic
PsiQuantum's Omega chipset, announced February 26, 2025 and published in Nature, contains all components for million-qubit-scale fusion-based machines fabricated in a high-volume fab, with compute centers under construction in Brisbane and Chicago; no logical-qubit count is demonstrated publicly [S35]. China's Jiuzhang line holds boson-sampling records: a programmable photonic processor (Nature, 2022) [S36], 255 detected photons with Jiuzhang 3.0 in 2023 [S20], and a programmable 3,050-photon Gaussian boson sampling demonstration posted August 12, 2025 (arXiv:2508.09092), with secondary reporting of a 25.6-microsecond output sample [S37][S38].

### Spin/silicon and topological
Intel, the actor this report tracks for the silicon-spin semiconductor-scaling path, has no dated fault-tolerance roadmap, record qubit count, two-qubit-fidelity figure or logical-qubit demonstration in the evidence reviewed — nor does any academic silicon-spin group appear there; the modality is therefore unrankable on this section's metrics here, a gap in the evidence rather than proof that none exists. What the evidence does fix is the modality's supply-chain position: dilution refrigeration, and with it helium-3, is mandatory for some spin-qubit machines as it is for superconducting and topological ones, while trapped-ion and neutral-atom systems operate at room temperature, per vendor and analyst descriptions [S39][S40]. Spin/silicon thus shares the same cryogenic bottleneck as superconducting: dilution refrigerators from Bluefors, Oxford Instruments and Leiden Cryogenics held roughly 70% of the market per an analyst estimate [S41], and bulk helium-3 traded at $1,900–$2,600 per liter per industry accounts [S39]. Microsoft's Majorana 1 (February 19, 2025), billed as the first topological-qubit processor, remains pre-error-correction: a 4x2 tetron array is to demonstrate entanglement, measurement-based braiding and error detection on two logical qubits, toward a 27x13 array; Microsoft claims Floquet codes cut overhead roughly tenfold, and DARPA selected it for the final US2QC/QBI phase [S42].

Cross-modality comparison needs care: quoted fidelities refer to different gates and averaging conventions, "logical qubit" spans error-corrected universal qubits to error-detected testbeds [S24][S25][S30], and spin/silicon cannot yet be placed on the count-fidelity-logical grid from the evidence at hand.

## Historical trajectory of records and milestones

### 2019-2022: advantage claims open the race
Google's roadmap counts its beyond-classical computation milestone as achieved in 2019 [S19]. China's USTC answered with Zuchongzhi-2, a 66-qubit superconducting quantum computational advantage demonstration in 2021 [S20]. In November 2022 QuEra's analog Aquila machine provided 256 physical atoms [S30], and Nature published a USTC-related demonstration with a programmable photonic processor, noting prior photonic machines were restricted to static gate sequences and vulnerable to spoofing [S36].

### 2023: logical qubits arrive
Quantinuum launched H2-1 on May 9, 2023 with 32 fully connected trapped-ion qubits and a Quantum Volume record of 65,536, per company announcements [S43]. In October 2023 the Harvard/MIT/QuEra collaboration demonstrated 99.5% two-qubit fidelity in parallel on 60 neutral atoms [S31]; in December 2023 it presented the first logical processor, 48 logical qubits from 250+ physical [S30]. Google counts its first logical-qubit prototype as achieved in 2023 [S19]. USTC's Jiuzhang 3.0 detected 255 photons in 2023, with a 10^16-fold speedup over supercomputers claimed in the authors' own account [S20].

### 2024: below-threshold error correction
January: Origin Quantum launched Wukong, with 72 working qubits plus 126 couplers (198 on chip) [S44]. June: Quantinuum expanded H2-1 to 56 qubits and, with JPMorgan Chase, ran random circuit sampling claimed to improve 100x on Google's 2019 result at about 30,000x lower power than classical supercomputers [S45]; Pasqal reported loading over 1,000 atoms in one shot [S32]. September: Microsoft and Quantinuum reported 12 logical qubits on H2 [S26]. IBM demonstrated its gross-code fault-tolerant memory in Nature [S3]. December: Google's Willow, a 105-qubit chip, showed error correction below the surface-code threshold, its distance-7 logical qubit living more than twice as long as its physical qubits [S1][S46].

### 2025: records on every axis
February brought PsiQuantum's Omega photonic chipset [S35] and Microsoft's Majorana 1 [S42]. In March, Zuchongzhi 3.0 (105 qubits, 99.62% two-qubit fidelity) ran an 83-qubit, 32-layer random circuit sampling task that USTC claims is 10^15 times faster than the fastest supercomputer and one million times faster than Google's latest published result — a self-reported claim without a comparable classical-cost counterfactual [S20][S21]. In June, IBM committed to Starling in 2029 (200 logical qubits, 100 million gates) and Blue Jay in 2033+ (2,000 qubits, 1 billion gates) [S3][S18]. October: Google claimed the first verifiable quantum advantage on Willow [S2]; Oxford Ionics claimed a 99.99% two-qubit fidelity record, against 99.97% cited elsewhere [S27][S28]. November: Quantinuum's Helios reached 99.921% all-pairs fidelity on 98 qubits, with 48 error-corrected logical qubits at ~2:1 encoding and a 94-logical-qubit GHZ state [S24]; QuEra shipped Gemini (260 qubits) and published an end-to-end fault-tolerant architecture with up to 96 logical qubits [S30]. DARPA selected 11 companies for Quantum Benchmarking Initiative Stage B on November 6, after Google Quantum AI joined Stage A in September [S12]. In December, Zuchongzhi 3.2 (107 qubits) reached below-threshold error correction (distance-7, Λ = 1.40(6)), which Quantum Computing Report calls the first such milestone outside the United States, about a year after Google [S22]. Origin Quantum's Wukong-180 (180 computational qubits) carries company-reported averages of 99.90% single-qubit, two-qubit and readout fidelity — self-reported specifications, not independently benchmarked, and above Zuchongzhi 3.0's peer-reviewed 99.62% [S44][S20].

### 2026 to date
IQM reported directional tile codes on June 23, 2026 — up to a 1,000x reduction in per-logical per-round error versus the surface code at ~30 physical qubits per logical — targeting fault tolerance by 2030 [S23]. A Zhejiang University group published the first demonstration of zero-noise extrapolation applied to logical qubits in error-correction circuits in Nature Communications on July 1, 2026 [S47].

### Base-rate patterns
Three patterns stand out. Superconducting qubit counts reached US-China parity at 105 (Willow, Zuchongzhi 3.0), while the US led on fidelity and error-correction milestones and China replicated below-threshold correction roughly a year after Google [S1][S20][S22]. Logical-qubit demonstrations grew from 12 in 2024 to 48-96 by 2025 under heterogeneous definitions [S26][S24][S30]. The EU's strongest records lie in fidelity (Helios) and error-correction efficiency (IQM) rather than raw scale [S24][S23].

## Roadmaps to fault tolerance by modality

Stated timelines to fault tolerance now cluster between 2028 and 2030, but on incompatible definitions: QuEra's 2028 machine is "megaquop"-scale (roughly one million logical operations) [S4], IBM's 2029 Starling runs 100 million gates [S3], and IonQ projects a machine it frames as cryptographically relevant for 2028 [S48]. These are vendor claims; definitions, more than calendar dates, carry the disagreement.

### Superconducting

IBM commits to IBM Quantum Starling in 2029, "a large-scale, fault-tolerant quantum computer capable of running quantum circuits comprising 100 million quantum gates on 200 logical qubits," to be built in Poughkeepsie, New York [S3]. Its overhead bet is the qLDPC [[144,12,12]] "gross code," which encodes 12 logical qubits into 288 physical qubits — roughly 10x fewer than an equivalent surface code — demonstrated as a fault-tolerant memory in a 2024 Nature paper [S3]. Dated interim milestones: in 2026, Nighthawk runs 7,500-gate circuits across up to three 120-qubit modules while Kookaburra prototypes a real-time decoder; in 2028, 15,000-gate circuits on up to 1,080 qubits with a complete fault-tolerant instruction-set prototype and magic-state distillation; Blue Jay follows "by 2033+" with 2,000 qubits running 1 billion gates [S18]. IBM also expects users to demonstrate quantum advantage over classical computing by the end of 2026 [S3].

Google publishes no dated fault-tolerance target; its roadmap is six milestones culminating in the ability to "make, connect, and control 1 million qubits" [S19]. Willow (105 qubits, December 2024) achieved the first below-threshold surface-code error correction, halving the error rate at each step from 3×3 to 7×7 grids [S1]. In October 2025 Google claimed the first verifiable quantum advantage on Willow; its next milestone, a long-lived logical qubit, requires "orders-of-magnitude improvement in system performance and scale, with millions of components" [S2]. Hartmut Neven told Reuters in February 2025 that Google aims to release commercial applications within five years, i.e. around 2030 [S49]. IQM (Finland) claims its "directional tile codes" cut per-logical, per-round error rates by up to 1,000x versus the surface code at about 30 physical qubits per logical, targeting fault tolerance by 2030 [S23].

### Trapped-ion

Quantinuum's Helios (November 2025) pairs 98 fully connected physical qubits with 99.921% two-qubit gate fidelity across all pairs, and has demonstrated 48 fully error-corrected logical qubits at roughly 2:1 physical-to-logical encoding under the Iceberg code, 94 entangled logical qubits in a record GHZ state, and 50 error-detected logical qubits — the lowest demonstrated overhead of any modality [S24]. Its roadmap targets Apollo in 2030, a fully fault-tolerant universal machine executing millions of gates on thousands of physical and hundreds of logical qubits [S50]; a later company blog pulls universal fault tolerance forward to "by 2029" [S51]. IonQ's June 2025 roadmap is far more aggressive — about 10,000 physical qubits in 2027 on an Oxford Ionics chip, ~20,000 physical (≈1,600 logical at ~10⁻⁷ error) in 2028, and ≥2,000,000 physical qubits by 2030 — orders of magnitude beyond any demonstrated ion system [S48]. Oxford Ionics, which IonQ is acquiring, reported a 99.99% two-qubit gate fidelity in October 2025 [S27].

### Neutral-atom

QuEra's Gemini (2025, first deployed at AIST Japan) offers 260 physical qubits at 99.5% physical gate fidelity as a roughly 100-logical-qubit error-correction testbed [S30]. Libra, launching on Amazon Braket in 2028, targets 256 logical qubits from >10,000 physical at a 10⁻⁶ logical error rate, with a gigaquop successor of 1,000+ logical qubits from >20,000 physical at 10⁻⁹ targeted for 2028/29 [S30][S4]. QuEra states its roadmap "only includes systems whose building blocks are scientifically demonstrated," citing its 48-logical-qubit processor (Nature, 2023) and an end-to-end fault-tolerant architecture with up to 96 logical qubits (Nature, November 2025) [S4][S31][S52]. Pasqal has reported loading over 1,000 atoms in a single shot (June 2024) and logical qubits outperforming physical qubits on differential equations [S32][S33]. Google Quantum AI has reportedly added neutral atoms as a second modality as of March 2026 [S34].

### Photonic, topological and spin qubits

PsiQuantum's Omega chipset (announced 26 February 2025, published in Nature) contains all components for million-qubit-scale fusion-based computers fabricated in a high-volume semiconductor fab; Quantum Compute Centers under construction in Brisbane and Chicago target the first utility-scale systems, though no logical-qubit count has been demonstrated publicly [S35]. Microsoft's Majorana 1 (19 February 2025) remains pre-error-correction: a 4×2 tetron array is to demonstrate entanglement, measurement-based braiding and quantum error detection on two logical qubits, toward a 27×13 array, with Floquet codes claimed to cut overhead roughly tenfold versus prior surface-code designs [S42]. DARPA selected Microsoft, with PsiQuantum, for the final US2QC/QBI phase [S42]. No dated silicon-spin roadmap from Intel appears in the collected evidence.

## US national strategy and public funding

### The funding baseline and the lapsed 2024 reauthorization

US federal agencies spent an estimated $1 billion on quantum information science research in 2022, the baseline CBO used in scoring reauthorization legislation [S7]. H.R. 6213, as reported on 25 July 2024, would have authorized $1.8 billion in appropriations for FY2025–2029 (CBO cost estimate: $1.3 billion), including $175 million per year in FY2025–2028 for up to five national quantum information science research centers, $25 million per year for a quantum instrumentation foundry program, and $10 million for a quantum reskilling workforce hub; it would also have let DOE, NSF and NIST reprogram $1.1 billion of previously authorized amounts [S7]. The bill lapsed without enactment, leaving authorization to the next Congress.

### The 2026 Senate reauthorization (S.3597)

Senator Todd Young introduced S.3597, the National Quantum Initiative Reauthorization Act of 2026, on 8 January 2026 [S53]. On 14 April 2026 the Senate Commerce Committee ordered the bill reported favorably with an amendment in the nature of a substitute [S53][S54]; committee and trade-press descriptions say it extends the NQI five years, to December 2034 [S55][S56]. Two evidentiary gaps matter for forecasting: no CBO cost estimate exists for S.3597, and neither the available amended text nor the committee's release states a dollar authorization [S53][S54]. At a House Science Committee markup on 29 April 2026, Ranking Member Zoe Lofgren stated, according to her opening statement, that "At the moment, this bill does not have any funds authorized" [S57]. A widely circulated "$138 billion" figure is not a US number: per that same statement, it is a fund China announced in 2025 for public-private partnerships in emerging technologies including quantum [S57].

### DARPA's Quantum Benchmarking Initiative

QBI's stated goal is to determine whether any quantum computing approach can reach utility-scale operation — computational value exceeding cost — by 2033 [S11][S12]. It proceeds in three stages — Stage A (utility-scale concept), Stage B (R&D plan and prototypes) and Stage C (government verification and validation) — as an expansion of the US2QC pilot [S11]. Nearly 20 companies entered Stage A (announced 3 April 2025), and 11 were selected for Stage B as of 6 November 2025 [S11][S12][S58]. Microsoft and PsiQuantum entered the final US2QC phase, equivalent to QBI Stage C, and Google Quantum AI joined Stage A in September 2025 [S12]. New solicitations keep the pipeline open: QBIT Stage A (DARPA-PA-26-02-02), published 9 March 2026 with a 30 November 2026 deadline, and an open announcement (DARPA-PA-26-02) published 14 November 2025 with a 30 December 2026 deadline [S11]. DARPA publishes no per-company award sizes; secondary reporting gives Stage B awards of "up to $15 million" [S59] and PsiQuantum an expanded $125 million Stage C agreement [S60][S61], while a grant aggregator's $1–5 million Phase B figure conflicts, likely reflecting different instruments [S62].

### DOE and CHIPS-related support

DOE remains a principal funder through the national-lab ecosystem, though no program-level DOE quantum budget beyond the CBO's $1 billion government-wide baseline appears in the collected evidence; the House bill's reprogramming provision points to unspent authorized capacity across DOE, NSF and NIST [S7]. The clearest CHIPS-linked dollar figure is PsiQuantum's: the company cites $100 million in proposed federal incentives under the CHIPS and Science Act from the Department of Commerce, alongside its $125 million DARPA agreement, which it calls its most valuable US government agreement to date [S60]. The domestic debate unfolds against rising international commitments: cumulative global public funding for quantum research reached an estimated $56.7 billion by end-2025, after an increase of more than $12.7 billion during 2025 alone [S63][S8].

## China's quantum strategy and state ecosystem

### Plan-driven mandates
China's program is set by five-year planning rather than market signals. The 14th Five-Year Plan (2021–2025) designated "quantum information" the second-ranked cutting-edge science and technology field, behind AI and ahead of semiconductors [S64]. The state launched an S&T megaproject on "quantum information and quantum computing" in 2016 — one of 16 top priorities for 2021–2035 — and established the National Lab for Quantum Information Science (NLQIS) in 2017 under Pan Jianwei, one of only six national labs [S64]. For the current cycle, Ranking Member Lofgren stated at the April 2026 House Science Committee markup that the CCP announced a $138 billion fund in 2025 for public–private partnerships in emerging technologies including quantum — a figure from US congressional testimony, not a verified Chinese budget document [S57].

### State laboratories and milestones
The CAS/USTC complex delivers China's flagship results. Zuchongzhi 3.0 (announced 3 March 2025; published in Physical Review Letters 134, 090601) is a 105-qubit, 182-coupler superconducting processor with parallel two-qubit gate fidelity of 99.62%, parallel readout fidelity 99.13% and 72 μs coherence time; USTC claims its 83-qubit, 32-layer random-circuit-sampling benchmark ran 10^15 times faster than the fastest supercomputer — a self-reported claim that depends on the assumed best classical algorithm [S20][S21]. Its successor Zuchongzhi 3.2 (107 qubits) demonstrated below-threshold error correction with a distance-7 surface code (suppression factor Λ = 1.40(6)), published as a PRL Editors' Suggestion on 22 December 2025 and characterized by trade press as the first such milestone by a group outside the United States [S22]. On photonics, Jiuzhang 3.0 detected 255 photons in 2023 [S20], and a programmable 3,050-photon Gaussian boson sampling demonstration was posted on 12 August 2025 [S37]. Commercially, Origin Quantum's 72-qubit Wukong (January 2024) and Wukong-180 (180 computational qubits) serve a national goal of a 1,000-qubit-class machine by around 2030, though Origin has published no significant error-correction work [S65][S44].

### State funding: large, opaque, contested
The most-cited figure is approximately $15 billion in government funding, originating from McKinsey's 2022 Quantum Technology Monitor and a non-public 14th Five-Year Plan document; Merics states it could not verify the USD 150 billion figure McKinsey attributed to that document, while European pledges (UK, Germany, France, EU, Netherlands) total about USD 12 billion [S64][S66]. ITIF cites Chinese public claims of over $15 billion [S67], and a circulating figure of $17.6 billion is described as mostly public [S68]. Private capital is thin by comparison: SCSP estimates cumulative Chinese private quantum funding at roughly $255 million versus about $3.7 billion for the United States [S69]; Origin Quantum has raised only about $150–165 million, all from domestic investors [S65], though rounds grew larger again in 2024–2026 [S70].

### Networks, controls and self-sufficiency
China Telecom's Tianyan quantum cloud platform has reportedly drawn over 37 million visits from users in more than 60 countries [S41]. US restrictions — from 2020–2021 Entity List additions covering CAS/USTC-linked bodies to the September 2025 listing of CAS's National Time Service Center among 32 entities — constrain the ecosystem [S41][S71], and a 2025 BIS action added seven Chinese entities "for acquiring or attempting to acquire U.S.-origin items in support of advancing the CCP's quantum technology capabilities" [S72]. RUSI assesses the controls disrupted China's hardware and talent development but accelerated domestic substitution: QuantumCTek's ez-Q Fridge entered mass production in February 2024 rated just under the 600 µW BIS control threshold, Origin's SL1000 refrigerator line went operational in Hefei in June 2024, and Liangyi Technology booked ¥74.35 million in 2024 revenue, up 177% [S73][S41]. China also controls 69% of global rare-earth reserves and about 90% of processing, and MOFCOM broadened rare-earth export controls on 9 October 2025 [S74][S75]. This state-funded resilience is the premise of the canonical frame's Long winter — 30% scenario, in which China's state funding sustains catch-up even as commercial value stalls.

## EU strategy: Flagship, EuroHPC and national programs

### The EU-level architecture
The EU stacks three instruments: the Quantum Flagship for long-horizon research, the EuroHPC Joint Undertaking for machine deployment, and the Quantum Europe Strategy for coordination. Merics tallies public pledges by the UK, Germany, France, the EU and the Netherlands at about USD 12 billion — the same order of magnitude as the widely cited Chinese figure, whose opacity makes comparison uncertain [S64]. EuroHPC's budget draws €900 million from Horizon Europe for research and innovation plus EUR 320 million from CEF-2 for HPC, quantum and data interconnection [S76][S77]. By 23 July 2026 EuroHPC had procured and inaugurated six quantum computers across Europe, including PIAST-Q in Poznań and VLQ in Ostrava [S78]. In 2026 it opened quantum-technology funding calls worth €119 million in total, including EUR 20 million for trapped-ion platform technologies and EUR 20 million for superconducting platform technologies over 3.5-year projects [S79][S80], alongside a EUR 2 million quantum-sensor grand challenge [S80].

### Germany
Germany anchors EuroHPC deployment. A procurement launched on 24 November 2023 totals EUR 42.71 million, co-funded by EuroHPC JU, Germany's BMBF and Bavaria [S81]; a further contract signed 15 October 2024 covers two systems at EUR 25 million [S82].

### France
France's national champion is neutral-atom firm Pasqal, which announced loading over 1,000 atoms in a single shot in a quantum processor on 25 June 2024 [S32] and has demonstrated logical qubits outperforming physical qubits on differential equations, presented as an industry first for neutral atoms [S33]. France was also among the first European states to introduce national quantum export controls, in February 2024 [S41].

### The Netherlands, Finland and choke-point equipment
The Netherlands contributes to the European pledge total [S64] and hosts Leiden Cryogenics; with Finland's Bluefors and the UK's Oxford Instruments, three firms hold roughly 70% of the global dilution-refrigerator market — Europe's clearest quantum choke point [S41]. Finland's IQM announced "directional tile codes" on 23 June 2026, reducing per-logical, per-round error rates by up to 1,000× versus the surface code at roughly 30 physical qubits per logical qubit; its roadmap targets fault tolerance by 2030 and a path to 1 million qubits, backed by a planned Nasdaq listing through merger with Real Asset Acquisition Corp [S23].

### Companies, adoption and security alignment
At end-2025 the EU hosted the world's largest count of pure-play quantum companies — 173, ahead of the United States at 164 — per QED-C [S8]. McKinsey's 2026 Monitor finds European companies lead quantum adoption and readiness even though most quantum start-ups are US-based [S9][S83]. Security policy has converged with Washington: on 8 September 2025 the European Commission adopted a Delegated Regulation adding quantum computers, electronic components for cryogenic temperatures, parametric signal amplifiers, cryogenic cooling systems and wafer probes to the EU dual-use control list, aligning with US, UK and Japanese controls [S84][S85]. One gap matters for the regional comparison: an EU-only, year-by-year private investment series could not be verified from primary sources; only global totals are firm — $2.35 billion in 2022 and $1.7 billion in 2023 [S86], and $12.6 billion in 2025 [S87]. The EU's profile — breadth across modalities, choke-point equipment and adoption leadership without a single dominant hardware champion — matches the canonical frame's Fragmented multipolarity — 15% scenario, in which different regions lead different modalities and applications.

## Private investment and company landscape by region

### Funding by year
Private capital followed a boom–plateau–boom path. McKinsey's PitchBook-sourced series records quantum-technology start-up investment of roughly $0.2 billion in 2019 and $2.1 billion in 2021 [S87], $2.35 billion in 2022 falling 27% to $1.7 billion in 2023 [S86], nearly $2.0 billion in 2024 [S88], then a surge to $12.6 billion in 2025 — 6.3 times the 2024 level — with public sources supplying only 3% of 2025 start-up funding versus one-third in 2024 [S87]; government and institutional start-up investment stayed at roughly $0.1–0.7 billion per year across 2019–2025 [S87]. QED-C, screening PitchBook venture deals more narrowly, counts $4.9 billion of global quantum venture capital in 2025, up 192% from 2024's previous record [S63]; the gap between the two 2025 totals reflects scope, since McKinsey includes capital-market deals beyond venture rounds. McKinsey's own vintages also restate history: its 2025 retrospective counts $1.3 billion for 2023 where the April 2024 Monitor had $1.7 billion [S88][S86]. Public money dwarfs private overall: QED-C puts cumulative global public quantum commitments at $56.7 billion by end-2025 after a rise of more than $12.7 billion during 2025 alone [S63][S8].

### Regional flows
The United States dominates private capital. SCSP's 2025 gaps analysis puts cumulative US private quantum funding at roughly $3.7 billion against about $255 million for China [S69]; a US 2025 figure of more than $2.7 billion in venture capital, about $1 billion more than in 2024, circulates in coverage of QED-C's 2026 report but could not be confirmed on the primary page [S63]. An independent analysis of McKinsey's 2026 Monitor reports most quantum start-ups as US-based and about 60% of 2025 investment concentrated in the top ten deals [S83]. China's ecosystem runs on state money — ITIF cites Chinese public claims of over $15 billion [S67] — while the widely cited McKinsey figure of approximately $15 billion traces to a non-public five-year-plan document that Merics states it could not verify [S64][S66]. Origin Quantum, China's leading commercial hardware firm, has raised only about $150–165 million, entirely from domestic investors [S65], though Chinese rounds grew larger again in 2024–2026 after a post-2022 lull [S70]. Europe is the mirror image: the largest pure-play company count and the deepest enterprise adoption, but no verified EU-only annual funding series [S8][S9].

### Company counts, workforce and customers
QED-C identified 7,418 quantum-engaged organizations worldwide at end-2025, including 556 pure-play quantum companies, up 8% since 2024 [S63][S8]. The EU leads pure-play counts with 173 firms, ahead of the United States (164), Canada (59) and the UK (56) [S8]; McKinsey's Monitor, by contrast, describes most quantum start-ups as US-based, a definitional difference that changes who counts as "leader" [S83]. The pure-play workforce numbered about 16,500 professionals in 2025, up almost 2,000 year-on-year, with more than 8,200 new quantum job and internship openings, up 11% [S8]. Revenue remains small: $1.4 billion in 2025, with North America holding roughly 44% share, Europe second and Asia-Pacific fastest-growing [S8]; QED-C projects the market to double to $3 billion by 2028 at about 30% annual growth [S63]. McKinsey counts over 300 companies actively adopting quantum computing (162 analyzed in detail), finds European companies leading adoption, and reports that one-third of surveyed large companies allocated more than $10 million to quantum in 2025, with 7% above $50 million [S9].

### Notable deals and data gaps
Consolidation marked 2025 — including IonQ's roughly $1.1 billion acquisition of Oxford Ionics [S83] — alongside government-coupled financing such as PsiQuantum's reported $125 million DARPA agreement and $100 million in proposed CHIPS Act incentives [S60]; Finland's IQM is preparing a Nasdaq listing via merger with Real Asset Acquisition Corp [S23]. The regional-by-year picture has an explicit gap: no source publishes a consistent annual split of private capital across the three regions. The US 2025 venture figure is unconfirmed [S63]; EU-only totals for 2019–2024 were not found in any primary source, leaving only the global series [S86][S87]; and China's private flows go largely unpublished because opaque domestic rounds blur private and state investors, with Origin Quantum's raise the main fragmentary data point [S65]. Until QED-C or McKinsey publish comparable regional series, company counts, adoption leadership and the North American revenue share serve as the closest proxies for regional private-capital weight [S8][S9].

## Key corporate and institutional actors

### United States
IBM anchors superconducting hardware with the sector's most explicit roadmap: IBM Quantum Starling, promised for 2029, is to run 100 million gates on 200 logical qubits in Poughkeepsie, New York, using qLDPC "gross" codes that encode 12 logical qubits in 288 physical qubits — roughly tenfold less overhead than the surface code — with Blue Jay (2033 or later) targeted at 2,000 logical qubits and 1 billion gates [S3][S18]. In 2026 its Nighthawk processor is to run 7,500-gate circuits across three 120-qubit modules [S18], and IBM claims its users will demonstrate quantum advantage by the end of 2026 [S3], while asserting it is the only organization that will run hundreds of logical qubits by decade's end — a competitive claim, not a measurement [S3]. Google Quantum AI publishes milestones rather than dates: six steps culminating in controlling one million qubits [S19]; Willow (105 qubits, December 2024) demonstrated below-threshold surface-code error correction [S1], and Google says its next milestone, a long-lived logical qubit, requires orders-of-magnitude scale-up to millions of components [S2]; Hartmut Neven told Reuters commercial applications should arrive within five years, around 2030 [S49]. Google joined DARPA's Quantum Benchmarking Initiative (QBI) Stage A in September 2025 [S12].

DARPA itself is an institutional gatekeeper: nearly 20 companies entered QBI Stage A in April 2025 and 11 were selected for Stage B in November 2025, toward verifying utility-scale operation by 2033 [S11][S12]. Finalists concentrate incentives — Microsoft, whose topological Majorana 1 (February 2025) remains pre-error-correction, with a 4x2 tetron-array roadmap toward a 27x13 array and a claim that Floquet codes cut overhead roughly tenfold [S42]; and PsiQuantum, whose photonic Omega chipset (Nature, February 2025) contains all components for million-qubit-scale machines fabricated in a high-volume fab, with utility-scale systems targeted at datacenters under construction in Brisbane and Chicago, though no logical-qubit demonstration yet [S35] — backed by its reported $125 million DARPA agreement and $100 million in proposed CHIPS incentives [S60].

Quantinuum (US/UK) holds the fidelity frontier: Helios (November 2025) delivers 98 fully connected trapped-ion qubits at 99.921% two-qubit gate fidelity across all pairs, with 48 fully error-corrected logical qubits at roughly two physical per logical under its Iceberg code [S24]; it targets Apollo, a fully fault-tolerant universal machine, by 2030 [S50] and separately claims scalable universal fault-tolerant computers by 2029 [S51], with its 12-logical-qubit Microsoft partnership signalling an incentive to be the substrate for Microsoft's stack [S50]. IonQ, the listed champion, pairs an aggressive roadmap — about 10,000 physical qubits in 2027 on an Oxford Ionics chip and roughly 1,600 logical qubits at ~10⁻⁷ error by 2028, reaching two million physical qubits (40,000–80,000 logical) by 2030 [S48] — with M&A-driven scaling through Oxford Ionics, which reported a 99.99% two-qubit fidelity record in October 2025 [S27][S29], and photonic-interconnect firm Lightsynq [S89]; its 2030 target exceeds any demonstrated ion system by orders of magnitude, suggesting public-market positioning. QuEra demonstrated 48 logical qubits in 2023 [S31] and will launch Libra in 2028 with 256 logical qubits at a 10⁻⁶ logical error rate, a gigaquop successor with 1,000+ logical qubits targeted for 2028/29, under a stated policy of roadmaping only scientifically demonstrated building blocks [S4].

### China
The ecosystem centres on USTC and the Chinese Academy of Sciences under Pan Jianwei: Zuchongzhi 3.0 (March 2025) delivered 105 superconducting qubits at 99.62% parallel two-qubit gate fidelity [S20][S21], and, according to Quantum Computing Report's summary of a December 2025 Physical Review Letters paper, Zuchongzhi 3.2 achieved below-threshold distance-7 error correction with suppression factor Λ = 1.40 — reportedly the first such result outside the United States [S22]. USTC also posted the programmable 3,050-photon Jiuzhang 4.0 boson-sampling demonstration in August 2025 [S37]. Origin Quantum sells the 72-qubit Wukong (January 2024) and the 180-qubit Wukong-180, whose 99.90% gate fidelities are self-reported specifications rather than independent benchmarks; it has published no significant error-correction work and missed its own 1,024-qubit-by-2025 target, consistent with a state-directed NISQ focus on a 1,000-qubit-class machine by around 2030 [S44][S65].

### Europe
IQM (Finland) announced directional tile codes in June 2026, cutting per-logical per-round error rates by up to 1,000x versus the surface code at about 30 physical qubits per logical, targeting fault tolerance by 2030 and a path to one million qubits [S23]. Pasqal (France) reports loading over 1,000 atoms in a single shot [S32] and logical qubits outperforming physical qubits on differential equations [S33]. EuroHPC JU acts as a demand-side institution, with six quantum computers procured across Europe as of July 2026 [S78].

## Supply chains and talent pipelines

### Cryogenics and helium-3

Superconducting, topological and some spin-qubit platforms require dilution refrigeration, and every dilution refrigerator runs on helium-3 — scarce because it comes primarily from tritium decay in nuclear-weapons programs rather than natural abundance [S14]. The US DOE Isotope Program, the dominant Western supplier, sources all of its He-3 from tritium processing at the Savannah River Site, South Carolina, making "thousands of liters" available each year [S14]. In the 2008 He-3 crisis demand ran as high as 70,000 liters per year against constrained supply; recycling and alternatives cut projected US federal demand to under 6,000 liters per year, but quantum computing is a new demand stream outside this managed federal allocation [S14]. Security and safeguards applications such as neutron portal monitors still account for roughly 80% of global He-3 consumption [S39]. Industry analyses report bulk prices of $1,900–$2,600 per liter — against historical US auction prices of $40–$85 — with purification to dilution-refrigerator grade exceeding $10,000 per liter [S39]. A single Bluefors XLD1000sl holds about 40 liters [S39], and Interlune's chief executive cites purified prices near $20 million per kilogram, about 7,500 liters [S39][S90]. Bluefors has hedged with lunar deliveries of up to 10,000 liters per year from Interlune for 2028–2037 [S91][S92], and Interlune claims 99%-pure He-3 separation demonstrated in early 2025 plus an AFWERX SBIR contract in November 2025 [S93]. DOE's position that the shortage "has been mitigated" covers federal allocations only; commercial buyers face the tighter market [S14][S39].

Refrigerator manufacturing is concentrated in three European firms — Bluefors (Finland), Oxford Instruments (UK) and Leiden Cryogenics (Netherlands) — holding roughly 70% of the global market [S41]. A refrigerator can take months to procure and cost more than the QPU it hosts [S94]. Exports of dilution refrigerators to Chinese research groups face a presumption-of-denial policy under the September 2024 US rules [S73]. China is localizing: QuantumCTek's ez-Q Fridge (base temperature ~10 mK, 450 µW at 100 mK — just under the 600 µW control threshold) entered mass production in February 2024; Origin Quantum's SL1000 line (1,000 µW at 100 mK, 840 coaxial lines) went operational in Hefei in June 2024; Liangyi Technology reportedly earned ¥74.35 million in 2024 revenue, up 177% [S41]. He-3 is irrelevant to trapped-ion and neutral-atom systems, which run at room temperature with laser cooling [S39][S40].

### Lasers and isotopes

Trapped-ion and neutral-atom machines share a precision-laser supply chain — named suppliers include M Squared Lasers (UK/US) and TOPTICA Photonics (Germany) — and the laser stack has been described as "the defining engineering challenge" of the neutral-atom modality [S40][S95]. Neutral-atom platforms rely on rubidium and strontium, tying them to mineral and rare-earth chains where China controls 69% of global reserves and roughly 90% of processing, leverage expanded by its October 2025 export controls [S74][S75].

### Control electronics

Who controls qubit control electronics is the least-documented link in this chain, and that asymmetry is a data gap rather than evidence of Western abundance: no leading Western control-electronics vendor, and no capacity or lead-time data for such suppliers, appears in the sources assembled here. Governments, however, already treat these components as strategic. The EU's 8 September 2025 delegated regulation updating its dual-use control list added quantum computers, electronic components for cryogenic temperatures, parametric signal amplifiers, cryogenic cooling systems and wafer probes [S84][S85]; US rules subject cryogenic circuits exported to Chinese quantum suppliers to a presumption of denial [S73]. IISS assesses that no single country dominates the quantum supply chain, with regime effectiveness resting on a small number of geographically dispersed critical-node firms [S96]; RUSI adds that the chain is shallower and more dispersed than semiconductors, and that controls have accelerated Chinese domestic substitution in microwave interconnects [S73]. Documented Chinese capability includes Origin Quantum's Tianji 4.0 control system, supporting 500+ qubits [S65].

### Talent pipelines

The pure-play quantum workforce was approximately 16,500 professionals globally in 2025 (up almost 2,000 from 2024), with more than 8,200 new quantum job and internship openings in 2025, an 11% increase [S8]. Europe hosts the most pure-play quantum companies (173), ahead of the United States (164) and Canada (59) [S8]. McKinsey projects almost 250,000 new quantum-computing jobs by 2035 and warns that fewer than half of quantum jobs in 2025 will be filled absent pipeline changes [S97][S98]; The Quantum Insider projects about 250,000 jobs by 2030 and 840,000 by 2035 [S99]. Controls also act on talent: RUSI assesses that US restrictions have disrupted China's quantum talent development even as they push China toward self-sufficiency [S73], while US firms' reliance on foreign-person employees shows in the deemed-export general license built into the 2024 rules [S100].

## Export controls and security restrictions

### The 2024 US control architecture

The US Department of Commerce's interim final rule, effective 6 September 2024 (89 FR 72926), added and revised Commerce Control List entries for quantum technologies and other advanced technologies, coordinated with international partners [S13]. The rule adds ECCNs covering quantum computing, materials and related assemblies, and imposes new deemed-export licensing requirements in the quantum sector [S100]. It creates License Exception IEC for countries that have implemented comparable controls — the UK introduced similar controls earlier in 2024 — and a new General License authorizing deemed exports of quantum technology and software to persons from Country Group D:1 and D:5 destinations, subject to annual reporting, end-use and end-user restrictions and recordkeeping [S100][S73]. Exports of dilution refrigerators to Chinese research groups, and of cryogenic circuits to Chinese quantum suppliers, face a presumption-of-denial review policy [S73].

### Entity List and investment restrictions

Controls predate and extend beyond the 2024 rule: quantum entities linked to CAS and USTC were added to the Entity List in 2020–2021 [S41]. In March 2025 BIS shifted upstream, adding seven Chinese supply-chain entities including the cryogenics companies Scikro and Physike and, for the first time, scientific equipment distributors [S41]. A Federal Register rule effective 12 September 2025 added 32 entities, 23 under China, among them the Chinese Academy of Sciences' National Time Service Center [S71]; a BIS press release states that "Seven entities located in China are added for acquiring or attempting to acquire U.S.-origin items in support of advancing the CCP's quantum technology capabilities" [S72]. Outbound investment rules effective 2 January 2025 prohibit US investment in Chinese quantum computing, military quantum sensing and quantum communication [S41]. The House passed the Remote Access Security Act 369–22 in January 2026 to extend controls to remote cloud access, even as China Telecom's Tianyan quantum cloud platform has reportedly drawn more than 37 million user visits from over 60 countries [S41].

### EU dual-use alignment and allied coordination

The European Commission adopted a Delegated Regulation on 8 September 2025 updating Annex I of EU Regulation 2021/821, adding controls on quantum computers, electronic components for cryogenic temperatures, parametric signal amplifiers, cryogenic cooling systems and wafer probes — aligning the EU with US, UK and Japanese controls [S84][S85]. France imposed comparable controls in February 2024, the UK in April 2024, and Japan and Canada in mid-2024 [S41]. IISS assesses that the US and allies have built aligned controls covering almost the entire quantum "stack" — complete quantum computers, enabling components, equipment and advanced materials — but that regime effectiveness depends on a small number of geographically dispersed critical-node firms rather than list comprehensiveness, because no single country dominates the quantum supply chain [S96].

### How controls reshape supply chains and competition

RUSI judges that US controls have disrupted China's quantum hardware and talent development while accelerating China's domestic supply-chain localization in microwave interconnects and dilution refrigerators; because the quantum supply chain is shallower and more dispersed than semiconductors, it is easier to innovate around, so controls "may run counter" to long-run US competitiveness [S73]. China has retaliated: it barred exports of several controlled minerals in December 2024 [S101], and on 9 October 2025 MOFCOM announced broad controls on rare-earth and battery materials and technology [S102]. Security policy also includes PQC mandates: NIST published its first three final post-quantum standards in 2024 and advises migration [S103]; under its draft transition plan (IR 8547) quantum-vulnerable algorithms such as RSA-2048 would be deprecated after 2030 and disallowed by 2035 [S103][S104], and National Security Memorandum 10 is reported to set 2035 as the target for completing federal migration [S105][S106]. In the funding race, Representative Lofgren noted China's 2025 announcement of a $138 billion fund for public-private investment in emerging technologies including quantum [S57]. Supply-chain bifurcation of this kind is the mechanism at the core of the Fragmented multipolarity — 15% scenario, while the localization it forces on China underwrites the state-funded catch-up posited by Long winter — 30%.

## Market size and sector value pools

### Current revenue and near-term trajectory

Global quantum-computing revenue was $1.4 billion in 2025, with North America holding roughly a 44% share, Europe second, and Asia-Pacific the fastest-growing region, driven by China, India and Japan, per QED-C's State of the Global Quantum Industry 2026 [S8]. QED-C projects the market to reach $3 billion in 2028 at roughly 30% annual growth [S8]. McKinsey's Quantum Technology Monitor 2026 likewise reports that quantum-computing companies generated more than $1 billion in revenue worldwide in 2025, and projects this could grow to as much as $4.4 billion by 2028 [S9]. Adoption is broadening: McKinsey counts more than 300 global companies engaging with quantum computing (162 analyzed in detail), finds European companies leading adoption, and reports that one-third of surveyed large companies allocated more than $10 million to quantum initiatives in 2025, with 7% allocating more than $50 million [S9].

### Revenue forecasts for 2030, 2035 and 2040

For 2030, The Quantum Insider's base case grows the hardware-plus-software vendor market from an estimated $1 billion in 2024 to approximately $5 billion by 2030 (36% CAGR), with a bullish scenario of $15 billion in annual revenue, and expects quantum-computing-as-a-service to exceed 40% of the market by decade's end [S99]. BCC Research projects $1.6 billion in 2025 rising to $7.3 billion by end-2030 at a 34.6% CAGR [S107], and MarketsandMarkets, cited by SpinQ, puts the market at $3.52 billion in 2025 growing to $20.20 billion by 2035 [S108] (both reported, not independently confirmed).

For 2035, McKinsey's 2025 Monitor projected quantum-computing revenue of $28–72 billion, within a total quantum-technology market of up to $97 billion (communication $11–15 billion; sensing $7–10 billion) [S88]; a secondary analysis of the 2026 Monitor reports an updated quantum-computing range of $43–71 billion inside a $60–100 billion quantum-technology market [S83]. Independent research firms sit far lower — Precedence Research projects $19.44 billion by 2035 [S109] and SNS Insider $18.91 billion at a 29% CAGR [S110] (both reported) — differences that reflect scope (narrow vendor revenue versus hardware, software and services) and adoption assumptions.

For 2040, BCG projects a sustained $90–170 billion annual market for hardware and software providers [S111], while McKinsey's 2025 Monitor sees the total quantum-technology market reaching $198 billion by 2040 [S88].

### Sector value pools

McKinsey identifies chemicals, life sciences, finance and mobility as the industries seeing the most quantum-driven growth, centered on molecular and materials simulation for candidate screening; its 2026 Monitor discloses no per-sector dollar figure for chemicals and life sciences [S112][S9]. For finance, McKinsey estimates $400–600 billion of value by 2035 — an average impact of 3.0–4.5% across the sector — produced by applying 1–8% impact hypotheses to 2035 sub-sector baselines (corporate banking $3,500 billion, retail banking $4,200 billion, payments $1,600 billion); the figure is documented on a third-party critique of the Monitor's top-down method [S113]. For pharmaceuticals, McKinsey's $50–400 billion by 2035 assumes 5–20% R&D savings and 5–15% clinical-trial savings against today's methods, and acknowledges overlap with generative AI [S83]. In aggregate, McKinsey projects $1.3–2.7 trillion in cumulative economic value for companies worldwide by 2035, up from roughly $2 trillion in its 2023 Monitor [S9][S111], while The Quantum Insider projects more than $1 trillion in total value creation between 2025 and 2035, of which vendors would capture about $50 billion [S99].

### Definitional caution

Baseline figures conflict: roughly $1 billion for 2024 [S99], $1.4 billion for 2025 [S8], more than $1 billion for 2025 [S9]; an earlier McKinsey figure of $4 billion for 2024 circulates but is unconfirmed on fetched pages. Lower ends of these ranges fit Long winter — 30%; Quantum leap — 15% would pull realized value toward the upper bounds earlier than vendor base cases.

## Macroeconomic impact: productivity, GDP, jobs

### Aggregate value creation by 2035 and 2040

The headline macro estimates are value-creation ranges rather than modeled GDP effects. McKinsey's 2026 Monitor projects $1.3–2.7 trillion of economic value for companies worldwide by 2035 [S9]. BCG's 2024 forecast is later and smaller: $450–850 billion of economic value by 2040, sustained by a $90–170 billion hardware-and-software market, under a phase model of NISQ until 2030, broad quantum advantage 2030–2040, and full fault tolerance only after 2040 [S6]. The gap reflects definitions — McKinsey counts value at stake to end users across use cases, BCG models realized value creation — and BCG explicitly concedes its near-term NISQ value assumptions "have proved optimistic and must be revised," while attributing 20–30% of future value to brand-new use cases that do not yet exist [S6]. No direct estimate of quantum computing's contribution to GDP growth was found in the evidence gathered for this report; what exists are sector value pools and vendor revenue projections [S9][S6].

### Productivity and adoption channels

Productivity gains are expected via molecular and materials simulation, optimization and machine-learning workloads; McKinsey's 2025 Monitor puts the quantum-computing market at $28–72 billion by 2035 within a quantum-technology market of up to $97 billion, rising to $198 billion by 2040 [S88]. Near-term productivity claims remain vendor assertions: IBM states its users will demonstrate quantum advantage — "solving problems cheaper, faster, or more efficiently than classical alone" — by the end of 2026, positioning quantum as an HPC accelerator [S3]. Corporate spending scales ahead of proven advantage: one-third of large surveyed companies allocated more than $10 million to quantum in 2025, and 7% more than $50 million [S9], while venture investors put $1.2 billion into quantum computing in 2023 even as overall technology investment fell by half [S6].

### Jobs

The pure-play quantum workforce was approximately 16,500 professionals globally in 2025, up almost 2,000 from 2024, with more than 8,200 new quantum job and internship openings in 2025, an 11% year-over-year increase [S8]. The Quantum Insider projects roughly 250,000 new quantum-computing jobs by 2030 and about 840,000 by 2035 [S99]; McKinsey projects almost 250,000 new sector jobs by 2035 and warns that, absent pipeline changes, less than 50% of quantum jobs in 2025 will be filled [S97][S98]. The near-identity between The Quantum Insider's 2030 figure and McKinsey's 2035 figure is coincidental; the projections differ in scope and are not directly comparable.

### Migration costs and the risk window

Post-quantum cryptography migration is the principal near-term macro cost. A July 2024 report to Congress from OMB with ONCD, CISA and NIST estimated about $7.1 billion (2024 dollars) to migrate prioritized federal civilian systems over 2025–2035, excluding national security systems, with some systems requiring hardware replacement [S114]. Enterprise migration is slower: a 2025 preprint estimates 12–15+ years for large enterprises [S115], and the PQC products-and-services market was estimated at $1.9 billion in 2025 by Future Market Insights [S116] (both reported). On the threat side, the Global Risk Institute/evolutionQ Quantum Threat Timeline Report 2025 (26 experts, published March 2026) puts the averaged probability of a cryptographically relevant quantum computer at 28–49% within ten years — the highest in the series' history — and finds 92% of experts assign at least 50% probability within twenty years [S117]; the 2024 edition estimated 19–34% within a decade [S118]. Harvest-now-decrypt-later is described by the Cloud Security Alliance as "not a forecast — it is an ongoing operation" [S119] (reported). These costs land earlier than quantum value: under Steady climb — 40%, moderate commercial value arrives by 2035 while migration spending is already committed, whereas Long winter — 30% delays the value side without deferring the security bill.

## Post-quantum cryptography migration and the risk window

### Standards and official deadlines

NIST published its first three final post-quantum cryptography standards in 2024 and now advises organizations to begin migrating, with cryptographic modules validated under the Cryptographic Module Validation Program [S103]. Its transition plan, NIST IR 8547 (initial public draft, November 2024), proposes deprecating quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms — RSA, ECDSA, ECDH and finite-field Diffie-Hellman — after 2030 and disallowing them entirely after 2035; the dates remain a draft proposal rather than settled rule [S10][S104]. NIST's stated rationale for the 2035 cutoff is the expectation of a viable quantum technique for breaking current encryption by that horizon [S120]. Federal policy points the same way: National Security Memorandum 10 (May 2022) sets 2035 as the target for completing migration across federal systems, reinforced by the Quantum Computing Cybersecurity Preparedness Act of December 2022 [S105][S106]. High-risk systems are expected to transition well before those deadlines [S103][S104].

### What migration costs

A July 2024 report to Congress from OMB, with ONCD, CISA and NIST, estimated approximately $7.1 billion (2024 dollars) to migrate prioritized federal civilian information systems to post-quantum cryptography over 2025–2035 [S114]. The estimate covers high-impact, high-value systems only and excludes national security systems, including DoD and intelligence networks; timelines also depend on vendor and CMVP certification capacity [S121][S114]. Part of the cost arises because cryptographic functions embedded in firmware or specialized hardware cannot support post-quantum algorithms without hardware replacement [S121]. The private sector faces a longer haul: a November 2025 preprint estimates migration timelines of 12–15+ years for large enterprises, extending well beyond the federal 2035 horizon [S115]. Spending on PQC products and services was separately estimated at $1.9 billion in 2025 — a market-revenue figure, not a total migration cost [S116].

### How soon RSA and elliptic curves can fall

The Global Risk Institute / evolutionQ Quantum Threat Timeline Report 2025 (26 experts, published 9 March 2026) puts the averaged expert probability of a cryptographically relevant quantum computer at 28–49% within ten years — the highest ten-year estimate in the survey's seven-edition history — and 51–70% within fifteen years, judging a CRQC "quite likely" by 2035 [S5][S122]; the optimistic averaged five-year likelihood is about 15% [S122]. Thirteen of 26 respondents put the ten-year likelihood at about 50% or higher, six above 70% [S122][S117]; at twenty years, 92% put it at 50% or higher and nearly half called it extremely likely [S117]. The averaged ten-year series has climbed every edition: 17–31% (2023), 19–34% (2024), 28–49% (2025) [S118][S122], with a third-party summary rendering it as roughly a quarter by 2030 and about half by 2035 [S123]. Recent cryptanalysis drives the revision: Gidney's sub-million-qubit RSA-2048 factoring estimates, Google Quantum AI's reduced resource estimates for elliptic-curve breaking, and a "Pinnacle Architecture" analysis placing RSA-2048 below 100,000 physical qubits under aggressive assumptions [S117]. Most experts commenting on covert, state-backed programs said such work could shift the timeline by two or more years, so the survey may understate the threat [S117].

### Harvest now, decrypt later

The operative risk is not the CRQC's arrival date but the gap between it and migration completion. Under harvest-now-decrypt-later, ciphertext recorded today is decrypted retroactively; the Cloud Security Alliance describes HNDL against AI infrastructure as "not a forecast — it is an ongoing operation" [S119]. The arithmetic is unforgiving: expert medians point to a machine in the mid-to-late 2030s [S5][S122] while large enterprises need 12–15+ years to migrate [S115], so any RSA/ECC-protected data whose confidentiality must survive past roughly the early 2030s is already inside the exposure window — precisely what NIST's 2030 deprecation and 2035 disallowance are designed to foreclose [S10][S104]. Under Quantum leap — 15%, error-correction breakthroughs deliver utility-scale machines before 2030 and turn migration into a crisis; on the frame's other paths the binding constraint is organizational speed rather than physics.

## Inflection points and regional leadership assessment

### Inflection 1: NISQ to demonstrated error correction (2024–2027)

BCG's phase model runs NISQ until 2030, broad quantum advantage 2030–2040, and full fault tolerance after 2040 [S6]. The first marker has already been passed: Google's Willow (105 qubits, December 2024) demonstrated below-threshold surface-code error correction, halving error rates with each code-distance step [S1], and in October 2025 Google claimed the first verifiable quantum advantage on the same chip [S2]. IBM states its users will demonstrate quantum advantage — solving problems cheaper, faster or more efficiently than classical computing alone — by the end of 2026, with Nighthawk running 7,500-gate circuits and Kookaburra prototyping real-time error correction [S3][S18]. These are benchmark demonstrations on physics problems, not commercially relevant advantage; Microsoft's competing topological path remains pre-error-correction, targeting two logical qubits before scaling [S42].

### Inflection 2: early fault tolerance and commercial advantage (2028–2033)

Dated commitments cluster tightly: QuEra's Libra in 2028 with 256 logical qubits at a 10⁻⁶ logical error rate (megaquop scale) [S4]; IBM Quantum Starling in 2029 with 200 logical qubits running 100 million gates [S3][S18]; Quantinuum's Apollo around 2030, universal and fully fault-tolerant at millions of gates [S50]; and DARPA's Quantum Benchmarking Initiative independently testing whether any approach reaches utility-scale operation by 2033 [S12]. Google publishes no dated fault-tolerance milestone [S19], though Hartmut Neven told Reuters in February 2025 that commercial applications are aimed at within five years [S49]. IonQ's claim of ~1,600 logical qubits at 10⁻⁷ error by 2028 and 2,000,000 physical qubits by 2030 [S48] sits far outside demonstrated trapped-ion capability and reads as public-market positioning. Because "fault-tolerant" spans megaquop to billion-gate systems, arrival will be a zone (2028–2033), not a date. Commercial proof will show in revenue: quantum computing companies generated more than $1 billion in 2025, with McKinsey projecting up to $4.4 billion by 2028 [S9]; QED-C puts 2025 revenue at $1.4 billion, doubling to $3 billion by 2028 [S8]. An independent engineering estimate places the first genuinely useful applications at 2035–2040, several million physical qubits from today's machines [S124].

### Inflection 3: cryptographic relevance (mid-2030s onward)

The GRI 2025 survey makes a CRQC "quite likely" by 2035 — 28–49% within ten years, 51–70% within fifteen [S5][S122]. No major vendor except IonQ (2028 claim) places cryptographic relevance before 2033 [S48]; expert consensus, NIST's 2035 disallowance and BCG's "full fault tolerance after 2040" all point later [S10][S6].

### Who leads at each stage

The United States leads through inflection 2: Google's below-threshold first [S1], US/UK Quantinuum's Helios with 98 trapped-ion qubits at 99.921% two-qubit fidelity across all pairs and 48 fully error-corrected logical qubits (November 2025) [S24], Oxford Ionics' claimed 99.99% two-qubit record [S27], IBM's dated roadmap [S18], and the deepest capital pool — global quantum venture investment hit $4.9 billion in 2025, up 192% [S63], and most quantum start-ups are US-based [S83]. China holds superconducting qubit-count parity: Zuchongzhi 3.0's 105 qubits match Willow's count at 99.62% two-qubit fidelity [S20][S21]; it leads photonics outright (Jiuzhang 3.0's 255 detected photons [S20]; Jiuzhang 4.0's 3,050-photon programmable sampling, August 2025 [S37]); and, per Quantum Computing Report, Zuchongzhi 3.2 achieved below-threshold correction in December 2025 — the first group outside the US, roughly a year after Google [S22]. With state funding widely cited near $15 billion, a figure whose provenance analysts have struggled to verify [S64][S70], and a 1,000-qubit-class machine targeted around 2030 [S65], China is running roughly a year behind in superconducting error correction and could narrow that at inflection 3. Europe leads in pure-play company count (173 versus 164 in the US) [S8] and in corporate adoption [S9], with credible fault-tolerance bets in IQM (2030 target, tile codes cutting error-correction overhead up to 1,000×) [S23] and Pasqal (over 1,000 atoms loaded) [S32], but lacks a US-scale champion. The frame brackets the outcomes: under Steady climb — 40%, the US converts 2029–2033 milestones into durable leadership and cryptographic relevance follows after 2035; under Quantum leap — 15%, a superconducting or neutral-atom breakthrough pulls utility scale before 2030; under Long winter — 30%, scaling stalls while China's state funding sustains catch-up; under Fragmented multipolarity — 15%, regions split modalities and none dominates by 2035.

## Scenario frame and probabilities

### Scenario Probability Distribution
- **Steady climb (40% probability):** Incremental progress on roadmaps delivers early fault-tolerant machines around 2030-2033 with the US leading, moderate commercial value by 2035 and cryptographic relevance after 2035.
- **Quantum leap (15% probability):** Error-correction breakthroughs in one modality (likely superconducting or neutral-atom) deliver utility-scale quantum computing before 2030, triggering large economic value and an urgent PQC crisis.
- **Long winter (30% probability):** Scaling stalls on fidelity and overhead across all modalities, commercial value stays niche through 2035, and hype-driven funding retrenches while China's state funding sustains catch-up.
- **Fragmented multipolarity (15% probability):** Different regions lead different modalities and applications, export controls split supply chains, and no single region dominates fault tolerance or commercial advantage by 2035.

The frame allocates probability across the space bounded by two evidence clusters. Vendor roadmaps put the first fault-tolerant systems at 2028–2030: IBM Quantum Starling in 2029 with 200 logical qubits running 100 million gates [S3], QuEra's Libra in 2028 with 256 logical qubits at a 10⁻⁶ logical error rate [S4], and Quantinuum's Apollo targeted for 2030 [S50]. Independent expert judgment sits later: the Global Risk Institute's 2025 survey of 26 experts puts a cryptographically relevant quantum computer at 28–49% within ten years and 51–70% within fifteen [S5], while BCG expects broad quantum advantage only between 2030 and 2040 and full fault tolerance after 2040 [S6]. Revenue remains small — more than $1 billion in 2025 per McKinsey [S9], $1.4 billion per QED-C [S8] — so scenario separation turns on milestones, not current cash flows.

### Steady climb — 40%

Causal path: error rates and error-correction overhead improve on near-current trajectories — qLDPC codes cutting qubit requirements roughly 10x [S3], Helios-class 99.921% two-qubit fidelity across all pairs [S24] — so an error-corrected machine with hundreds of logical qubits operates around 2030–2033, first in the US. Supporting evidence includes IBM's dated roadmap (Starling 2029; Blue Jay at 2,000 qubits and 1 billion gates by 2033+) [S18], Quantinuum's 48 error-corrected logical qubits at roughly a 2:1 encoding on Helios [S24], and Google's below-threshold Willow demonstration [S1]. Moderate value by 2035 matches BCG's $450–850 billion by 2040 [S6]; cryptographic relevance after 2035 matches the survey's ~15% five-year average with most mass beyond ten years [S122]. Signposts: IBM's 2026 Kookaburra real-time decoder and 2028 15,000-gate milestone [S18], and QBI Stage C outcomes [S11]. The probability falls if Starling's gate-count specification slips past 2030 or no Stage B approach survives verification [S12].

### Quantum leap — 15%

Causal path: one modality — most plausibly superconducting or neutral-atom — compresses the below-threshold path faster than planned, reaching utility scale before 2030. Feasibility evidence: Willow halved the logical error rate at each step from 3x3 to 7x7 grids [S1]; IonQ's June 2025 roadmap claims ~1,600 logical qubits at ~10⁻⁷ error by 2028 [S48]; QuEra targets 1,000+ logical qubits in 2028/29 [S30]; the expert ten-year bin jumped from 19–34% (2024) to 28–49% (2025) [S118][S122], and a majority of experts commenting on covert programs said these could pull the timeline in by two years or more [S117]. Why only 15%: vendor "fault-tolerance" claims span megaquop to billion-gate systems and are not comparable [S4][S18]; cryogenic supply is slow and expensive, with helium-3 near $2,500 per liter [S94]; Origin Quantum's 2021 promise of 1,024 qubits by 2025 went unmet [S65]. A distance-9+ code below threshold with suppression factor above 2 on commercial hardware, or a PsiQuantum Omega-fabricated system running utility-scale circuits [S35], would raise it.

### Long winter — 30%

Causal path: physical-error rates fall more slowly than code thresholds require, overhead stays near 1,000:1, commercial value stays niche through 2035, and the 2025 funding boom retrenches while China's state funding sustains catch-up. Evidence: BCG states its near-term NISQ value assumptions "have proved optimistic" and that quantum computing today provides no tangible advantage [S6]; McKinsey's $400–600 billion finance value estimate has been criticized as top-down with an AI-overlap problem [S113]; 2025 start-up investment of $12.6 billion was 6.3x 2024's level [S87], a spike vulnerable to reversal; China's public commitment — widely cited near $15 billion from McKinsey's reading of a non-public plan, with a $150 billion variant Merics could not verify — dwarfs its roughly $255 million of cumulative private capital [S66][S64][S69][S67]. Signposts: QBI Stage B attrition [S12], 2027 investment below 2025, consecutive IBM or Google milestone slips, and continued state-line progress such as the reported below-threshold Zuchongzhi 3.2 result of December 2025 [S22].

### Fragmented multipolarity — 15%

Causal path: export controls harden into two partially disconnected stacks and different regions lead different modalities, so no region dominates fault tolerance or commercial advantage by 2035. Evidence: IISS finds no single country dominates the quantum supply chain, with control effectiveness resting on dispersed choke-point firms [S96]; RUSI assesses that US controls accelerated China's domestic refrigerator and interconnect industry, including QuantumCTek and Origin production lines [S73][S41]; the EU leads pure-play company counts with 173 firms versus 164 in the US [S8]; China holds the photonic advantage line with 3,050-photon Gaussian boson sampling (August 2025) [S37] while the US leads fidelity (Helios at 99.921% [S24]; Oxford Ionics claims 99.99% [S27]); the EU aligned its dual-use list in September 2025 [S84] and China retaliated with rare-earth controls [S102]. Signposts: divergent regional PQC mandates [S10], and neutral-atom leadership consolidating in Europe (Pasqal's logical-qubit demonstrations) rather than the US [S33].

## Resolution-ready forecasts and indicators to watch

The forecasts below are dated, binary, and calibrated against three baselines: the Global Risk Institute expert series, primary vendor roadmaps, and the QED-C/McKinsey market series. Probabilities are this report's calibration, not vendor claims.

1. **Starling at specification by 2029.** IBM delivers Quantum Starling meeting its published specification — 200 logical qubits, 100 million gates — by 31 December 2029. 40%. The commitment is dated and specific [S3][S18], but the largest error-corrected demonstrations to date run on tens of logical qubits [S24][S30].
2. **Hundred logical qubits by 2030.** At least one vendor operates a fully error-corrected system with ≥100 logical qubits by 31 December 2030. 55%. Four roadmaps converge there [S3][S50][S4][S48], and Helios already entangled 94 logical qubits in a GHZ state [S24].
3. **Commercial advantage by 2030.** A quantum computer demonstrably beats classical methods on a commercially relevant problem for a paying external customer by 31 December 2030. 50%. IBM claims user advantage by end-2026 [S3]; Deloitte's scenario analysis finds vendors projecting tangible benefits by 2030 [S125]; Google's quantum lead told Reuters in February 2025 that commercial applications could arrive within five years [S49]; BCG places broad advantage in 2030–2040 [S6].
4. **QBI verification by 2033.** DARPA's Quantum Benchmarking Initiative verifies at least one utility-scale concept by 31 December 2033. 45%. That is QBI's stated test [S11], with 11 companies in Stage B as of November 2025 [S12].
5. **CRQC by 2035.** A cryptographically relevant quantum computer breaks RSA-2048 or equivalent elliptic-curve cryptography by 31 December 2035. 30%. Averaged expert likelihood within ten years (to ~2036) is 28–49%, with only ~15% within five years [S5][S122]; reduced resource estimates below one million qubits [S117] remain hardware-untested.
6. **CRQC by 2040.** The same threshold is met by 31 December 2040. 60%. The fifteen-year average is 51–70% [S5]; at twenty years, 92% of respondents put the probability at ≥50% [S117].
7. **Revenue above $5 billion in 2030.** Global quantum-computing revenue exceeds $5 billion in calendar 2030. 55%. QED-C projects $3 billion in 2028 at ~30% annual growth from $1.4 billion in 2025 [S8]; The Quantum Insider's base case is ~$5 billion by 2030 [S99]; McKinsey projects up to $4.4 billion by 2028 [S9].
8. **NIST keeps 2035.** The final NIST IR 8547 retains 2035 as the year quantum-vulnerable algorithms are disallowed. 70%. The November 2024 draft would deprecate them after 2030 and disallow them after 2035 [S10][S104].
9. **Federal migration by 2035.** Prioritized US federal civilian systems are substantially migrated to post-quantum cryptography by 31 December 2035. 40%. The estimated cost is ~$7.1 billion over 2025–2035, constrained by certification capacity and embedded cryptographic hardware [S114][S121]; enterprise migrations are estimated at 12–15+ years [S115].
10. **China's kiloqubit by 2030.** China fields a superconducting processor with ≥1,000 physical qubits by 31 December 2030. 40%. China's stated goal is a 1,000-qubit-class machine around 2030 (reported) [S65]; verified records are Zuchongzhi 3.0 at 105 qubits and 99.62% two-qubit fidelity (March 2025) [S20] and the 180-qubit Wukong-180 [S44].
11. **Chinese Λ≥2 by 2030.** A Chinese group demonstrates below-threshold error correction with suppression factor Λ≥2 by 31 December 2030. 45%. Quantum Computing Report reports USTC's Zuchongzhi 3.2 reached Λ=1.40 in December 2025 [S22]; Google's Willow result is cited at Λ≈2.14 [S47].
12. **European fault tolerance by 2032.** A fault-tolerant system from an EU-headquartered vendor operates on EuroHPC infrastructure by 31 December 2032. 25%. IQM targets fault tolerance by 2030 with directional tile codes [S23], but EuroHPC procurements to date are NISQ-scale, in the tens of millions of euros [S81][S82].
