## Part 2 — Framework & Synthesis

### Framework: How This Forecast Is Built

The method is straightforward: fix a rigorously documented baseline, define the end-states on an observable continuum, weigh the drivers that move the war between states, and let the driver evidence set probabilities rather than narrative preference.

Three design choices matter. First, the continuum is anchored to measurable criteria — signed instruments in force, monthly Shahed-type UAV launch counts, casualty rates, mobilization decrees — so every probability in Part 1 is falsifiable. Second, probabilities start from historical base rates of war termination and are then adjusted by conflict-specific evidence. Third, contested quantities (Russian casualties, recruitment, territorial momentum) are treated as ranges, never point estimates, and never allowed to carry a conclusion alone.

The base rates are sobering. And roughly half of terminated conflicts recur within five years. These priors push toward continuation at reduced intensity, or a de facto freeze, rather than a signed settlement — and they shape the scenario probabilities defended below.

### Baseline: The War in Late October 2026

Attrition is heavy and unresolved in direction.

Diplomacy runs through a single channel: after Putin's ninth meeting with US envoy Steve Witkoff on October 21, 2026, the Kremlin agreed to resume trilateral technical-level talks, possibly in Abu Dhabi, on or after November 10 [S6]. Putin bundles goodwill — Iran, fertilizer, detainee releases — while holding core demands: Ukrainian demilitarization and a permanent NATO ban. The gap is the security-guarantee architecture itself.

The sanctions coalition is split.

### The Causal Logic of the Probabilities

**Driver 1: military sustainability.** Neither side can force a decision. Recruitment lags but does not crater.

**Driver 2: recruitment versus attrition.** Continued recruitment shortfall pushes toward a freeze as offensive capacity erodes; a new mobilization decree would push toward re-intensification.

**Driver 3: the single diplomatic channel.** The trilateral track is the only path to a signed instrument.

Broadening sanctions, or Congress binding the waivers over White House objection, increases pressure toward a signed instrument;

Absent visible curtailment, Russian drone and munitions output is sustained, which caps Ukraine's ability to convert attrition into battlefield decision — again favoring continuation over settlement.

### Decisive Evidence

Five observations do most of the probabilistic work. (1) The front's near-stasis — net Russian losses since September 2026 with ISW calling the Donetsk offensive defeated — removes an escalation-from-strength path. [S14] (3) The Russian 2027 budget's dependence on borrowing and windfall taxes shows a war economy that can fund attrition but not a decisive escalation. [S43]

Contested evidence was handled by preferring the more conservative bound. Where recruitment-versus-losses bands overlap, we do not claim Russian manpower collapse;

### Weighing Prediction-Market Anchors

Where market anchors exist, they were used as calibration checks, not as primary evidence. Markets price probability-weighted consensus including liquidity distortions and attention bias; Where market-implied ceasefire odds exceeded our estimate, we held to the driver evidence — the untouched core terms, the split coalition, and termination base rates — and only shaded toward markets where our driver analysis independently pointed the same way.

### Implications Logic

The implications forecasts in Part 1 follow mechanically from the scenario mix. the dollar stays firm on safe-haven demand that only fades in the settlement tail. CEE currencies, already backing off highs [S76], track the same gradient.

The bottom line: the most probable end-2027 world is a quieter but unfinished war — territory within a few percent of today's lines, strike tempo below the 2026 peak but nonzero, no signed instrument — and every Part-1 probability is built to survive that world being tested. [S23]

## Baseline: The War in Late October 2026 — Territory, Attrition, Diplomacy, and Sanctions

This chapter establishes that verified baseline. Its central finding is that the baseline is genuinely ambiguous in a way that raw headlines obscure: Russia still holds roughly a fifth of Ukraine, but its main offensive has failed; strike tempo is near record highs, but the diplomatic channel has just reopened; and the sanctions coalition is simultaneously tightening (in Brussels) and fraying (in Washington).

### Territory and Front-Line Dynamics: A Stalled Offense, Not a Stalemate

The proximate cause was Ukraine's Operation Vivaldi in the Lyman direction, which defeated the northern pincer of the planned encirclement. This matters for forecasting in two ways. First, it removes the most plausible path by which Russia could have converted attrition into a territorial breakout during 2026–2027, which mechanically raises the weight of reduced-intensity and freeze scenarios relative to a Russian-victory path. [S43] Second, it changes the negotiating context: Zelensky's insistence that "Russia is not winning" now has independent battlefield verification, strengthening Kyiv's refusal to surrender Donbas. but the recruitment data reviewed below suggest regeneration is getting harder, not easier.

The escalation pressure, meanwhile, has migrated off the front line and into the strike campaign and the Black Sea littoral. Most consequentially for alliance politics, likely Russian drones struck two commercial tankers in Romanian territorial waters on October 15, 2026 — a strike on NATO-member waters that produced no allied military response. [S17] The pattern is unmistakable: tactical defeat on the ground, escalating punishment of the rear. Ukraine has answered in kind — its October 7, 2026 strikes on Russian military and oil assets were among its largest of the war [S1, S15].

### Attrition Accounting: Three Irreconcilable Numbers and What They Do Agree On

Casualty figures for this war arrive in competing accounting regimes that must be presented, not averaged. These figures are consistent with one another once scope is respected: the General Staff's number includes all personnel "losses" (killed, wounded, and possibly captured), the Western estimate covers killed-plus-wounded, and the probate count counts only verified deaths.

The more decision-relevant number is the flow rate, and here the evidence is genuinely contested.

Ukraine's manpower picture is less well documented in public data, but its dependency structure is clear. On the manpower ledger, Ukraine's recruitment-versus-replacement balance is not established in public data — an information gap this report flags rather than fills.

### Diplomacy: One Channel, Twice Restarted, Zero Agreement on Core Terms

All serious diplomatic traffic runs through a single US-mediated trilateral channel that has been restarted twice. The channel revived on October 21, 2026, when Putin held his ninth in-person meeting with US envoy Steve Witkoff at the Constantine Palace in St. [S6] Petersburg and agreed to resume technical-level trilateral talks, possibly in Abu Dhabi, on or after November 10, 2026, focused on post-war security guarantees [S6]. A preparatory meeting in Oslo on October 20 brought Witkoff together with Zelensky, Nordic leaders and EU/NATO representatives, where Ukraine arrived with "new ideas and proposals" while the Kremlin was reportedly "less upbeat". [S14] Reporting on October 13, 2026, describes a joint US-European four-point plan envisaging territorial concessions from both sides, security guarantees, and economic agreements — though its content remains unverified. [S3]

The obstacle structure is stark. Zelensky has held a standing offer since November 2024 — restated on October 21, 2026 — to freeze the current frontline for an immediate ceasefire, with US and European security guarantees to follow, while refusing to surrender Donbas [S2, S6]. [S6] Putin's reported demands run in the opposite direction: Ukraine must drastically shrink its army and permanently forswear NATO membership, positions that do not intersect with US-assured "strong security guarantees" for Kyiv [S1, S6]. Putin's negotiation style in the revived channel is instructive: at the October 21 meeting he bundled cooperation offers on Iran and fertiliser, a detainee release, and even a floated Trump–Xi meeting — goodwill side-payments that keep the process alive while avoiding movement on any core Ukraine term [S6]. For a mediator fixated on deal reputation, process itself is a deliverable;

### Sanctions and Aid: A Coalition Splitting Along the Atlantic

The Western pressure regime is no longer unitary. The European Commission's formal response called the waivers "regrettable". The result is a sanctions regime whose trajectory depends on an institutional tug-of-war inside Washington, not on a unified allied strategy.

Enforcement statistics on the oil price cap and shadow fleet are not publicly established — a transparency gap noted in the research record — but the coalition split between US waivers and EU rounds is now the defining feature of the regime. For Moscow, this split is strategically valuable: it allows Russia to sell diesel to Western-adjacent channels while Brussels alone bears the reputation cost of escalation, and it gives Putin an incentive to keep talking to Washington precisely to widen the transatlantic gap.

Ukraine's financing baseline is equally precarious.

### The Macro Backdrop: A War Premium Already in the Price

Finally, the baseline must register that global markets are already pricing sustained conflict.

The baseline, in sum: a war in its fifth year in which the attacker's principal offensive has failed, attrition is near-even but trending against Russian recruitment, the sole diplomatic channel has just reopened without any convergence on core terms, the sanctions coalition is fracturing along the Atlantic, and markets carry a premium consistent with continuation. These are precisely the conditions under which wars historically do not end by signed accord but by fighting that gradually loses intensity — the modal pattern that Chapter 2 formalizes into probabilities.

## The War-State Continuum: Defining and Forecasting the End-2027 Position

### Defining the Continuum: Five States with Measurable Boundaries

This chapter fixes a five-point continuum and attaches to each point resolution criteria that can be verified after the fact from open sources — strike counts, casualty documentation, territorial trackers, and the existence (or absence) of a registered legal instrument.

**(b) Grinding attritional conflict at reduced intensity.** The modal single outcome. Criteria: no signed ceasefire instrument in force; This is a war neither escalated nor ended — fighting at a tempo both sides can finance but neither can convert into decision.

The distinction from (b) is measurable quietude, not paperwork.

**(d) Formal armistice or ceasefire agreement.** Criteria: an instrument signed by authorized Russian and Ukrainian representatives, verified by UN or OSCE registration or official gazette publication, and in force on December 31, 2027. [S43] Signature alone without entry into force does not qualify.

**(e) Comprehensive peace settlement.** Criteria: a settlement covering territorial terms, security guarantees, and sanctions-relief sequencing simultaneously signed and in force — the highest bar, and historically the rarest.

Two properties of this continuum deserve emphasis. Second, movement between states is asymmetric: war termination research finds the modal ending of armed conflict is fighting that "simply ceases," not victory and not a signed peace accord [S9, S24]. The base rates therefore pull probability mass toward the middle-left of the continuum — toward quiet without paperwork — rather than toward the formally negotiated right end.

### The External View: What War-Duration History Actually Implies

Naive base rates mislead here, and it is worth being explicit about why. the Russia-Ukraine war is in its fifth year. Slantchev's duration analysis of interstate wars 1816–1985 found termination hazards that are duration-dependent, with a median duration on the order of eleven months [S28] — meaning this war has already survived roughly five times the median, and the survivors of that filter are disproportionately the stubborn cases.

The negotiated-outcome scenarios get their modest weight from the Korea precedent rather than from the base rates: armistices do emerge from protracted stalemate negotiations, but the Korean case took more than two years of talks *after* the front stabilized [S25, S26]. The trilateral channel — restarted after Putin's ninth in-person meeting with US envoy Steve Witkoff on October 21, 2026, with technical talks possibly in Abu Dhabi from November 10 [S6] — is roughly fourteen months from its serious opening at the resolution date. That is early by Korean-clock standards, which is why a signed instrument carries roughly one chance in six, not one in three.

### Territory at End-2027: The Narrow-Band Forecast

Territorial movement has slowed to a crawl. Recent momentum collapsed to roughly three square miles of change in four weeks. Russia is not collapsing; it is also not regenerating offensive mass.

The symmetric downside is capped by Russia's demonstrated inability to convert its 17.1-trillion-rouble 2027 defense budget [S43] into renewed territorial conquest, given the Donetsk offensive's defeat and recruitment that lags losses at the margin [S13, S37].

The analysis below explains the mechanics of each path, why the "silent freeze" is historically the modal way wars of this type end, what would have to happen for a signed instrument to emerge within fourteen months, and why the comprehensive-settlement tail remains thin.

### The Frozen Frontline (30%): The Historically Modal Ending Nobody Signs

The single most important finding of war-termination research is counterintuitive: most armed conflicts do not end through negotiated instruments at all. The UCDP Conflict Termination dataset shows that the modal termination type is fighting that "simply ceases," rather than ending in victory or a signed peace agreement [S9][S24]. Analyses of the Ukraine war's termination options have reached the same structural conclusion: endings are harder than they seem, and formal settlement architectures routinely outrun the diplomatic capacity of the belligerents [S30].

Three mechanisms drive the freeze. First, mutual exhaustion of offensive capacity. An army that cannot advance does not necessarily stop shooting, but its strike tempo becomes economically rationed: glide bombs, Iskander missiles, and drone barrages are consumed for deterrence and signaling rather than for territorial gain. Second, Russian manpower arithmetic. Third, American pressure. The Trump administration's leverage-first framing — a negotiated end, in the State Department's May 2026 formulation, backed by "take a deal or watch it get worse" pressure directed mostly at Kyiv — gives Washington both the incentive and the tools (aid, intelligence, Starlink/Starshield access) to push Ukraine toward accepting quiet on existing lines. [S6]

Zelensky's standing offer — held since November 2024 and restated on October 21, 2026 — to freeze the current frontline for an immediate ceasefire with US and European guarantees to follow is, ironically, the political template: a freeze without an instrument is a freeze Ukraine has already said it would accept, so if guarantees stall in negotiation while fighting subsides on its own, the battlefield outcome converges on the Ukrainian opening position minus the legal form [S6].

The rebuttal is that the historical record of *how* fighting ceases — abruptly, without paperwork, when both sides' capacity and willingness exhaust simultaneously — does not require the war to have been quiet before; it requires the exhaustion event. The freeze, in other words, is not diplomacy succeeding; it is economics finishing what diplomacy could not.

### The Formal Ceasefire (17%): Korea's Lesson and Its Limits

Signed interstate ceasefires, in the small constructed sample available, have been strikingly durable. The channel's survival through an unrelated Middle Eastern war suggests the Trump administration's deal-reputation incentive is structural, not tactical.

The obstacles within fourteen months are nonetheless formidable, and they explain why the probability sits below one in five. Russia's reported terms demand that Ukraine drastically shrink its military and pledge never to join NATO; the US has assured Ukraine "strong security guarantees"; and these two positions do not intersect — Washington's guarantee implies a durable Western security alignment with Kyiv that Moscow's NATO ban is specifically designed to prohibit. None of these gaps is individually unbridgeable; the conjunction within fourteen months is what is improbable. A reported joint US-European four-point plan circulating in October 2026 — envisaging territorial concessions from both sides, security guarantees, and economic agreements including a deal with Europe — shows the architecture is being drafted, but its content remains unverified and both capitals have disclaimed parts of it. [S3]

The most plausible route to signature is a partial instrument: a ceasefire covering the ground front and energy infrastructure, with territorial and guarantee questions explicitly deferred — a Korea-style armistice without a political settlement. This is precisely what the Korean precedent models and what Russia's demand structure permits, since a ceasefire-in-place requires Moscow to abandon nothing it holds while freezing Ukrainian gains. The risk, discussed in Chapter 5, is symmetric: the same defer-all-hard-questions design that makes signature feasible makes the instrument vulnerable to collapse, and roughly half of terminated conflicts recur within five years.

### The Comprehensive Settlement (5%): Why Full Packages Stay Rare

The base rate alone justifies skepticism: comprehensive settlements within two years of serious talks opening are rare in the interstate record, and this war's core-term structure has not moved in a year of trilateral contact. The territorial question is binary and insoluble on current terms: Zelensky's stated red line is no surrender of Donbas, while Russia's maximal aim remains full seizure of Donetsk Oblast — an offensive ISW has just judged operationally defeated, meaning Russia cannot take Donbas but will not legally relinquish its claim to it. Any settlement text must either recognize the annexations (unacceptable to Kyiv and to most of the coalition) or repudiate them (unacceptable to Moscow). Settlements resolve tractable bargains; this is not one.

Guarantee architecture compounds the problem. The three available models — NATO membership, bilateral/multilateral guarantees, or no guarantees — each break a different coalition link. NATO membership collides directly with Russia's permanent-ban demand and would require alliance unanimity among members who have just watched Russian drones strike commercial tankers in Romanian territorial waters without triggering an allied military response. Bilateral US guarantees are Washington's stated intent but depend on a single administration's continuity and a Congress already in Republican backlash over Russia policy, pushing binding sanctions legislation against the White House's waiver authority [S61]. European-only guarantees are Brussels' preference but would leave Kyiv exposed to the exact US reliability question that the Graham Act waivers have already raised. A comprehensive package must satisfy all three constraints simultaneously; the probability of convergence on all fronts within fourteen months is why this scenario is a tail.

### Market and Normative Payoffs Across the Three Paths

The three de-escalation outcomes differ sharply in what they deliver.

The normative stakes are equally asymmetric. A freeze without signature establishes the precedent that territorial gains by force can be consolidated de facto — an outcome Global South states can abstain on and that erodes the territorial-integrity norm without anyone voting to erode it. A signed armistice, by contrast, would at minimum stabilize the norm's formal shell even while deferring the sovereignty question, much as Korea's armistice froze a division without legitimizing conquest.

## Scenario Forecast III — Full-Scale Re-Intensification (12%) and the Upside-Risk Tail

### The 12% Assignment and Why It Is Not Lower

### Food Prices: The Muted Channel With a Fat Tail

The grain channel is the mirror image of the energy channel: in the base case it is quiet, but its tail is severe.

The second layer is duration-conditioned hazard. That base rate is misapplied here: this war is in its fifth year, well past the point where that unconditional hazard binds.

The third layer is case-specific friction on the negotiated path. Termination requires closing three gaps simultaneously — security guarantees, territorial status, and sanctions-relief sequencing — and the observed diplomatic record shows movement on goodwill bundles (Iran, fertiliser, detainee releases) but not on core terms.

**Confidence is stated as medium, with uncertainty bands.** The attrition and freeze scenarios each carry a subjective band of roughly ±8 percentage points; the signed-ceasefire branch ±6; intensification ±5; comprehensive settlement ±3. Two asymmetries deserve honesty.

### Per-Scenario Resolution Criteria
- **[35%] Attrition grinds on**: As of 2027-12-31: (a) no signed ceasefire/armistice instrument is in force; (b) verified combined monthly strike tempo (Shahed-type UAVs plus missiles) averages ≥3,000/month over Q3–Q4 2027; (c) no stable de facto ceasefire — ≥1,000 documented casualties/month (UK MoD or ISW estimates) sustained through H2 2027.

- **[17%] Formal ceasefire signed**: A ceasefire or armistice instrument signed by authorized Russian and Ukrainian representatives (verified by UN/OSCE registration or official gazette publication) enters into force on or before 2027-12-31 AND hostilities (documented casualties) fall below 200/month for the two consecutive months following entry into force AND no comprehensive settlement (territory + guarantees + sanctions relief) has simultaneously entered into force.
- **[12%] Full-scale war intensifies**: As of 2027-12-31: (a) any month in H2 2027 with Shahed-type UAV launches >8,161 (May 2026 peak) OR a Russian mobilization decree officially promulgated; AND (b) Russian net territorial gains >148 km²/month average over any 3-month window in 2027 (2x the 74 sq mi/month trailing average); AND (c) no ceasefire instrument in force.
- **[5%] Comprehensive peace settlement / other (residual)**: Scenario resolved TRUE if either: (a) a comprehensive settlement covering territorial terms, security guarantees and sanctions relief is signed and enters into force on or before 2027-12-31 (verified via UN registration and simultaneous US/EU sanctions-relief legal acts); OR (b) a documented change in Russian head of state or equivalent structural break occurs that fundamentally alters the war's framework before 2027-12-31 and none of the four other scenarios' criteria are met.
