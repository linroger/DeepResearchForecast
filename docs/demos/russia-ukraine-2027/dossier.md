# Forecast the state of the Russia-Ukraine war as of December 31, 2027, and its implications for the global economy, geopolitics, international relations, and…

## Executive Summary

Definitions used: the war's state is scored on the brief's continuum — from (a) full-scale active war at current or higher intensity through (b) reduced-intensity attritional conflict, (c) frozen conflict or de facto ceasefire and (d) formal armistice or ceasefire agreement to (e) comprehensive peace settlement — as an end-state holding on December 31, 2027, not merely touched earlier; a signed instrument counts only if in force on that date. "By" a date means on or before it; "on" a date means the state holds that day. Territory is settled by ISW control-of-terrain assessments [S1] and Russia Matters/DeepState compilations [S2], strike tempo by Ukrainian Air Force monthly summaries [S3], sanctions status by the European Commission [S4]; UCDP codes a de facto low-activity ending as fewer than 25 battle-deaths per year [S5].

Baseline, late October 2026: Russia holds about 45,196 square miles (~19% of Ukraine), including Crimea and pre-2022 Donbas [S2]; it net-gained 753 sq mi in the 12 months to October 19, 2026 but has net-lost ground since September 1, 2026, with ISW judging its Donetsk offensive "operationally defeated" [S1]. Strike tempo stays high — September 2026 saw 6,732 Shahed-type drones per Ukrainian Air Force monthly summaries [S3] — while belligerent loss totals are reported as claims, not findings. Diplomacy runs through a single US-mediated trilateral channel, restarted twice: after Putin's ninth in-person meeting with Steve Witkoff on October 21, 2026, the Kremlin agreed to resume technical-level talks, possibly in Abu Dhabi, from on or after November 10, 2026, on post-war security guarantees, with goodwill bundled with Iran and fertiliser items rather than movement on Ukraine terms [S6]. Markets already price the stress: Brent stood at 102.0181 U.S. dollars per barrel (September 2026) against 67.6091 a year earlier [S7], and ICE Dutch TTF Nov26 at 88.430 EUR/MWh on October 22, 2026 [S8].

Central thesis: neither side can impose its maximal aims by the horizon, but the gaps on territory, security guarantees and sanctions sequencing are too wide for a signed text in force by end-2027, and the termination literature finds fighting more often "simply ceases" than ends by agreement [S9]. Continued fighting carries 50% and some cessation 50%, with the modal state (b). These are our calibrated forecasts: no source publishes outcome probabilities and no options-implied price distribution appears in the evidence.

- Attrition grinds on — 35%: talks stall; the war continues at reduced but substantial intensity.
- Frozen frontline — 25%: a de facto ceasefire freezes the current line without a signed accord.
- Formal ceasefire signed — 20%: the trilateral track yields a ceasefire in force before end-2027, guarantees and sanctions relief unsettled.
- Full-scale war intensifies — 15%: negotiations collapse and Russia re-escalates with a new offensive.
- Comprehensive peace settlement — 5%: a full settlement with territorial terms, guarantees and sanctions relief in force by end-2027.

Leading indicators: whether technical-level trilateral talks open after November 10, 2026 and rise to political level [S6], which moves toward Formal ceasefire signed, with failure locking in Attrition grinds on; net territorial change, where continued Russian net losses [S1][S2] support Frozen frontline and renewed gains support Full-scale war intensifies; monthly Shahed tempo [S3], whose decline signals (b) or (c) and record highs signal (a); the Russian draft 2027 war budget's borrowing path and National Wealth Fund drawdowns, where forced monetization would flag fiscal strain; and US aid votes and EU sanctions rounds [S4], with any sharp Brent [S7] or TTF [S8] repricing on cessation news as market confirmation.

Over the twelve months to October 19, 2026 Russia net-gained 753 square miles, but momentum collapsed in the final month: 3 square miles in the four weeks to October 19 against 37 in the prior four, and a net loss of 20 square miles in the six days October 13–19 [S2]. ISW, counting 2026 only, assesses Ukraine liberated about 1,080 square kilometers since January 1, 2026 while Russia net-gained about 73 square kilometers, then net-lost about 73 square kilometers from September 1 [S1]; the trackers differ in windows and control thresholds, but both show the front shifting toward Ukraine since September [S2][S1]. ISW judges that Operation Vivaldi in the Lyman direction defeated the northern pincer of Russia's planned encirclement of Donetsk Oblast — Putin's stated objective since 2022 — leaving that offensive "operationally defeated" as of October 21, 2026 [S1].

On attrition, the only cumulative Western estimate in evidence is a February 2026 figure from a "highly-informed former high-ranking Western official" cited by Russia Matters: 1,000,000 Russian and 250,000–300,000 Ukrainian casualties, with no later update retrieved [S2]. Mediazona/Meduza's probate-based count, a lower bound covering deaths only, put Russian soldiers killed at 352,000 as of its May 2026 estimate [S12]. Russia Matters' analysis puts Russian losses at roughly 5,000–8,200 per week against recruitment of 5,500–8,000 — overlapping ranges that establish neither surplus nor deficit [S13]. Ukraine's General Staff, a belligerent source, claims about 1,570,540 Russian personnel losses as of October 21, 2026 (1,820 on October 20) plus 12,436 tanks, 51,403 artillery systems and 573,392 UAVs — far above independent figures [S14].

Strike tempo is high and rising on the drone axis: 6,732 Shahed-type UAVs in September 2026 (ISIS full-month reconstruction 7,400–7,700), now flown day and night [S3]; on the night of October 19 Russia used Iskander-M/S-400 and Iskander-K missiles with 269 strike drones, 59 jet-powered [S15]. Glide-bomb use hit a record 8,766 in August 2026 (~283/day) per Ukrainian General Staff data [S16]; September interception rates were 85.59% for drones and 69.84% for cruise missiles but 0% for ballistic missiles [S12]. Likely Russian drones struck four civilian vessels, two tankers in Romanian territorial waters, on October 15 [S17]; ISW calls Ukraine's October 7 strikes on Russian military and oil assets among its largest of the war [S18], and conditions Kyiv's initiative on Western support and Starlink/Starshield access, reportedly restored October 15 [S1][S17].

What would change the view: a second consecutive month of net Russian territorial loss [S2][S1]; whether the talks slated for on or after November 10, 2026 convene [S6]; Mediazona's next confirmed-killed update and OHCHR's October figures [S12][S10].

## Base rates of war termination

Definitions: "termination" is any cessation of interstate fighting — victory, peace agreement, ceasefire or armistice, or a de facto low-activity ending (UCDP codes low activity as fewer than 25 battle-deaths per year) [S5]; "by a date" means on or before that date; durability is read over five years, the recurrence literature's window; settling sources are the UCDP Conflict Termination dataset (1946–2005) and COW/IWB interstate lists.

### Base rates

| Reference class | n | Prior / finding | Source class |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Interstate wars, 1823–2003 | 97 wars (1,708 battles) [S21] | 79% ended no more than 2 years after onset — unconditional, onset-based [S22] | Peer-reviewed; universe per dataset abstract |
| Interstate wars ≥3 years since 1945 | ≈8–10 [S23] | ~20–40% end in any form within a following 2-year window — constructed, not published [S23] | Constructed from episode lists |
| All terminations, 1946–2005 | UCDP universe | Modal ending is fighting that "simply ceases," not victory or peace agreement [S24][S9] | Peer-reviewed, verified |
| Signed interstate ceasefires | 3 constructed cases [S23] | Korea armistice (27 July 1953, after more than 2 years of talks post-stalemate) held 70+ years; Iran–Iraq 1988 ceasefire held; Ethiopia–Eritrea 2000 held 20 years to 2020 [S25][S26][S23] | Constructed; Korea dates reported |
| Frozen lines | 2 constructed cases [S23] | Karabakh's 1994 ceasefire recurred in 2016, 2020, 2023; India–Pakistan 1971 war followed by 1999 Kargil [S23] | Constructed |

Fifty-five of 111 government–rebel dyads (49.5%) experienced conflict recurrence within five years of termination, per a UCDP-based study [S27].

That recurrence rate is intrastate: Kreutz excludes interstate conflicts from recurrence analysis for lack of post-Cold War interstate terminations, so published recurrence figures extrapolate weakly to this war; he also finds recurrence less likely after decisive victories or peacekeeper deployment [S9][S24]. On duration, Slantchev's interstate-war study (1816–1985) reports a median duration on the order of 11.2 months and duration-dependent termination hazards [S28]; a war in its fifth year sits in the tail where the unconditional 79% two-year figure overstates the hazard — a conditioning inference of this analysis, not the source's. The exact count — of interstate wars past three years, how many terminated within the following two years — exists in no published source accessed and must be built case-by-case from termination datasets [S23][S29]; the 20–40% band above is constructed, not a published statistic [S23]. The Korea precedent implies a multi-year negotiating lag between stalemate and signature [S25][S26]; the Stimson Center's assessment is qualitative — frozen conflict or ceasefire most likely, "potentially sooner than a decade," a negotiated armistice only over a longer horizon — and no source in the evidence publishes a numeric probability for any dated termination event [S30]. Without strong Western guarantees, comparative termination models judge ceasefire-type endings prone to recurrence or escalation [S31].

As a market cross-check, the stored official series show Brent Crude at 102.0181 U.S. dollars per barrel (World, 2026-09 monthly) [S7] and the Nominal Broad U.S. Dollar Index at 119.2152 (Jan 2006=100, 2026-09; latest daily 121.4048 on 2026-10-16) [S20] — levels embedding a geopolitical premium consistent with continuation; neither series publishes a termination probability, and Fitch's raised 2027 Brent assumption of USD70/barrel (from USD65, 22 September 2026) is a rating-case assumption of premium decay, not a probability [S32].

These references align with the canonical frame's priors: persistence past a two-year window (Attrition grinds on — 35%), de facto endings as the modal class (Frozen frontline — 25%), a Korea-style lag before signature (Formal ceasefire signed — 20%), rarity of full settlement within two years of talks (Comprehensive peace settlement — 5%), and residual re-escalation risk (Full-scale war intensifies — 15%).

What would change the view: an executed k-of-n count from IWB/UCDP termination dates replacing the constructed band [S21][S23]; publication of any dated numeric termination probability, of which the evidence currently contains none [S30]; verified evidence that Russian weekly losses exceed recruitment outside the overlapping bands [S13].

### Manpower

Personnel is Russia's binding constraint. Q1 2026 intake (~71,000, the weakest first quarter in three years, ~20% down year-on-year) ran near 70% of required pace despite record 1.47-million-rouble March bonuses, with covert coercion documented in at least 17 regions [S33][S36]; Medvedev's claim of 200,000 H1 contracts is hard to reconcile with budget data [S33]. Casualty estimates diverge by method: a former senior Western official estimated ~1,000,000 Russian and 250,000–300,000 Ukrainian killed and wounded by February 2026 [S2][S12]; Mediazona/Meduza's probate method sets a 352,000 lower bound on Russian dead by May 2026 [S12]; Ukraine's General Staff claims 51,403 artillery systems and 573,392 UAVs destroyed, 1,820 UAVs on October 20 alone [S14]. Russia Matters' weekly loss (5,000–8,200) and recruitment (5,500–8,000) ranges overlap [S13], and Western officials assessed a ~9,000 one-month shortfall in early 2026 [S37]. Ukraine's recruitment against replacement need is not established in public data.

### Production and aid

Estonian intelligence reports Russian output of ~7 million artillery shells, mortar rounds and rockets in 2025, a more-than-17-fold rise on 2021 [S34]; Ukraine's own shell output is unverified beyond an aggregator claim of 3 million-plus annually, though Kyiv and Brussels state a 2026 goal of matching Russia [S38][S39]. HUR estimates Russia builds ~4,000 jet-powered long-range drones and small cruise missiles monthly plus ~2,000 Geran-2s, up to 10,000 of all types [S40]; Ukraine tracks toward 4.5 million FPV drones in 2026 with 8–10 million capacity across ~500 firms — a category not comparable with Russian long-range output [S41][S42]. Sustainment stays Western: ISW conditions Ukraine's initiative on US aid, intelligence and Starlink/Starshield access, restored October 15, 2026 [S1][S17]; Kiel shows Europe the largest donor yet procuring ≥EUR 3 billion from US firms in H1 2026, within an EUR 90 billion 2026–27 EU loan with EUR 11 billion allocated May–June [S35].

## Russian war economy under sanctions

Definitions used: fiscal sustainability means Moscow funds the 2027 war budget through planned borrowing, tax rises and National Wealth Fund drawdowns at market rates, without forced monetization or debt distress; durability window is through December 31, 2027; settling sources are Russian Ministry of Finance data as relayed by Reuters and the Carnegie Endowment, plus the IMF/FRED price series.

### The 2027 budget

The draft 2027 budget allocates 17.1 trillion roubles (about $202.58 billion at $1 = 84.4113 roubles) to defence — ~27% above the 13.5 trillion originally budgeted for 2027, the highest since 2022, and 35% of federal spending — within a planned 50 trillion roubles for 2027–29 [S43][S44]. Actual outlays are classified, so published figures understate the war effort [S43][S44]. The 2026 budget was already stressed: the deficit was raised to 3.2% of GDP from 1.6%, spending lifted 13.2% to 48.6 trillion roubles (20.9% of GDP), and the oil and gas revenue estimate cut to 7.6 trillion from 8.9 trillion roubles [S43].

### Financing strain

Cover is increasingly expensive. Net borrowing rises 26% to 5 trillion roubles in 2026 plus a 459-billion-rouble NWF drawdown (~11% of liquid assets), and 43% to 7.7 trillion roubles in 2027; state debt reaches 21.7% of GDP in 2027 from 19.9%, past the ~20% authorities deem safe [S43]. A metals-and-mining windfall tax is expected to add ~200 billion roubles annually from 2027 [S43]. Carnegie notes the fiscal rule's optimistic $53/bbl cutoff (VEB baseline $46, stress $41) has the ministry buying FX for the NWF while borrowing at 14–15%, costing ~45 billion roubles a year [S44].

### Oil revenues and the cap

A Reuters calculation puts October 2026 federal oil and gas tax revenue near 1 trillion roubles (~$11.97 billion), up 10% year-on-year [S45]; Ukraine's foreign intelligence counts $57.97 billion for January–August 2026, 16.7% lower year-on-year — a period-scope difference, both from MinFin data [S46]. The IMF world series shows Brent Crude at 102.0181 U.S. dollars per barrel in September 2026, against 67.6091 in September 2025 [S7], keeping revenues buoyant despite the cap; the EU natural gas price at 17.93 U.S. dollars per million BTU in July 2026, from 9.46 in December 2025 [S19], carries the same premium. Current price-cap enforcement statistics and Urals discount levels are unknown, so shadow-fleet effects on revenue cannot be quantified.

No source publishes a probability for Russian sovereign default or forced monetization; fiscal-risk assessments here are qualitative. On these numbers the war economy is financed but degrading, with recruitment economics (record 1.47-million-rouble bonuses [S33]) and 14–15% borrowing costs as the real constraints through 2027.

What would change the view: Brent settling below the $53/bbl cutoff toward VEB's $46 baseline [S44][S7]; signs of NWF liquid exhaustion after the 459-billion-rouble drawdown [S43]; windfall-tax yields undershooting ~200 billion roubles [S43]; coalition enforcement action narrowing the Urals discount.

Growth has slowed each year since 2023, and the IMF's July 2026 projection of 1.0% sits below its own February range of 1.8–2.5%, with independent economists' median cut to 1.1% — a decelerating, war-dragged economy rather than a collapsing one [S47][S48][S49]. The nominal upgrade to UAH 10,193 billion rests on a deflator above 10.5%, i.e. prices rather than real activity [S50]. Donors finance this amid war-driven energy stress: EU natural gas averaged 17.93 U.S. Dollars per Million Metric British Thermal Unit in July 2026, up from 11.4706 in July 2025 [S19], and Brent averaged 102.0181 U.S. dollars per barrel in September 2026 against 67.6091 a year earlier [S7] — pressure on the European budgets supplying 80% of external aid [S51].

Dependence is structural: the €90 billion EU loan covers roughly two-thirds of 2026–27 needs, leaving a €70 billion 2027 gap that non-EU partners (expected €45 billion) are slow to fill; the UK has committed £25 billion cumulatively and Canada the equivalent of €5.4 billion military plus €8.2 billion financial assistance [S51]. Kiel data show military allocations holding up while financial and humanitarian aid runs 41% below 2025's monthly average — budget finance is the binding constraint [S35]. Terms remain costly (15.16% one-year domestic yield; 16.00% policy rate) [S53][S54], and Ukrenergo completed an $825 million note restructuring in October 2026 [S56]; against annual aid flows, the World Bank's $588 billion reconstruction estimate implies reconstruction cannot scale while war continues [S52].

What would change the view: completion of future EFF reviews with revised growth; an EU decision closing the €70 billion gap; arrival of the partners' €45 billion; recovery of financial/humanitarian shares in Kiel updates; NBU rate cuts signalling stabilisation.

## Diplomacy and negotiation tracks

All ceasefire diplomacy runs through a single US-mediated trilateral channel, now restarted twice. On October 21, 2026, after Putin's ninth in-person meeting with US envoy Steve Witkoff at the Constantine Palace in St Petersburg, the Kremlin agreed to resume trilateral talks with Ukraine at technical level, possibly in Abu Dhabi, from on or after November 10, 2026, focused on post-war security guarantees for both Ukraine and Russia [S6]. The accompanying goodwill — release of the retired US teacher Stephen Hubbard — was bundled with offers of help on Iran and fertiliser supplies, not with movement on Ukraine terms [S6].

## Scenarios for end-2027

### Scenario Probability Distribution
- **Attrition grinds on (35% probability):** Talks stall over territory and sanctions sequencing and the war continues at reduced but substantial intensity through 2027; prior: most wars of this duration persist past a 2-year window.
- **Frozen frontline (25% probability):** A de facto ceasefire freezes the current line without a signed accord as both sides exhaust offensive capacity and the US presses Kyiv; prior: frozen-conflict terminations are common in post-Soviet wars.
- **Formal ceasefire signed (20% probability):** The trilateral track produces a signed ceasefire or armistice in force before end-2027, with security guarantees and sanctions relief still unsettled.
- **Full-scale war intensifies (15% probability):** Negotiations collapse and Russia re-escalates with a new offensive, returning the war to current or higher intensity.
- **Comprehensive peace settlement (5% probability):** A full settlement with territorial terms, security guarantees and sanctions relief enters into force by end-2027; prior: comprehensive settlements within 2 years of talks are rare.

### Attrition grinds on
Talks agreed October 21, 2026 to resume on or after November 10, 2026 [S6] stall where all prior rounds stalled: ceasefire, territory, Zaporizhzhia NPP, guarantees [S64]. Russia's reported demands — sharp Ukrainian force cuts and a permanent NATO-membership ban [S57] — do not intersect US-assured "strong security guarantees" [S65]. Territory stays near ~19%, moving within a band on the order of the past year's 753-sq-mi shift [S2]; UAV tempo runs in the thousands per month below the May 2026 peak [S3].

### Frozen frontline
Mutual exhaustion without paper: Russian contract recruitment at roughly 70% of required pace (about 71,000 in Q1 2026 against a ~409,000 annual target) [S33], Russia net-losing since September 2026 [S1], and Zelensky's standing offer to freeze the line [S1]. Modal terminations in the UCDP class are those where "fighting simply ceases" [S24]. The line freezes near the current ~45,196 sq mi Russian-held [S2]; strike tempo collapses to sporadic.

### Formal ceasefire signed
The Witkoff channel — Putin's ninth meeting, a detainee release as goodwill [S6] — converts to signature with guarantees deferred, per the reported US-European four-point plan [S66] (a mediator's outline, not a belligerent aim). The Korea precedent warns on timing: armistice only on 27 July 1953 after more than two years of talks [S25]. Line as at signature; tempo near zero with violations. A signed-then-collapsed ceasefire still resolves this ever-touch scenario yes while the end-state migrates to (a)/(b).

### Full-scale war intensifies
Talks collapse and Russia regenerates capacity: roughly 7 million shells produced in 2025 per Estonian intelligence [S34]; about 4,000 jet-powered long-range drones and small cruise missiles per month per HUR estimate [S40]; China's $10.3 billion in common-high-priority exports, "the decisive external enabler" [S63]. Requires reversing the net-loss trend [S1] and sustaining UAV tempo above 8,161/month [S3].

### Comprehensive peace settlement
Requires closing territory, guarantees, sanctions relief and reconstruction at once [S66], against Stimson's judgement that a negotiated armistice is plausible only over a longer horizon [S30] and the rarity of decisive endings in long wars [S24]. Sequencing is the chokepoint: US waivers of the Graham Act were called "regrettable" by the Commission [S4]. Treaty-set line; near-zero tempo.

## Geopolitical and normative implications

Operational test: events "by 31 December 2027" resolve on or before that date; a posture "at end-2027" holds on that date; durability is judged across the roughly 14 months to the horizon, with ISW/Russia Matters assessments and official EU/US records as settling sources [S1][S2][S4].

### NATO posture and security guarantees

The core unresolved trade is Russia's reported demand that Ukraine drastically shrink its military and pledge never to join NATO [S57] against US assurances, relayed by Zelenskyy on October 20, 2026, of strong security guarantees [S65]. Escalation touching allied territory is no longer hypothetical: on October 15, 2026 likely Russian drones struck two commercial tankers in Romanian territorial waters [S17]. Because the EU already provides 80% of Ukraine's external aid via a €90 billion 2026–27 loan [S51], under Attrition grinds on (35%) European support hardens into a de facto guarantee without treaty form; under Frozen frontline (25%) or Formal ceasefire signed (20%) the guarantee architecture remains unsettled at end-2027 by the frame's own theses, leaving NATO enlargement off the table and bilateral/European arrangements the operative fallback. Full-scale war intensifies (15%) would push NATO from aid-provider toward alliance-threshold decisions after strikes on allied waters [S17].

### US-China-Russia triangle and sanctions evolution

Moscow is bundling: at Putin's ninth in-person meeting with Steve Witkoff on October 21, 2026, he offered US cooperation on Iran and fertiliser and raised a possible Trump–Xi meeting [S6]. China remains, in the US-China Economic and Security Review Commission's August 2026 assessment, "the decisive external enabler of Russia's war in Ukraine," having exported $10.3 billion worth of Common High Priority List items, trained Russian personnel on drone operations, supplied drone engines, reportedly deployed a Silent Hunter laser system, and provided satellite imagery supporting strike targeting [S63]. Washington has begun paying for engagement: after formally adopting the Graham Act in September 2026, it waived the act's principal sanctions and tariffs — which the European Commission called "regrettable" [S4] — and, per reporting, waived diesel-deal sanctions through April 2027 [S62]. The coalition is thus splitting between a US trading relief for engagement, constrained by a Republican backlash pursuing binding sanctions legislation [S61], and an EU still adopting rounds — over 30 between April 2025 and September 2026, which the Commission says deprived Russia of over €1 trillion [S4] — making relief sequencing the hinge of any settlement [S67].

### Nuclear signaling and territorial-integrity norms

The evidence base contains no primary reporting on nuclear signaling in late 2026, so that channel is unknown here. Normatively, a reported US-European four-point peace plan envisages territorial concessions from both Ukraine and Russia [S66]; ratifying them would write force-acquired control of about 19% of Ukraine [S2] into a negotiated text. Case evidence suggests interstate ceasefires can hold for decades (Korea 1953, Iran-Iraq 1988) [S23], but the literature finding that roughly half of terminated conflicts recur within five years [S72] implies guarantees — not signatures — determine whether a frozen line repairs or further erodes the territorial-integrity norm [S31]. Comprehensive peace settlement (5%) remains the frame's tail outcome on exactly this basis.
