# Forecast the outcome of France budgetary and political crisis through the first round of the presidential election in April 2027, explicitly incorporating the…

## Executive Summary

France enters the final six months before the presidential first round scheduled for 18 April 2027 [S1] with a minority government, a contested 2027 budget and the aftermath of the October 2026 riots. The central thesis: the modal path is muddle-through, not rupture. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu, in office since 8 September 2025 [S2][S3], presented the PLF 2027 on 1 October 2026 [S4] into an Assembly where, since the 2024 snap election, budgets have passed only through Article 49.3, and censure after a 49.3 voids the bill [S5]; three prime ministers — Attal, Bayrou, Lecornu — have cycled through office in 2024–26 [S2][S6]. On this structure, the most likely resolution is enactment of the loi de finances before 31 December 2026 via 49.3, a survived censure, an election held on schedule and Rassemblement National qualifying first — with the risks skewed toward escalation rather than stabilisation.

The fiscal margin is thin. The government's revised 2026 deficit forecast is 5.4 percent of GDP [S4]; the Commission's Spring 2026 forecast is 5.1 percent of GDP for 2026, "before edging up to 5.7% in 2027" [S7]; and the IMF projects net general government borrowing of -5.352% of GDP for 2026 — a publisher projection, not an observation [S8]. Every 2026 figure sits below the 5.5 percent resolution line, so exceedance requires fresh slippage, with no cyclical cushion from French growth of 0.841 percent in 2025 [S9] against a publisher-projected euro-area 1.2 percent for 2026 [S10], and with PLF 2027 already carrying interest charges rising to €91.2 billion in 2027 from €79.2 billion [S4]. Markets price the fragility: French 10-year yields trade above Italy's and Greece's, the widest premium since the early-2010s crisis [S11], though still well short of the aggregator's all-time high of 225.1 bps on 17 November 2011 [S12].

Public order is the swing variable. Around 3,000 shops were looted, per Justice Minister Darmanin [S13], with at least 3,000 damaged, double the prior day's count [S14], before Lecornu credited "targeted curfews" with restoring calm while Macron urged calm without declaring a state of emergency [S15][S13]. Recurrence before April 2027 drives every adverse scenario, because security salience compounds the lead Le Pen already holds at 32.5–38% in first-round voting-intention polls under differing candidate scenarios — polls, not forecasts [S16][S1][S17]. Legally, the election is hard to move: the runoff is fixed for 2 May 2027 [S1], and French law offers no routine mechanism to shift these dates, which are set by organic law and decree rather than the emergency regimes [S18].

The canonical scenarios:

- Muddle-through austerity — 40% — the budget passes via 49.3, censure is survived narrowly, riots de-escalate, the deficit lands near 5–5.5 percent and the election proceeds on schedule with RN qualifying first.
- Escalation and paralysis — 25% — recurring riots force emergency measures, censure or resignation produces a caretaker technocratic cabinet, the budget slips past 31 December 2026, spreads widen sharply and RN gains further.
- Censure and left surge — 20% — a budget censure topples the government, instability shifts the race toward the left-wing coalition and the deficit exceeds 5.5 percent.
- Stabilisation rebound — 15% — unrest fades, a cross-party budget is enacted without 49.3, the spread tightens and Macron-affiliated and centre-right prospects rise into the runoff.

Leading indicators: the resumption of the solemn first-part vote on the PLF 2027, postponed from 20 October with more than 2,800 amendments pending [S19][S20][S21]; any 49.3 engagement and the ensuing censure motion [S5]; new insurer catastrophe declarations and curfew extensions [S13][S14]; the OAT-Bund spread against its 225.1 bps record [S12]; polling-average shifts for Le Pen, the left and the centre [S16][S1][S17]; and deficit revisions from INSEE, the Cour des comptes and the Commission against the 5.4 percent forecast [S4][S7], plus rating-agency actions on the sovereign and French banks.

## Fiscal and political baseline

Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu's minority government presented the PLF 2027 to the Conseil des ministres on 1 October 2026, carried by Economy and Finance Minister Roland Lescure [S4][S22]. Since the 2024 snap election produced a hung parliament, successive governments have passed budgets only by engaging Article 49.3, and censure after a 49.3 voids the bill [S5]. The dossier projects a state deficit of €156.4 billion in 2027, €19.2 billion worse than 2026, as interest charges rise to €91.2 billion from €79.2 billion [S4].

### Where the PLF 2027 stands

The finance commission rejected the revenue part on 9 October 2026 by 31 votes to 3, with 2 abstentions [S23]. Floor debate opened on 13 October on the government's original text, commission amendments being indicative only [S24]; the solemn first-part vote was fixed for 20 October and the whole-budget vote for 17 November [S19][S25]. Le Monde reported the first-part vote postponed, with no guarantee of adoption absent a majority [S21], and LCP expects it to "more than probably" end in rejection, sending the text to the Senate in that state; LCP reports only that Lecornu "could" resort to 49.3, with no formal engagement as of 21 October [S26]. Budget Minister David Amiel warned that Assembly-approved amendments would worsen the deficit by €10 billion [S5]. The 2026 loi de finances was itself adopted only on 2 February 2026 [S27]. If nothing passes, Reuters outlines an emergency "special law" rolling over the 2026 budget, or the never-used ordinance "nuclear option", which legal experts expect would trigger censure [S5].

### Minority arithmetic

Censure requires 289 of 577 deputies, favourable votes only [S28]. The RN group held 122 members at the 6 July 2026 vote [S29]; the presidential bloc, renamed Ensemble pour la République, publishes a composition page dated 19 October 2026, but no verified seat count for it — nor a documented Les Républicains line on the PLF — exists in the record [S30]. The blocking minority is established; a pro-budget majority is not.

### Base rates and the pivotal bloc

The Assembly's official list records, as reported, a single adoption since 1958 — against the Pompidou government in 1962 [S39][S44]. That near-zero base rate accumulated under majority government; in the minority era the operative test is narrower. The left's high-water mark, 269 votes on 23 January 2026, fell 20 short, with the Socialist Party abstaining, per BFM TV and Radio France [S40][S41]. The PS is therefore the pivot — roughly a 20-vote shift toward censure topples the cabinet — and its pre-election positioning is undocumented in the record.

The RN's 122-member group [S29] has never invested itself in censure: its motions drew 140 and 142 votes [S28][S40], and the July roll-call shows support came essentially from the left [S29]. With its candidate at 32.5–38% in mid-October first-round polls [S16][S1], the RN's revealed preference is to wait for April 2027 rather than govern through a censure-borne majority; a budget it deems punitive is the main falsifier. No motion appears in the record after 6 July 2026, and no 49.3 engagement on the PLF 2027 had been declared as of 21 October [S26] — the likeliest trigger runs through the 17 November whole-budget vote [S25].

### Technocratic alternatives

A technocratic cabinet is a residual, not a plan. Reuters' analysis holds that past mid-December a budget passed by ordinance — never attempted under the Fifth Republic — would survive a later fall of government, and that President Macron would likely struggle to install any successor before the election, leaving Lecornu's cabinet in a caretaker role [S5]; censure after a 49.3, by contrast, voids the bill and forces a fresh appointment sequence [S5]. The macro backdrop stiffens every bloc's refusal to inherit austerity: euro-area growth of 0.7% per annum in 2024 (publisher estimate), with publisher projections of 1% for 2025 and 1.2% for 2026 [S10]. In the canonical frame, Muddle-through austerity — 40% — prices narrow survival; Censure and left surge — 20% — has the budget motion topple the government; Escalation and paralysis — 25% — produces a caretaker technocratic cabinet. Signposts: 49.3 engagement on the PLF or PLFSS, any PS declaration on censure, LR votes on the first-part text, and each motion's tally against the 269-vote high-water mark [S26][S25][S28].

## October riots: scale and costs

### Casualties and arrests
Violence swept French cities over the weekend of 17–19 October 2026, in what Reuters described as France's worst unrest in years [S15]. Casualty counts conflict: CNN reports at least two deaths from the weekend violence, with serious injuries including two people who lost their hands [S13], while the Interior Ministry tally relayed by Reuters counts a single protester dead after a cardiac arrest during the Paris demonstrations of Saturday 17 October; the one-death figure is unconfirmed [S15][S45]. Reuters also relayed interior-ministry figures of about 2,400 arrests across France and nearly 1,000 injured police officers, which remain unconfirmed [S15][S45]. An interior-ministry estimate of roughly 550,000 participants on 17 October circulates via social media and is unverified [S46].

### Commercial damage
Justice Minister Gérald Darmanin said around 3,000 shops were looted across the country as of Tuesday 20 October [S13]; Euronews likewise reports at least 3,000 shops damaged, double the count of a day earlier [S14]. Carrefour CEO Alexandre Bompard said 50 of the retailer's shops were damaged, including 20 looted, with losses "running into the tens of millions of euros" [S15]. The government's roughly 3,000-shop tally already exceeds the ~2,500 vandalised businesses CCI France counted in the June–July 2023 riots [S14].

### Public-order measures
Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu credited the "targeted curfews" with restoring calm and admitted intelligence failures; President Emmanuel Macron urged calm but did not declare a state of emergency as of 18–19 October [S15][S13]. Localised overnight curfews were reported from 19 October in parts of Paris (from 23:00 around Place de la République and Les Halles), Bordeaux, Lille and the northern towns of Wattignies, Douai and Denain (from 18:00); these municipal details are unconfirmed [S13][S47].

### Insured losses
France Assureurs said on 20 October it could not yet provide an initial loss estimate or claims count [S14]. Morningstar's 20 October estimate expects total insured losses to remain below EUR 1.2 billion, assuming no further widespread riots, and judges the event not to be a capital event for major French insurers — a third-party estimate, not an industry figure [S48]. Press-relayed figures span roughly $700 million (an FT figure relayed via a secondary source) to below €1.1 billion [S49][S50]. Claims fall on ordinary policies and insurers' riot guarantees because the mandatory, state-backed riot-insurance mechanism created by the 2026 budget law was not yet operational when the riots struck [S14][S48]; no catastrophe declaration or activation trigger has been documented. For scale, the June–July 2023 Nahel riots ultimately cost France Assureurs EUR 730 million on ~15,600 claims, revised to EUR 793 million on ~17,000 claims [S48][S14] — early figures in past episodes moved up as claims matured, so October 2026 estimates are best read as floors. The bill lands on an economy that grew 0.841% in 2025, after 1.19% in 2024 (World Bank, GDP annual % growth, France; 2025 stored observation) [S9].

## Unrest recurrence base rates

### Reference class of severe episodes
France has recorded five severe nationwide or territorial riot episodes in about 21 years, four of them metropolitan (2005, 2018–19, 2023, 2026) plus New Caledonia in 2024 [S48][S14]. Severity per episode is rising and the metropolitan recurrence interval has shortened — from about 13 years (2005 to 2018–19) to about 3 years (2023 to 2026). On this list, three of the four episodes preceding 2026 were themselves followed by another severe episode within five years; the sample is small (three closed transitions) and cannot be read as a probability, but it rules out comfort from long lulls. Composition matters for the catastrophe criterion: in 2023, commercial property was ~65% of insured cost and municipal property ~27% [S48], with average claims of ~€46,600 [S14].

### Emergency-measure precedents
France's four emergency frameworks — Article 16, the Article 36 state of siege, the 1955 state of emergency and the 2020 sanitary state of emergency — have been used in 1955, 1958, 1961, 1988, 2005, 2015 and 2017 [S18]. The November 2015 state of emergency, in force until 31 October 2017 [S18], spanned the 2017 presidential election in which Marine Le Pen reached the runoff [S1] — precedent that voting can proceed under emergency policing powers. Curfews, the lower rung of response, were already imposed in October 2026 [S15][S13], and AFP reported Prime Minister Lecornu saying on 19 October that a state of emergency could be declared "if needed" [S51].

### Catastrophe-declaration channel
The insurance trigger for judging recurrence is unsettled: the state-backed riot-insurance mechanism created by the 2026 budget law was not operational when October's riots hit, claims run through ordinary riot guarantees, and no catastrophe declaration or activation rule has been documented [S14][S48].

### Read-through to April 2027
Recurrence risk is fed by a weak economy: France's GDP grew 0.841% in 2025 after 1.19% in 2024 (World Bank annual % growth series) [S9]. Renewed large-scale rioting before the first round scheduled for 18 April 2027 [S1] — an insurance-declared catastrophe event, renewed curfews or emergency measures — is the signature of Escalation and paralysis — 25%; a de-escalated winter fits Muddle-through austerity — 40%. Signposts: the weekend of 24–25 October 2026 (calm or renewed violence); France Assureurs' first claims count and loss estimate; any catastrophe declaration or activation of the new mechanism; extension or lifting of municipal curfews; any Council-of-Ministers decision under the 1955 Act.

## Riots and electoral shifts

The October 2026 riots — around 3,000 shops looted per Justice Minister Darmanin [S13], at least 3,000 damaged, double the prior day's count [S14] — landed on a race Le Pen already led at 32.5–38% [S16][S1][S17]. Prime Minister Lecornu credited "targeted curfews" with restoring calm, while Macron urged calm without declaring a state of emergency [S15][S13].

### The immediate polling signal

The first post-riot poll, Ifop-Fiducial, fielded from Saturday 17 October evening to Monday 19 October, shows Le Pen at 34–38% across scenarios with "little change" versus before the riots, per POLITICO; these figures are unconfirmed here [S57]. The government's attempt to blame Mélenchon for stoking the 17 October violence did not move his voting intentions, and Paris prosecutor Laure Beccuau said she could not confirm the government's account from arrestee profiles [S57] (unconfirmed). No polling yet measures order-and-security salience itself [S57].

### Salience, turnout and bloc shifts

Order-and-security salience plays to RN's framing: in Escalation and paralysis — 25% — recurring riots and emergency measures boost RN further. De-escalation plus a cross-party budget deal instead lift Macron-affiliated and centre-right prospects into the runoff in Stabilisation rebound — 15%, while budget-driven instability shifts the race toward the left-wing coalition in Censure and left surge — 20%.

Recurrence base rates frame the exposure: four severe riot episodes in roughly 21 years — 2005 (≈EUR 200 million insured), 2018–19 gilets jaunes (≈EUR 220 million), 2023 Nahel riots (EUR 730 million per France Assureurs, or EUR 793 million and ~17,000 claims per the later tally, with a Senate inquiry near EUR 1 billion) — with intervals shortening from ~13 years (2005→2018) to ~3 years (2023→2026) [S48][S14]. The government's ~3,000 looted shops already exceed CCI France's ~2,500 vandalised businesses in 2023 [S14]. Morningstar expects 2026 insured losses below EUR 1.2 billion assuming no further widespread riots — a publisher estimate, unconfirmed [S48]. The backdrop stays weak: French GDP growth was 0.841% in 2025, down from 1.19% in 2024 [S9], sustaining cost-of-living grievances.

Two election-relevant unknowns remain. No abstention-intention figures for 2027 exist in the evidence [S16][S1][S57], yet runoff composition hinges on differential turnout among RN, left and centrist electorates; and prior episodes' vote-shift effects are not quantified in the gathered evidence, so salience effects are inferred from the frame rather than observed. Centrist fragmentation compounds exposure: Philippe fell to 12% from 15% in September while Attal polls 7% [S17], and Le Pen has lost both runoffs she contested, in 2017 and 2022 [S1] — runoff consolidation, not first-round strength, is her binding constraint.

## Legal constraints on the election

The first round of the presidential election is scheduled for 18 April 2027, with the runoff on 2 May 2027 [S1], and the Constitution fixes the second ballot on the fourteenth day following the first [S52][S53]. French law offers no routine mechanism to move these dates; timing is set by organic law and decree rather than by the emergency regimes [S18].

### Four emergency regimes, none of which reschedules elections

| Emergency regime | Legal basis | Preconditions and powers | Effect on the electoral calendar |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exceptional presidential powers | Article 16, Constitution | "Serious and immediate" threat to the institutions of the Republic, the nation's independence, territorial integrity or international commitments, with the regular functioning of the constitutional public authorities interrupted; the Constitutional Council is consulted and Parliament meets as of right [S18] | Contains no provision suspending or rescheduling elections; never used to move one [S18][S58] |
| State of siege | Article 36, Constitution | Declared in Council of Ministers when the integrity of the national territory is threatened or an imminent danger results from foreign war or armed insurrection; police powers pass to the army and military courts may function [S18][S59] | A far higher bar than the 1955 regime; no election provision [S18] |
| State of emergency | Act of 3 April 1955 | Declared by the President; permits house arrests, searches and administrative closures; reported to require parliamentary approval with 12-day renewable durations [S18] | Policing powers only; elections have been held while it was in force [S18] |
| Sanitary state of emergency | Act of 23 March 2020 | Measures taken by the Prime Minister [S18] | The 2020 municipal runoff was moved only by a separate statute [S58] |

### The only routes to postponement

Three paths exist, all exceptional. First, Article 7 of the Constitution: if a candidate dies or becomes incapacitated before the first round, the Constitutional Council declares the election postponed — the only explicitly constitutional postponement route [S52]. Second, a specific statute: the March 2020 municipal runoff was pushed from 22 March to 28 June 2020 by the emergency law of 23 March 2020, an ordinary statute amending the electoral code, not the 1955 regime [S58]. Third, Article 16 measures — a route never used to move an election under the Fifth Republic and widely considered legally possible only if constitutional institutions were genuinely interrupted [S18][S58].

### Precedents point to voting under emergency rule

The four regimes have been used in 1955, 1958, 1961, 1988, 2005, 2015 and 2017 [S18]. France held the 2015 regional elections and the April–May 2017 presidential rounds while the November 2015 state of emergency remained in force, until 31 October 2017 [S18] — the closest analogue to a riot-clouded 2027 vote. The operative precedent is therefore an election held under emergency policing powers, not a postponed one.

### Current posture and falsifiers

As of 18–19 October 2026, President Macron urged calm but did not declare a state of emergency, while Prime Minister Lecornu credited "targeted curfews" with restoring calm [S15][S13]; Lecornu additionally said, per an AFP report, that a state of emergency could be imposed "if needed" [S51]. Under "Escalation and paralysis — 25%", recurring riots force emergency measures, but on these precedents the legal default remains an election held on schedule under such measures, as in 2017. The falsifier to watch is any invocation of Article 16 or a bespoke postponement statute touching the 18 April date — neither has precedent in the Fifth Republic.

## Fiscal path resolution

The projet de loi de finances pour 2027 (PLF 2027) was presented to the Conseil des ministres on 1 October 2026 under Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu [S4]. Passage is structurally hard: the finance commission rejected the revenue part on 9 October by 31 votes to 3, with 2 abstentions, per Legiwatch [S23]; the solemn first-part vote, calendared for 20 October, was postponed with more than 2,800 amendments still pending, per LCP and Le Monde [S19][S20][S21]; Budget Minister David Amiel warned that amendments already approved in the Assembly would worsen the deficit by €10 billion [S5]; and the 2026 budget itself was adopted only on 2 February 2026, per touteleurope — a precedent for slippage [S27].

### Resolution criteria

1. **Enacted before 31 December 2026**: YES only if the PLF 2027 is definitively adopted — by explicit vote or by an engagement of responsibility under Article 49.3 that survives censure — and promulgated, observable as publication in the Journal officiel; otherwise NO, leaving the special-law or ordinance fallbacks [S5].
2. **Via Article 49.3**: YES if the Prime Minister engages 49.3 on the PLF (whole text or a part) and the text stands; censure requires an absolute majority of 289 of 577 deputies, only favourable votes counted [S28].
3. **2026 deficit above 5.5% of GDP**: resolved by INSEE's first full-year 2026 national-accounts estimate, due in spring 2027 [S38]. Every published baseline — 5.4% (government) [S4][S31], 5.1% (Commission) [S7], −5.352% (IMF projection) [S8] — sits below 5.5%; exceedance implies roughly 0.1 percentage point of slippage against the government's own figure, plausible with riot-related emergency spending but not the central path.

The canonical frame maps directly onto these criteria: under Muddle-through austerity — 40% — the budget passes via 49.3, censure is survived narrowly and the deficit lands near 5–5.5 percent; under Escalation and paralysis — 25% — the budget slips past 31 December 2026; under Censure and left surge — 20% — consolidation stalls and the deficit exceeds 5.5 percent; under Stabilisation rebound — 15% — a cross-party budget deal is enacted without 49.3.

Signposts before resolution: Moody's review of the Aa3 (negative) sovereign rating on 23 October 2026 [S66]; the Commission's Autumn 2026 forecast, unreleased as of 21 October, where France already sits among member states under the Excessive Deficit Procedure [S37][S36]; the socialist abstention margin — the left's 27 January 2026 censure motion reached 267 of the 289 votes required [S28]; and INSEE's quarterly debt releases, which put end-Q2 2026 Maastricht debt at €3,595.5 bn, 119.0% of GDP [S33].

## Scenario probabilities

### Scenario Probability Distribution
- **Muddle-through austerity (40% probability):** The minority government passes the 2027 budget via 49.3, survives censure narrowly, de-escalation follows the riots, the deficit lands near 5-5.5 percent and the election proceeds on schedule with RN qualifying first.
- **Escalation and paralysis (25% probability):** Recurring riots force emergency measures, censeure or resignation produces a caretaker technocratic cabinet, the budget slips past 31 December 2026, spreads widen sharply and security salience boosts RN further.
- **Censure and left surge (20% probability):** A censure motion over the budget topples the government, new instability shifts the race toward the left-wing coalition, fiscal consolidation stalls and the deficit exceeds 5.5 percent.
- **Stabilisation rebound (15% probability):** Markets and polling recover as unrest fades and a cross-party budget deal is enacted without 49.3, tightening the spread and lifting Macron-affiliated and centre-right prospects into the runoff.

The frame rests on two base rates against a thin fiscal margin. The government's revised 2026 deficit forecast is 5.4 percent of GDP [S4], the Commission's Spring 2026 forecast 5.1 percent [S7], and the IMF projection −5.352 percent [S8] — all below the 5.5 percent line, so exceedance requires fresh slippage, with no cyclical cushion from French growth of 0.841 percent in 2025 [S9] against a publisher-projected euro-area 1.2 percent for 2026 [S10].

### Scenario analysis

Muddle-through austerity — 40% extends the January 2026 playbook: 49.3 on the PLF 2027, censure fails on renewed PS abstention (the left's best tally: 269 of 289 [S28][S40]), and riots de-escalate as after the targeted curfews credited with restoring calm [S15]. Every budget since the 2024 hung parliament has used 49.3 [S5]; the 2026 law passed only on 2 February 2026 [S27]. Le Pen's 32.5–38 percent first-round polling underwrites the RN-first leg [S16][S1][S17]. Moves up on a rejected first-part vote followed by 49.3 [S19][S26]; down if the PS converts or riots recur. Consistent.

Escalation and paralysis — 25% rides the unrest class: 3 of 4 severe episodes recurred within five years, at a shrinking interval near three years [S48][S14]. Roughly 3,000 looted shops already exceed the ~2,500 businesses vandalised in 2023 [S14]; the PM has said a state of emergency could come "if needed" [S51]. Slippage has precedent [S27]; the ordinance option ends in a caretaker cabinet [S5]; the spread's one-year high of 150.8 bps [S12] widens on recurrence, though Morningstar's below-EUR 1.2 billion insured-loss cap assumes no further riots [S48] and the first post-riot poll reportedly shows little immediate shift in Le Pen's support [S57]. Consistent.

Censure and left surge — 20% must break the strongest anchor — one adoption since 1958 [S39] — but January's 20-vote gap [S28][S40] marks the bridge: the PS censures a budget it once tolerated after the 2026 target was softened to 5.0 percent [S34][S28]. Mélenchon polls second at 15–18.5 percent [S16][S1]; the fiscal leg echoes the Commission's unchanged-policy 5.7 percent for 2027 [S7], though 2026 must slip past 5.4 percent [S4]. RN motions drew only 140–142 votes [S28][S40], so the left must win alone; falsifier: renewed PS abstention. Consistent.

Stabilisation rebound — 15% is the thinnest branch: no budget has passed this Assembly without 49.3 [S5]; the finance commission rejected the revenue part 31–3 [S23] and the floor unravelled flagship articles [S26]. It needs LR votes, fading unrest, and centre reunification between Philippe (12–17 percent) and Attal (7–11 percent) [S16][S17]. Evidence leans, if anything, lower.
