# Forecast the global economic, macro-financial and geopolitical consequences of the Russia-Ukraine war through December 31, 2027, with the war's end-state as…

## Executive Summary

Definitions. The forecast object is the war state holding on 31 December 2027: "by" a date means on or before it, "on" a date means the state holds then. Intensity is read from the DeepState series compiled by Russia Matters and ISW assessments [S1][S2], formality from signed texts of the parties [S3][S4]; as of 23 October 2026 no written ceasefire or framework text exists between Russia and Ukraine, so all termination paths below are forecasts. Durability is judged over the 12–24 months after any signing; prices are last-trading-day official settlements.

Central thesis. By end-2027 the war's end-state, not its daily tempo, is the central pricing driver, with the probability mass on de-escalation without a signature. The canonical frame: Active high-intensity war — 15% — escalation dynamics and Russian war-economy output keep fighting at or above late-2026 intensity; Reduced-intensity attrition — 35% — mutual manpower and munitions constraints lower the tempo without a durable deal; Frozen conflict — 32% — Zelensky's stated openness to freezing the frontline and partial-truce precedents yield a de facto ceasefire without signature; Formal armistice — 15% — sustained US mediation and mutual exhaustion produce a signed, monitored line; Comprehensive peace settlement — 3% — only a political rupture in Moscow or a decisive battlefield outcome. Unsigned de-escalation carries 67% — the modal path runs through lapsing truces to a de facto freeze — signed termination 18%; escalation 15%.

The baseline. The forecast is set against the ECB deposit facility rate at 2.5% (2026-09) [S5], euro-area core HICP at 2.5% change from year ago (2026-09) [S6], gold at 4334.7467 U.S. dollars per troy ounce (2026-09) [S7], and EU natural gas at 25.494 U.S. dollars per million metric British thermal unit (2026-09) [S8] — a baseline already carrying the Middle East energy shock both central banks cite, plus this war [S9][S10]. Ukraine enters 2027 with a funding gap of around €70 billion against another planned €45 billion from the EU, per Reuters [S11], on the EUR 90 billion EU Ukraine Support Loan [S12]. No end-2027 Brent, TTF or gold quantiles are in evidence; ranges are scenario-priced, not market-implied.

Pricing by state (this report's forecasts): Active high-intensity war — Brent $100–135/bbl; the IEA warns a prolonged Hormuz disruption "could render unavailable the vast majority of the world's spare production capacity" [S13]. Reduced-intensity attrition — Brent $85–105/bbl, TTF €65–85/MWh, Ukraine GDP growth 2–3%; partial truces lapse and revive while the EU maritime-ban basis stays on the books [S14][S15]. Frozen conflict — a partial unwind of energy and safe-haven premia as long-range strikes halt. Formal armistice and Comprehensive peace settlement — Russian supply returns, TTF and gold premia unwind, and Ukrainian assets — GDP, restructuring terms, the hryvnia — reprice upward. European rearmament rises under every state on NATO's 2035-anchored pledge, EU instruments and German budgets [S16][S17][S18][S19][S20].

Leading indicators: monthly net territorial change and strike tempo in the DeepState/ISW series [S1][S2] — three or more months of near-zero movement would confirm Frozen conflict; negotiation milestones in Kremlin, Ukrainian-presidential and US-envoy statements [S21][S22][S3] — any signed text shifts weight toward Formal armistice; sanctions steps in the EU Council timeline and US Treasury actions [S14][S15] — any legally effective oil-cap or maritime-ban waiver reprices Russian supply; and official price and inflation series from Eurostat, the ECB and the BLS [S23][S8][S24][S25].

What would change the view: a signed ceasefire or framework text (energy and gold down, Ukrainian assets up); a Russian mobilization wave or strike-tempo surge (toward Active high-intensity war); legally effective EU or US relief on Russian oil before 2028 (Brent toward the lower band); and slippage in German or NATO defense budgets (weakening the rearmament floor).

### Territory and the year's movement

Russia, including Crimea and pre-2022 Donbas, controls approximately 45,196 square miles of Ukraine, about 19% of its territory [S1]. Over the twelve months to October 19, 2026, its net gain was 753 square miles — 0.3% of Ukraine, an average of 74 square miles a month: a slow, costly grind [S1]. Momentum then reversed. The net four-week gain fell from 37 square miles (Aug 25–Sep 22) to 3 (Sep 22–Oct 19); Russia lost a net 20 square miles in the six days to October 19; The Economist put Ukraine's four-week recapture at 30 square miles [S1]. ISW's own series shows Ukraine liberating about 1,080 square kilometers since January 1, 2026, Russia net-gaining about 73 square kilometers over that span and net-losing about 73 since September 1 [S2]. The two series diverge by an order of magnitude on annual windows (753 versus roughly 28 square miles) — different map series, control thresholds and windows; both are reported, not averaged [S1][S2].

ISW assesses that Ukrainian counteroffensive operations have "operationally defeated" the planned Russian offensive to complete the seizure of Donetsk Oblast — Putin's self-defined objective since 2022 [S2]. The counteroffensive ("Operation Vivaldi") defeated the northern pincer meant to encircle the "Fortress Belt"; Brig. Gen. Andrii Biletskyi said on October 19 that Ukrainian forces had reached the Zherebets River, shortening the front [S27]. Constraints persist: Starlink/Starshield speed restrictions are degrading drone interception, logistics and counter-drone air defense, per Zelensky and analyst Sergei Beskrestnov [S27]; Zelensky said on October 20 that US early-warning intelligence sharing "is now working," after three nights in which warnings did not arrive [S27].

### Attrition and casualties

Ukraine's General Staff claims record Russian losses of 46,230 personnel killed or wounded in September 2026 and 326,900 for January–September, monthly losses up 45.8% from January to September — belligerent figures, not independently verified, best read as upper-bound estimates [S26]. Ukrainian casualty estimates span an order of magnitude by method: 250,000–300,000 killed and wounded (a former high-ranking Western official's estimate, February 2026) [S1]; roughly 600,000 killed, wounded or missing (a CSIS study, January 2026) [S28]; 1,225,000 killed and wounded (a UK estimate, January 2026) [S29]. No official Ukrainian figure exists.

### Mobilization and munitions

Russia plans about 120,000 conscripts for the autumn 2026 draft, per Col. Gen. Yevgeny Burdinsky, head of the General Staff's mobilization directorate [S26]. Putin ordered a fourth 2026 increase in authorized strength — plus 15,500, to 2,441,630, including 1,550,500 military personnel [S26]. A July 2026 Duma law lets people with criminal convictions sign military contracts during mobilization, and Meduza reports a federal directive for regional administrations to cut male staff by 10–15% by October 2026, freeing them for mobilization [S30]. Estonian intelligence assesses Russian factories produced about 7 million artillery, mortar, tank and rocket rounds in 2025, up roughly 17-fold from about 400,000 four years earlier — a secondary account of classified data mixing round types [S31][S32].

Ukraine fields an estimated 880,000–900,000 personnel against Russia's 1.55 million authorized, on a roughly 1,200 km front [S33]. Defense Minister Mykhailo Fedorov estimated in January 2026 that 200,000 soldiers were absent without leave and 2 million men avoided draft notices [S34]; the average soldier is over 40, and Kyiv rejects lowering the conscription age from 25 to 18 [S34]. May 2026 reforms raise specialist pay and expand foreign-volunteer recruitment [S33]; only 10% of recruits had joined via decentralized mechanisms by February 2026, against a 20–30% goal [S33]. Drone contracts in 2026 year-to-date run at 5.5 times the 2025 level, with deliveries up almost 3.7 times [S35]; the 6–7 million FPV drones targeted for 2026 is a plan, not delivered output [S36]. Ukraine cannot make 155mm shells domestically [S37]; Rheinmetall delivered its first Unterlüß 155mm rounds in July 2026, targeting about 1.5 million a year by end-2026 [S38].

### What would change the view

Sustained Ukrainian net gains in the ISW and Russia Matters series over four or more weeks; Russian four-week gains rebounding above the late-August 37-square-mile pace; a Russian mobilization decree beyond authorized-strength increases — a tripwire for the Active high-intensity war — 15% scenario; an autumn draft well above 120,000; and evidence that Ukrainian absence and draft-evasion totals are falling, which would support the Reduced-intensity attrition — 35% and Frozen conflict — 32% states.

### Positions and the sequencing gap

Putin stated on October 23 that Russia is ready for three-way talks on the security parameters of a final peace deal, not a "short" ceasefire [S4]; Ushakov framed the resumed talks as "building a security system for Russia and Ukraine after the conflict ends" [S3]. Putin told Witkoff the Oslo proposals contained "little that was useful," and Peskov said Russia "needs peace, not a ceasefire" — which ISW assesses effectively rejects Ukraine's and Europe's proposals [S21]. Zelensky, on Fox News on October 21, said Ukraine is ready to freeze the conflict along the current front line — "Right now we have to freeze, and I think this is a real way to end this war" — called a ceasefire the minimum Ukraine expects, rejected surrendering Donbas, and proposed ceasefires on food security, maritime activity and energy infrastructure [S22][S21]. Trump is pressing Kyiv to make a deal, and Putin has offered to help Trump with Iran, linking the track to wider US–Russia bargaining [S3][S51]. The gap — ceasefire-first versus final-deal-first — is the central obstacle; these are stated positions, not agreed text.

### Security guarantees

Zelensky named three unagreed postwar options on October 22: NATO membership; direct US guarantees modeled on NATO's Article 5; and a European "Coalition of the Willing" backed by Washington [S52]. Putin's stated red line is a Western presence: he warned Europe of war if peacekeeping troops are deployed to Ukraine (Washington Post, September 14, 2026) [S48]. Whether Kyiv would accept a freeze without a US guarantee remains open.

### Sanctions in force and US relief

The EU has adopted a 20th package: sanctions on 120 individuals, the basis for a future maritime-services ban on Russian crude oil and petroleum products, further port-access bans and, per a law-firm summary, 20 more Russian banks under a transaction ban (70 EU-excluded in total); the Council's adoption date could not be confirmed [S14][S53]. The US track runs opposite. Trump signed an executive order on October 16, 2026 waiving the newly passed "Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026" [S54][S55]; he announced a Russian diesel purchase deal on October 9, with a Treasury license suspending US sanctions on Russian diesel deliveries until April 2027 [S15][S56]; and Reuters reported on October 14–15 that the administration is weighing lifting sanctions on some shadow-fleet vessels to cut freight rates [S57]. The driver is domestic: weekly US diesel prices rose from $3.48/gallon on January 5, 2026 to a $6.53/gallon peak on September 21, 2026, ahead of the November 3 elections [S15][S51]. Trump told Ukraine to stop attacking Russian refineries; Zelensky said Ukraine would comply only if Russia reciprocated or the US provided more Patriot interceptors; Ukraine paused energy-infrastructure strikes for three days, then resumed [S15][S51]. Relief is thus being sequenced unilaterally and pre-deal, diverging from the EU's ratcheting. Russia's immobilized sovereign assets (about EUR 185 billion at Euroclear) enter no official text; a non-official, Sachs-published framework proposes all sanctions lifted and the assets released in stages by December 31, 2027 [S58].

### Military-aid regime

The EU Ukraine Support Loan — EUR 90 billion for Ukraine's budgetary and defense needs in 2026–2027 — drove nearly EUR 11 billion of allocations in May–June 2026 (EUR 4.3 billion military, EUR 6.7 billion financial and humanitarian), even as financial and humanitarian aid ran 41% below 2025 on a monthly average in H1 2026, per the Kiel Institute [S12]. Europe is the largest military donor, but US-made weapons underpin it: at least EUR 3 billion of European-procured military aid in H1 2026 came from US companies (~30% of industry-procured aid), and US stockpiles supplied more than 90% of aid under NATO's PURL initiative [S12]. US assistance continues below FY2022–FY2024 levels, per the Congressional Research Service [S59]. Reuters reports another €45 billion planned from the EU for 2027 against an estimated ~€70 billion funding gap, with leaders to discuss new financing — possibly another loan — on November 26–27 [S11]. US–Ukraine relations "deteriorated sharply" after the diesel deal, per Reuters, over intelligence-sharing and Starshield disruptions that the October 20 Oslo meeting agreed to resolve [S51].

### What would change the view

Whether trilateral talks convene after November 10, 2026, and at what level (Kremlin and White House readouts); a first signed text — an energy or maritime truce would point toward the Formal armistice — 15% scenario, while a quiet front without signature fits Frozen conflict — 32%; the EU adopting or deferring the maritime-services ban (Council decision); Congress overriding the Russia waivers (vote record); and Kyiv accepting a freeze conditioned on a US guarantee.

### Manpower

Ukraine fields 880,000–900,000 personnel against Russia's 1.55 million authorized military [S33], but Defense Minister Fedorov counted 200,000 soldiers absent without leave and 2 million men evading draft notices in January 2026, with the average soldier over 40 and the conscription age still 25 [S34]. May 2026 reforms raised pay for scarce specialties and expanded foreign recruitment to widen the volunteer funnel [S33]. Russia replenishes coercively: about 120,000 autumn conscripts [S26], a July 2026 law allowing convicts to contract during mobilization, and a directive to cut male regional-administration staff by 10–15% by October 2026, which CEPA reads as mobilization preparation [S30].

### Munitions, industry and aid

Estonian intelligence estimates Russian factories produced about 7 million artillery, mortar, tank and rocket rounds in 2025, roughly 17 times the level of four years earlier [S31][S32]. Ukraine's contracted drones rose 5.5-fold year-to-date, deliveries 3.7-fold (Defense Ministry, October 11, 2026) [S35]; its 6–7 million FPV-drone figure for 2026 is a stated target, not delivered output [S36]. Ukraine cannot make 155 mm shells domestically; Rheinmetall's Unterlüß plant targets about 1.5 million rounds a year from end-2026 [S38]. The EUR 90 billion EU Ukraine Support Loan covers 2026–27 budgetary and defense needs, yet H1 2026 financial and humanitarian aid ran 41% below 2025 monthly averages [S12]; Europe leads military aid but US weapons underpin it — over 90% of NATO PURL aid came from US stockpiles [S12] — new US assistance runs below FY2022–FY2024 levels [S59], and Reuters reports €45 billion planned for 2027 against an estimated €70 billion funding gap [S11].

### The Russian fiscal position

Russia's 2026 deficit estimate was raised to 3.2% of GDP from 1.6%, the oil-and-gas revenue estimate cut to 7.6 trillion from 8.9 trillion roubles, and borrowing set at 5 trillion roubles plus 459 billion roubles — about 11% of the liquid National Wealth Fund [S60]. In 2027 state debt rises to 21.7% of GDP, above the 20% threshold authorities deem safe, and borrowing jumps 43% to 7.7 trillion roubles [S60]. Carnegie notes wartime deficit targets have never been met (2025: 0.5% targeted, 2.6% actual) and that the fiscal-rule cutoff was cut to $50 per barrel against official 2027 oil-price forecasts of $46–53, with the ministry borrowing at 14–15% [S61] — far above US policy rates of 3.875% per year in September 2026 [S39]. CREA put July 2026 fossil-fuel export revenues at EUR 683 million per day, down 12% month-on-month [S62], against Brent averaging 102.0181 US dollars per barrel in September 2026 versus 67.6091 a year earlier [S23], and EU gas at 25.494 US dollars per MMBtu versus 11.003 [S8].

### Balance of sustainability through 2027

Neither side shows a 2027 collapse on this evidence. Russia can still add manpower and shells, but pays from a thinning buffer: National Wealth Fund drawdown, debt crossing its own safe threshold, repeated deficit overshoot, and export revenue flat in volume terms. Ukraine's binding constraint is manpower — desertion, evasion, an over-40 average age — while drone output and EU financing scale. That asymmetry, Russian material against Ukrainian manpower, underpins the modal frame states Reduced-intensity attrition — 35% and Frozen conflict — 32%; Active high-intensity war — 15% requires Russia to sustain tempo despite fiscal strain, while Formal armistice — 15% and Comprehensive peace settlement — 3% turn on bargaining rather than capacity.

### What would change the view

- A second Russian mobilization wave, or conscripts sent to the front, would raise the odds of Active high-intensity war — 15%.
- Ukrainian AWOL counts passing 300,000, or a mobilization-age cut below 25, would signal manpower rupture and accelerate a freeze.
- The November 26–27, 2026 EU summit failing to close the €70 billion 2027 gap [S11] would cut Ukraine's war-fighting floor.
- Brent settling below Russia's $50-per-barrel fiscal-rule cutoff [S61] would tighten the 2027 financing squeeze.
- Russian OFZ yields forced above 16%, or liquid National Wealth Fund assets below one-tenth of GDP, would make financing Russia's binding constraint.

### The bargaining condition

The obstacle is sequence. Putin frames the resumed talks around "the security parameters of a final peace deal," not a "short" ceasefire [S4]; Peskov says Russia "needs peace, not a ceasefire," and Putin called the Oslo proposals "little that was useful" [S21]. Zelensky's stated minimum reverses the order — "Right now we have to freeze," then guarantees [S22][S52] — so the divisible issue (a front line) stays blocked by the indivisible one (security architecture). Trump is pressing Kyiv to deal [S3], and Washington is granting relief unilaterally and pre-deal on a pre-midterm clock: an October 9 diesel purchase deal with a Treasury license suspending sanctions on Russian diesel deliveries until April 2027 [S15][S56], a reported October 16 waiver of the newly passed Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act [S55][S63], and consideration of shadow-fleet relief [S57]. That rewards Russia's waiting strategy — its fiscal strain is real but not binding, as wartime deficit targets have never been met [S61] — and splits the coalition: the EU still ratchets up with a 20th package (120 individuals; a prospective maritime-services ban; 20 more banks excluded) [S14][S53]. Ukraine's binding constraint is manpower rather than munitions [S34][S35]; told to stop striking refineries, Zelensky demanded reciprocity or more Patriots [S15], and restored US intelligence and Starshield access remains contingent [S27][S51]. No source publishes a probability for the documented escalation hazards, such as Russia's May 2026 nuclear signaling [S64][S69], so none is attached to them here.

### Mapping to the war states

- **Active high-intensity war — 15%**: Russia converts mobilization signals (convict contracts; a directive to cut male state staff 10–15% by October 2026) [S30] into higher tempo despite fiscal tightening [S60].
- **Reduced-intensity attrition — 35%**: mutual constraint — Ukrainian manpower [S34], Russian fiscal strain [S61], US pressure for lower tempo [S15] — with no durable deal.
- **Frozen conflict — 32%**: Ukraine's freeze offer and partial truces (energy, maritime, food security) [S22] hold de facto while Russia refuses to sign.
- **Formal armistice — 15%**: the US mediation track (ninth Witkoff–Putin meeting; talks from around November 10, possibly Abu Dhabi) [S49][S3] closes the sequencing gap.
- **Comprehensive peace settlement — 3%**: no framework text exists; the only published relief-sequencing plan is non-official [S58].

### What would change the view

1. Trilateral talks convening after November 10, 2026 at principals level (Kremlin, White House and Ukrainian presidency statements) would raise the odds of a signed line; envoy-level collapse would confirm attrition.
2. EU adoption of the 20th package's maritime-services ban alongside US waivers extending past April 2027 (EU Council decisions; Treasury licenses) would harden transatlantic divergence.
3. Execution of Russia's staff-cut directive or a formal mobilization wave [S30] would raise the intensity outlook.
4. Ukraine scaling foreign recruitment toward the OSW estimate of 120,000–200,000 [S70], with falling AWOL counts, would extend Kyiv's staying power.
5. US retail diesel near $4/gallon after November 3 would cut Washington's urgency for Russia-side deals.

## Base rates for war termination and ceasefire durability

### Definitions and settling sources
A war state is assessed as holding **on** 31 December 2027 (in force that day); a ceasefire or armistice counts only if it entered into force **by** that date. Durability is judged over 12- and 24-month windows from entry into force. Historical settling sources are Correlates of War (COW) interstate-war coding and the UCDP Conflict Termination dataset (1946–2005) [S71]; the live case settles on the text of any agreement and on ISW/DeepState front-line assessments [S2][S1].

### Duration and survivorship
The war is in its fifth year of full-scale fighting — longer than the First World War by mid-2026, per The Conversation [S78] — against a 2.1-year interstate average [S72]. Unconditional averages understate the hazard for a war that has already survived this long; Iran-Iraq, the closest analog, ran eight years to a ceasefire [S79]. This survivorship logic is why the frame keeps half its mass on continued fighting: Active high-intensity war — 15% — plus Reduced-intensity attrition — 35%.

### Termination type
Interstate conflicts since 1946 are far more likely than civil wars to end in a negotiated peace, per research cited in an escalation-dynamics study [S72] — the anchor for the 50% placed jointly on Frozen conflict — 32% — Formal armistice — 15% — and Comprehensive peace settlement — 3%. The termination-type distribution for interstate wars lasting 4–6 years could not be computed from UCDP within this investigation [S71], so the split among the three termination states rests on fragility evidence rather than a clean count.

### Ceasefire fragility and durability
Mechanism-similar evidence is bearish on first ceasefires holding: Minsk-era Ukraine ceasefires produced "at least a few weeks" of reduced violence on average [S80], and the 63-of-2,202 figure is only a lower bound on fragility, since its qualifying clause was truncated in the source [S75]. Monitoring is a recognized durability covariate [S81], and Fortna finds agreement content, including peacekeeping provisions, lengthens peace after interstate wars [S76] — but the magnitude is unquantified here (a widely cited figure near a 90 percent risk reduction could not be confirmed in the fetched text [S82]), and the literature is contradictory: Diehl, Reifschneider and Hensel find no significant UN-intervention effect [S82]. Live diplomacy caps durability further: Putin insists the resumed talks cover a final settlement's security parameters, "not a 'short' ceasefire" [S4], while Zelensky says Ukraine is ready to freeze the conflict along the current front line [S22] — convergence on a freeze, not a monitored armistice. Russia's stated rejection of a Western presence (Putin warned Europe of war if peacekeeping troops are deployed to Ukraine, per a Washington Post report of September 14, 2026) [S48] removes the covariate most associated with durable ceasefires, one reason Frozen conflict — 32% — outweighs Formal armistice — 15% — and Comprehensive peace settlement — 3%.

### The macro-political accelerant
Domestic economic pain is the documented accelerant of Western termination pressure. Weekly US diesel prices rose from $3.48/gallon on January 5, 2026 to a peak of $6.53/gallon on September 21, 2026, hurting Republicans ahead of the November 3 legislative elections [S15]. The IMF estimates US CPI at 2.73% for 2025 and projects 3.648% for 2026 and 2.835% for 2027 [S24]; the US policy rate stood at 3.875% at end-September 2026 [S39] and the ECB deposit rate at 2.5% [S5], so war costs now transmit through inflation and rates directly into electoral politics — the mechanism behind the frame's election-cycle thesis.

### What would change the view
Computed UCDP/COW termination-type counts for 4–6-year interstate wars would recalibrate the armistice–settlement split [S71]. A written framework text from the post-November 10 trilateral talks [S51] would shift mass toward Formal armistice. Russian acceptance of any third-party monitoring mission would raise durability priors [S81]. General Russian mobilization — signals already visible in a July 2026 Duma law on convict contracts and a directive to cut male regional staff by 10–15% [S30] — would raise Active high-intensity war. No source in the evidence publishes an explicit probability for nuclear use, NATO enlargement or a rupture in Moscow; none is estimated here.
