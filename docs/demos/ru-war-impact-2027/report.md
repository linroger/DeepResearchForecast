## Part 2 — Framework & Synthesis

### Analytical Framework: Pricing the War State, Not the War

The logic is transmission-based. The war state determines (i) whether Russian energy exports return to western markets and at what pace, (ii) the size of the geopolitical risk premium embedded in oil, gas and gold, (iii) the energy pass-through into euro-area and US inflation and hence central-bank reaction functions, and (iv) fiscal trajectories — European rearmament in all states, Russian war-economy sustainability in the fighting states. Everything else in Part 1 is derived pricing, not independent judgment.

### The Late-2026 Baseline

The war is in its fifth year of full-scale fighting, roughly double the 2.1-year interstate-war average duration and approaching Iran-Iraq's eight-year arc to ceasefire. Whatever the mapping dispute, direction is agreed: momentum has reversed.

Militarily, both sides face binding but different constraints.

Diplomatically, no ceasefire text exists. after the ninth Witkoff-Putin meeting, Putin agreed to resume trilateral talks after November 10 [S51]. The sequencing gap is the central obstacle: Putin insists on negotiating final-deal security parameters, "not a short ceasefire," while Zelensky says Ukraine is ready to freeze the front line now, provided a ceasefire comes first with security guarantees [S4].

The coalition is splitting on sanctions.

Markets carry a double shock — the Middle East energy disruption layered on the war. TTF around €92/MWh; gold averaged $4,334.7/oz in September, up 18.2% year-on-year [S7];

### Why the Probability Mass Sits at 58% on Non-Termination

Three forces suppress escalation probability. Second, both drone-production curves are scaling toward mutual deterrence at the front, favoring stasis over breakthrough. Third, Russia's 2027 budget — 27% higher military spending — sustains the war but at rising fiscal cost, incentivizing positional consolidation over renewed offensive gambits [S60].

Russia's fiscal trajectory caps but does not force termination; US pressure lowers tempo via sanctions relief without a signed deal; Attrition-without-termination is also the historical modal endpoint of long interstate wars that have exhausted offensive options.

A freeze requires no Russian signature, no sequencing resolution, no security-guarantee architecture — merely reciprocal operational exhaustion and a quiet understanding. Base rates support this: frozen lines are the modal end-state of long interstate conflicts, and partial truces (Black Sea grain precedent) have held without formal agreements.

The sequencing gap — ceasefire-first versus final-deal-first — has collapsed nine rounds of Witkoff mediation and a Geneva process. Putin's incentive structure rewards waiting: US unilateral relief is already arriving without Ukrainian concessions, validating a hold-out strategy.

Confidence is low, and honestly so: the November 3 US midterms, the November 10+ trilateral track, the November 26–27 EU summit on 2027 Ukraine financing, and the April 2027 expiry of the US diesel license are genuine state-contingent branch points [S11].

### Pricing the Two Fighting States

the dollar and franc strengthen. Euro-area growth stalls near 0.5%. [S5] Russia's fiscal strain tightens but does not bind within the horizon.

### Pricing Freeze and Termination

The critical pricing insight: a freeze without signature compresses but does not eliminate the risk premium. Sanctions relief requires a signed instrument the EU can sequence against, and Brussels holds to ratcheting relief. Brent $85–100/b; TTF €50–75/MWh;

Termination is the only state in which premia fully unwind. TTF drops to €35–55/MWh. The ECB cuts to 1.75–2.25%;

### Probability-Weighted Central Forecasts

Putin's stated consideration of uranium, titanium and nickel export restrictions [S119] is a tail risk priced mainly into the fighting states. Palladium supply security remains an EU vulnerability regardless of state [S118].

### Geopolitics Under the Modal Path

Ukraine's membership is a settlement-state question only. European strategic autonomy advances on the SAFE instrument and German pivot but remains US-weapons-dependent. Nuclear signaling persists in fighting states; proliferation incentives in the Global South tick up precisely because the likely end-state rewards nuclear-armed violation of territorial-integrity norms. Sanctions relief, if it comes before a deal, comes unilaterally from Washington — eroding the regime's integrity as an instrument and its deterrent value as a precedent.

### How Prediction-Market Anchors Were Weighed

Where market anchors existed for ceasefire timing, they were treated as one calibrated input among base rates — informative on near-term negotiation noise, systematically over-optimistic on long-horizon termination given nine failed Witkoff rounds and the unresolved sequencing gap. The spine's probabilities are defended on that evidentiary basis: de-escalation without signature is what the front line, the fiscal arithmetic, and the diplomacy all point toward — and it is what the Part-1 numbers price.

## Baseline as of Late October 2026: Conflict, Diplomacy, Sanctions and the Market Starting Point

*Key Metric Trajectories (research-extracted)*

As of late October 2026, Russia and Ukraine remain in armed conflict with no written ceasefire or framework text in existence; every termination path is a forecast, not a fact. Yet the battlefield, the diplomacy, the sanctions regime and the market backdrop have all shifted materially over the prior twelve months, and in directions that pull against each other. This chapter establishes that dual baseline — conflict and market — because the pricing of every end-state scenario in this report is anchored to it.

### The Conflict Baseline: A Front That Has Stopped Moving

The two series diverge by an order of magnitude on annual windows, and both are reported here rather than averaged — but they agree on the direction of the most recent quarter. ISW's assessment is categorical: Ukraine has "operationally defeated" Russia's Donetsk offensive [S2].

That stasis is the product of two asymmetric constraints. Ukraine's binding constraint is manpower.

Russia's constraint is financial rather than human. Its war economy is strained but not yet binding. The National Wealth Fund buffer is thinning but still functional. neither threshold is near as of late October 2026, though both figures derive from secondary budget analysis rather than primary records. [S62]

Ukraine's other constraint is external. financial and humanitarian aid ran 41% below 2025 on a monthly-average basis in the first half of 2026 per the Kiel Institute, even as Europe leads on total aid while remaining dependent on US weapons for key capabilities. [S118] EU leaders meet on November 26-27, 2026 to discuss new 2027 financing — possibly another loan — making that summit the single most important near-term test of Ukraine's war sustainability [S11].

### The Diplomatic Baseline: Nine Meetings, No Text

Diplomacy has restarted without producing anything binding. On October 20, 2026, US envoy Witkoff met Zelensky and Finnish, Norwegian, European and NATO representatives in Oslo, where proposals were tabled with no resulting text; [S49] Witkoff then met Putin in St. Petersburg on October 21 — their ninth in-person meeting, lasting three hours — after which US citizen Stephen Hubbard was released [S51]. Ushakov announced that Russia agreed "in principle" to resume trilateral talks after November 10, possibly in Abu Dhabi, though no agenda text exists [S51].

The obstacle is sequencing, and both leaders have stated it publicly. Putin's position is that Russia is ready for talks on the security parameters of a final peace deal, "not a short ceasefire" [S4]; Peskov has said Russia "needs peace, not a ceasefire," which ISW assesses effectively rejects both Ukraine's and Europe's proposals; and Putin told Witkoff the Oslo proposals contained "little that was useful". Zelensky, speaking on Fox News on October 21, said: [S22]

> "Right now we have to freeze, and I think this is a real way to end this war"

The result is a structural gap: Kyiv wants a ceasefire first plus guarantees; Moscow wants a final-deal framework first and rejects the Western presence any guarantee would require.

One further wrinkle: the US mediation track is entangled with wider US-Russia bargaining. Putin has offered to help Trump with Iran, linking the Ukraine track to Middle East diplomacy [S51] — which matters for market pricing because the current energy-price baseline already embeds a Middle East disruption premium alongside the war premium, and the two cannot be cleanly separated in observed prices.

### The Sanctions Baseline: A Coalition Splitting in Real Time

The US has moved to unilateral, pre-deal relief. On October 9, Trump announced a Russian diesel purchase deal, backed by a Treasury license suspending US sanctions on Russian diesel deliveries until April 2027 [S15]; on October 16 he signed an executive order waiving the newly passed Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026 [S63]; The diesel deal also strained US-Ukraine relations over intelligence-sharing and Starshield disruptions, which the October 20 Oslo meeting agreed to resolve; [S49] restored US intelligence and Starshield access remains contingent.

The EU is moving in the opposite direction. The Council's adoption date could not be confirmed against primary records, so the 20th package is treated as reported rather than settled.

This divergence is the mechanism through which US domestic politics transmits into the war's end-state. US relief without a deal rewards Russia's waiting strategy — Putin's incentive structure improves as long as US relief flows unilaterally while Ukraine's funding gap stays open — and it erodes the price-cap enforcement architecture that depends on coalition unity. relief tied to talks would push toward armistice; a congressional override vote (which the evidence flags as a live possibility) would reverse the channel. The November 3 midterms are the first test of durability.

### The Market and Macro Baseline: A Double Shock Priced at the 95th Percentile

Market levels as of late October 2026 embed both the war and a Middle East energy disruption, and they sit near the top of their five-year empirical ranges. Brent spot stood at $115.22/barrel on October 20, 2026 [S40];

European gas is the sharper shock.

The macro picture is one of energy-driven headline inflation over sticky-but-contained core. German 10-year Bunds yield 3.47% [S46].

### The Rearmament Baseline: Committed, Budgeted, Front-Loaded

European defense spending has already crossed into a structurally higher regime. The critical baseline fact for this report's forecasts: because 2027 budgets are drafted and legislated in 2026, the defense-spending trajectory for 2027 is largely locked in regardless of the war's end-state — rearmament rises under every scenario, and even a comprehensive peace settlement would not unwind the Hague pledge. [S61]

### Why This Baseline Points Toward Freeze Without Signature

Assembled, the baseline carries three implications that structure the rest of this report. First, the military trend — a front that has stopped moving, Russia's offensive defeated, Ukraine unable to convert defense into reconquest — is the classic precondition for a frozen conflict, and Zelensky has explicitly offered one [S2]. Second, the diplomatic deadlock is about sequencing, not substance: both sides accept the current line as a negotiating reality, but neither can accept the other's route to ending the war, and Putin's peacekeeping red line blocks the guarantee architecture Ukraine requires [S4]. Third, the sanctions coalition has already split, with the US buying Russian diesel while the EU prepares a maritime-services ban — a divergence that lowers the cost to Moscow of waiting and, paradoxically, makes a signed deal less necessary for Russia even as it makes one more attractive to Washington [S15][S14]. Each is priced in the chapters that follow.

### Why Tempo Falls: The Two-Sided Exhaustion Mechanism

The core mechanism is that both belligerents' binding constraints tighten simultaneously, but neither becomes fatal.

On the Russian side, the constraint is fiscal-financial. ## Scenario III — Frozen Conflict / De Facto Ceasefire (28%): Freeze Without Signature

This is a termination in behavior, not in law, and that distinction drives every price in this chapter. the freeze means mutual exhaustion has hardened into a stable military equilibrium that neither side expects to break cheaply, even though no signature exists.

### Why the Freeze Is the Modal De-escalation State

The mechanism is asymmetric exhaustion meeting an unbridgeable sequencing gap. The front is approaching stasis from a direction few expected a year ago: not because Russia chose to stop, but because its offensive capacity was broken.

Politics, not the battlefield, is what converts stasis into a freeze rather than a signature. Zelensky said on Fox News on October 21, 2026 that Ukraine is ready to freeze the conflict along the current front line, that a ceasefire is "the minimum" Ukraine expects, and that he rejects surrendering Donbas; [S22] he also proposed narrower ceasefires on food security, maritime activity and energy infrastructure. Putin's position is the mirror image: Russia is ready for three-way talks on the security parameters of a final peace deal, "not a short ceasefire," and Peskov has said Russia "needs peace, not a ceasefire." The result is a gap in which both sides can live with silence on the front line but cannot agree on paper. This is precisely the configuration that historically produces frozen lines: the modal resting state of long interstate conflicts after the maneuver phase exhausts itself.

A critical covariate pushes the probability toward the freeze state and away from a signed armistice: Putin's rejection of any Western troop presence. Research on interstate ceasefires shows that strong, monitored agreements significantly lengthen peace — and Putin has removed the covariate most associated with durable monitoring before talks even resume. So the deal that could be signed cannot be monitored to Ukraine's satisfaction, and the deal Ukraine would accept cannot be signed to Russia's. The equilibrium is a quiet front without text.

The most likely timing runs through the negotiation calendar already visible. Trilateral talks resume after November 10, 2026, possibly in Abu Dhabi, after Russia agreed "in principle" at the ninth Witkoff–Putin meeting [S51]. The report's modal trajectory is that these talks — like the Abu Dhabi and Geneva rounds of January–February 2026, which ended without text — fail to produce a framework document, but that the mutual military exhaustion visible in October's front-line data deepens through winter 2026–27, producing a consolidating de facto freeze in the second half of 2027. The frozen-conflict state is therefore best understood as the terminal state of a failed negotiation, not a successful one.

### Pricing Energy: Asymmetric Sanctions Relief and the Brent Band

The mechanism is an asymmetric partial unwinding. On the US side, sanctions relief is already flowing pre-deal: a Treasury license suspends sanctions on Russian diesel deliveries until April 2027, Trump signed an October 16 executive order waiving the Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026, and Washington is weighing lifting sanctions on some shadow-fleet vessels to cut freight rates. In a frozen-conflict world, these waivers extend — Congress may vote to override, and that vote record is the single most important indicator for whether Brent moves toward the lower or upper end of the band. a quiet front line removes the urgency to reverse it.

But the EU does not follow. The result is a split-price regime: Russian crude re-flows toward US-linked and non-aligned buyers at softer discounts while EU enforcement keeps a floor under the global risk premium.

A quiet front line removes strike risk to Ukrainian and regional energy infrastructure — one of Zelensky's own proposed truce areas — which is the main channel by which the freeze discounts gas more than oil, since Russian pipeline transit to Europe remains absent regardless of war state and Europe's marginal supply remains LNG.

### Gold, Safe Havens and the Disinflation Trade

Two forces pull in opposite directions. the euro-area real effective exchange rate, at 103.27 in September [S42], appreciates modestly as the region's terms-of-trade shock unwinds.

### Europe's Economy and the Ukrainian Recovery Asymmetry

The freeze is the best continuing-war state for European growth precisely because it delivers most of the energy relief without requiring any of the political settlement. Lower energy costs act as a tax cut on households; the defense build-out adds demand.

Ukraine is the biggest relative winner among the continuing-war states. The IMF program engagement continues. The hryvnia stabilizes and appreciates modestly from an undervalued base; sovereign-restructuring terms improve at the margin as market participants price a durable-if-unsigned truce. That is why Ukrainian assets re-rate only partially — they price a truce, not a peace.

### Defense Spending and the Geopolitical Residue

The freeze does not disarm Europe; it institutionalizes rearmament, because the unsolved war proves that deterrence must be indigenous. Notably, a freeze removes the immediate-war urgency that might otherwise justify front-loading, but the 2027 budgets are already legislated — the spending is committed ahead of the war state resolving. [S60]

The geopolitical residue of a freeze without signature is corrosive rather than stabilizing. Sanctions relief stays partial and asymmetric: the US-first sequence (diesel, Graham waiver, possible shadow-fleet relief ) proceeds without Ukrainian concessions or EU agreement, splitting the coalition and eroding the credibility of sanctions as an instrument — a lesson not lost on Beijing. Nuclear signaling quiets but does not disappear; the proliferation incentive — that nuclear-armed aggression secured durable territorial gains — is the freeze state's darkest legacy for the Global South and for territorial-integrity norms, which are eroded in fact even as they remain intact in law, since no major power recognizes Russia's annexations.

The falsification test for this scenario is explicit: a written framework text emerging from the post-November 10 trilateral talks would shift probability mass toward formal armistice; [S49]

## Scenarios IV & V — Formal Armistice (13%) and Comprehensive Settlement (9%): Termination Pricing

The distinction between them is not cosmetic. An armistice stops the fighting; a settlement attempts to end the conflict's underlying dispute over territory, security architecture, and sanctions. Each demands a different political rupture, and each prices differently across Brent, TTF, gold, Bunds, CEE assets, and Ukrainian sovereign paper.

### Scenario IV — Formal Armistice (13%): Pricing a Signature Where No Text Exists

**The defining feature of this scenario is that it requires producing something the diplomatic record shows does not exist.** As of late October 2026, there is no agreed ceasefire text between Russia and Ukraine; [S49] talks collapsed in Geneva in February 2026, and the central obstacle remains sequencing—Putin insists talks must cover the security parameters of a final peace deal and explicitly rejects a "short ceasefire," while Zelensky says Ukraine is ready to freeze the front line now and wants a ceasefire first plus security guarantees. [S22] Resolution for this scenario is strict: a signed ceasefire or armistice document, recognized by the UN, the US, the EU, and both belligerents, entering into force on or before December 31, 2027, with a named monitoring mechanism. [S60]

The most plausible route runs through sustained US mediation plus mutual exhaustion. The building blocks are visible: Putin agreed after the ninth Witkoff-Putin meeting to resume trilateral Russia-Ukraine-US talks after November 10 [S51], Russia released a US teacher during those talks after a Trump appeal, and the US administration has already demonstrated it will pay for Russian cooperation with pre-deal sanctions relief—suspending new Russia sanctions under the Graham Act and licensing Russian diesel deliveries to April 2027. the US mediates a signed line-freeze with third-party monitoring; and each side signs because the alternative—continuing to burn manpower and money at near-zero territorial yield—becomes worse than the domestic cost of appearing to settle.

**Why only 13%?** The sequencing gap is not a technical detail; it is structural. Putin's final-deal-first position means a signed armistice requires him to accept the very thing he has named as insufficient, or requires Kyiv to concede final-status parameters—territorial recognition, security limits—before a ceasefire, which Zelensky's stated red lines (refusing to surrender Donbas, demanding guarantees) preclude. if not, pressure toward a suboptimal signed truce rises.

**Pricing under armistice.** The energy unwind is large but incomplete, because a signed ceasefire that leaves sanctions formally in place returns only some Russian barrels and cubic meters to transparent markets:

- Russian pipeline flows return only partially and slowly even under a signed truce, because EU sanctions architecture, including the prospective maritime-services ban basis in the 20th package, remains law until sequenced relief. a cold winter pins the range at its top.
- The dollar softens modestly as war-driven safe-haven demand recedes; the Swiss franc gives back more of its crisis bid;
- CEE currencies appreciate and sovereign spreads tighten as the regional risk discount compresses. Ukraine's three unagreed security-guarantee options—NATO membership, direct US guarantees modeled on Article 5, or a European Coalition of the Willing backed by Washington—remain unresolved under a mere armistice, capping the re-rating.

### Scenario V — Comprehensive Settlement (9%): The Full Unwind

**A comprehensive settlement requires a rupture, not a negotiation.** The defining judgment is that no framework text exists, and the base rate for a full interstate peace settlement within a single forecast year is low; the modal pathway here is either a Moscow political rupture or a decisive battlefield outcome forcing one side to accept final-status terms covering security guarantees, territory, and sanctions relief, ratified at least by the Ukrainian side.

If it happens, the pricing is the armistice trade amplified and extended:

- OPEC+ manages the return but accepts lower prices as the price of normalized market share.
- **Sanctions and geopolitics:** Sequenced EU relief, with the 20th-package architecture unwound in negotiated stages;

### The Durability Problem: Why Fortna Matters for Pricing

A signed document is not a settled peace, and markets will price the difference. An armistice *with* a credible named monitoring mechanism deserves the fuller unwind at the top of its band; Interstate agreements perform better than that record, and monitored ones better still, but the pricing implication is clear: the market will discount termination states for violation risk, holding Brent $5–10 above its pure fundamentals and gold several hundred dollars above a no-risk level, until a ceasefire survives its first crisis.

The response is that the same US relief that enables talks also *rewards Russia's waiting strategy*—Putin's incentive structure improves with every unilateral American concession that arrives without Ukrainian counterparts, and his February 2026 position (final-deal parameters, Western peacekeeping troops as a red line) has not moved. [S51] Relief without a deal is a subsidy to delay, which is precisely why the modal outcome remains an unsigned freeze rather than a signed armistice.

### Binary implications carried to the calibration chapter

All three resolve against ISW front-line assessments, NATO communiqué text, and the EU Council sanctions timeline [S14]—sources whose series, release dates, and thresholds anchor the final calibration table.

## Key Actors and Decision Nodes: The Players Who Determine the War State

The through-line is a single structural fact: the two belligerents want incompatible sequencing, and the third party with the most leverage, Washington, is paying Russia to de-escalate before asking anyone to sign anything.

### The Belligerents: A Sequencing Gap That Prices the Whole Board

Volodymyr Zelensky has moved to a freeze-ready posture. On October 21, 2026 he said on Fox News that Ukraine is prepared to freeze the conflict along the current front line, called a ceasefire the minimum Ukraine expects, rejected surrendering Donbas, and proposed narrower truces on food security, maritime activity and energy infrastructure [S2]. The next day he named three unagreed security-guarantee options: NATO membership, direct US guarantees modeled on Article 5, or a European "Coalition of the Willing" backed by Washington. A leader retaking ground does not sign away territory; he freezes from strength.

Vladimir Putin holds the mirror-image position. Peskov said Russia "needs peace, not a ceasefire," which ISW assesses effectively rejects Ukraine's and Europe's proposals; and Putin told Witkoff that the Oslo proposals contained "little that was useful". Two red lines define his bargaining space. First, Western peacekeeping troops in Ukraine: the Washington Post reported on September 14, 2026 that Putin warned Europe of war if peacekeeping troops are deployed. [S35] This matters for pricing because the research on interstate cease-fires finds that strong, monitored agreements are the ones that durably lengthen peace — and Putin has specifically prohibited the monitoring presence most associated with durable ceasefires.

Putin's other incentive is time. Meanwhile the United States is relieving sanctions unilaterally, without any deal: a Treasury license suspends sanctions on Russian diesel deliveries until April 2027, Trump waived the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026 by executive order on October 16, and the administration is weighing lifting sanctions on some shadow-fleet vessels. [S62] As long as that relief flows without conditions, Putin's dominant strategy is to wait. His linkage of the Ukraine track to helping Washington on Iran adds another reason to keep talks alive without concluding them [S51].

### Washington: Diesel Politics as Grand Strategy

The operative domestic driver of US Russia policy is a fuel-price line.

This creates a coalition-splitting dynamic that runs through every scenario in this report. Congress is the check: the Graham Act passed before it was waived, a possible override vote is a documented indicator, and Representative Meeks has already opposed the waiver publicly. The midterms on November 3 are therefore the first true decision node of the forecast — not because they settle the war, but because they settle whether the waiver architecture survives contact with a new Congress.

Washington also holds direct levers over Kyiv beyond money: intelligence-sharing and Starshield access, which were disrupted over the diesel deal before the October 20 Oslo meeting agreed to resolve them.

### Brussels and Frankfurt: The €70 Billion Fault Line and the Pass-Through Problem

The European Union carries two roles that pull against each other. As sanctions owner, it is the actor whose credibility erodes if US relief re-prices Russian barrels unilaterally; Success — even a partial loan — locks in the modal reduced-intensity path.

The ECB is not a war actor, but it prices like one. It hiked to a 2.50% deposit rate in September 2026, citing Middle East conflict inflation pressure, and states it is "not pre-committing to a particular rate path" [S5].

### Beijing, Moscow's Checkbook, and the Second-Order Players

Beijing's material support is the quiet reason Russia's war economy does not rupture in 2027, and it is also the channel through which any US-China deterioration could raise Russian capability — a dependency that cuts both ways, since Moscow's war economy is now hostage to Chinese export-policy decisions in Washington's trade war. [S60]

Around the principals sit actors with narrower but real veto power. US Treasury tracks the oil price cap; any legally effective cap waiver would reprice Russian supply outright. And Jeffrey Sachs's published "Framework for Peace in Ukraine," proposing all sanctions lifted and immobilized Russian assets released in stages by December 31, 2027, marks one pole of the relief-sequencing debate even as the EU's sequenced approach marks the other. [S33]

### The Dated Decision Map

Assembled, the nodes that resolve uncertainty are few and dated:

- Tests waiver durability; a hostile Congress could force an override vote on the Graham waiver.
- Russia agreed "in principle" on October 21 to resume talks; [S51]
- **November 26-27, 2026 — EU summit.** [S11]
- Extend-without-deal reinforces attrition; lapse raises escalation and upper-band oil risk.

A steelman deserves an answer: could the sequencing gap close precisely because everyone is exhausted — Putin fiscally, Zelensky demographically, Washington electorally? the ninth Witkoff-Putin meeting, the Hubbard release, and Putin's own agreement to talk are all consistent with a leadership shopping for exit architecture. But the same evidence shows what Putin means by architecture: Ushakov framed the resumed talks as building a post-conflict security system, and Putin rejected the short-ceasefire format that is the only thing Zelensky's domestic politics and Ukraine's manpower clock can deliver quickly [S4]. Exhaustion produces stillness on the front line before it produces signatures.

## Geopolitical Reordering Under Each End-State: NATO, the Triangle, Sanctions and the Norm

The war's end-state will not just move prices; it will reorder institutions. The through-line: rearmament is the only trajectory that rises under *every* state, while the sanctions regime is the variable most likely to fracture — and the fracture line runs through Washington, not Moscow.

### NATO Posture and Ukraine's Contested Pathway

Ukraine's membership pathway is the genuinely state-conditional variable. Putin's stated red line — Western peacekeeping troops in Ukraine, with a reported warning to Europe of war if they deploy (Washington Post, September 14, 2026) — removes precisely the covariate most associated with durable ceasefires in the historical record, where strong, monitored agreements lengthen peace. [S35]

The pathway by state: under attrition and high-intensity war, membership stays open-ended and effectively frozen — summit language dating a pathway would itself be a signpost shifting weight toward armistice or settlement. Under a frozen conflict, the modal outcome is a "Coalition of the Willing" guarantee without NATO treaty cover: enough to stabilize the line, not enough to satisfy Kyiv. Under formal armistice, expect explicit pathway language at the next summit. Under comprehensive settlement, the guarantee question becomes the deal's center of gravity — and the most likely failure point. US disengagement would strand that layer under every state.

### The Triangle: China's 75% Anchor and Sanctions Primacy

This calculus is largely state-invariant on the support side — China gains from a dependent Russia whether the war is hot or frozen — but the *sanctions-primacy erosion* accelerates dramatically when Washington itself waives enforcement. That is what October 2026 delivered: a Treasury license suspending sanctions on Russian diesel deliveries until April 2027, an October 16 executive order waiving the Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026, and reported consideration of shadow-fleet relief to cut freight rates. [S62]

Congress could vote to override the waivers — the vote record is itself a key indicator for whether Brent migrates toward the lower band.

By state: under attrition or frozen conflict, expect this asymmetric equilibrium — US-first relief, EU architecture intact, Russian barrels re-flowing at discount-tolerant prices into non-EU markets. Under armistice, relief becomes *sequenced*: the EU's maritime-services ban basis is the relief lever traded against Russian compliance.

### Nuclear Signaling, Strategic Autonomy and the Rearmament Floor

Russia conducted nuclear signaling in May 2026, including an exercise, identified as a documented escalation hazard through 2027; SCEEUS assesses Russia holds a conventional, nuclear and hybrid escalation menu, and SWP Berlin tracks signaling from official Russian statements and exercises. A repeat of the May 2026 signaling package or a formal doctrine change in 2027 would shift weight toward the active-war state, with the proliferation incentive — that nuclear weapons confer coercive leverage without use — hardening in the meantime. [S64] The proliferation logic runs one way: every year the war persists without nuclear use but with nuclear signaling as routine coercive currency, the perceived insurance value of nuclear weapons grows for exposed states — the norm erosion is silent but cumulative, and a frozen conflict without guarantees teaches the sharpest lesson of all.

The mechanism to watch is slippage: SAFE payment delays or a trim to the German pending ask would cap the aggregate spending path under every war state. Paradoxically, autonomy deepens most not under war but under *armistice or settlement*: if US security engagement recedes with a deal, Europe's guarantee architecture (the Coalition of the Willing option) must stand alone, pulling spending toward the upper band of the post-deal range.

### The Sanctions Fault Line, the Global South and the ~19% Norm

Sanctions relief sequencing is the deal-maker or breaker. the EU's ratcheting 20th package represents the maximal-pressure pole; the actual US policy — unilateral, pre-deal, diesel-driven — sits awkwardly between them. Relief without a deal rewards Russia's waiting strategy, which is exactly the incentive structure Putin's final-deal-first stance exploits: he refuses a "short" ceasefire precisely because US relief flows regardless [S4]. The November 3 midterms test waiver durability;

Global South positioning tracks food, fuel and legitimacy. Zelensky's proposed ceasefires on food security, maritime activity and energy infrastructure are aimed squarely at this constituency — partial truces that trim grain and product premia while leaving the war unresolved. The legitimacy question is sharper: a sanctions regime visibly split between its two authors (US waivers vs EU packages) is one the Global South can arbitrage rather than join. Under attrition or freeze, expect continued non-alignment plus discount purchases; under armistice, gradual re-engagement with Russian supply on commercial terms.

Finally, the territorial-integrity norm. Under every state short of comprehensive settlement, this becomes the new base rate: force can revise borders and keep the gains, frozen without signature.

**Steelman and response.** The counterargument holds that US unilateral relief is tactical and reversible — a fuel-price instrument that Congress will claw back after midterms, restoring coalition discipline and making armistice sequencing viable. That is plausible; the October 2026 oil-futures pullback after the waiver shows markets price reversibility. [S94] But reversibility cuts both ways: if relief *produces* lower diesel and a quiet freeze, the political constituency for re-imposing sanctions on a de-escalating war evaporates. Probability mass stays with the unsigned states, and with them, a world where the borders moved and the paperwork never came.

## Risks, Wild Cards and Decision Implications for Policymakers and Investors

The preceding chapters priced five war end-states. This chapter inverts the lens: instead of asking what each end-state implies for markets, it asks what could break the forecast itself, and what each class of decision-maker should do with that map. Most tail risks compound each other rather than offsetting, the single largest source of forecast error is the interaction between the Middle East shock and the war premium rather than either alone, and the biggest mispriced object in markets today is the difference between a *freeze without signature* and a *signed armistice*, which carry nearly opposite asset implications despite being adjacent on the war-state continuum.

### The Double-Shock Problem: The War Is Not the Only Thing in the Price

This cuts both ways, and the asymmetry matters more than the level. On the upside, the IEA warns that a prolonged Strait of Hormuz disruption "could render unavailable the vast majority of the world's spare production capacity" [S13]—and that spare capacity is precisely the buffer that would otherwise cushion any war-driven Russian supply loss. Investors treating the upper band as remote should note the floor under that question is unknown.

### Escalation Tails: Mobilization, Nuclear Signaling and the Manpower Rupture

Three escalation tails deserve separate treatment because they operate on different clocks.

**Russian mobilization.** overt general mobilization would shift weight decisively toward the active high-intensity war state. A mid-2027 budget amendment that cuts security spending would signal tempo degradation toward attrition; [S60] one that expands it alongside a mobilization decree would confirm escalation. Two financial tripwires identify when financing becomes Russia's binding constraint: OFZ yields forced materially above current levels, or liquid National Wealth Fund assets falling toward a low single-digit share of GDP (this report's estimate of the thresholds at which the war-economy model breaks).

**Nuclear signaling.** Russia retains a menu of conventional, nuclear and hybrid escalation options, and Putin has publicly urged consideration of export restrictions on uranium, titanium and nickel [S119]—a reminder that the escalation menu extends into commodity supply itself. official Russian statements and announced exercises provide the verifiable monitoring source. The pricing point: nuclear signaling historically moves gold and the dollar more than oil, because it threatens horizontal escalation of the conflict rather than supply. With gold's five-year range already capped by the February 2026 peak of $5,014.65 [S7], a signaling episode would test that ceiling; sustained trading above it is the cleanest market confirmation that escalation risk is being priced rather than dismissed.

**The Ukrainian manpower rupture.** This is the risk most likely to materialize on the current path, and the most underweighted in markets. A new IMF financing arrangement for Ukraine would be a materially view-changing event; conversely, evidence of deposit flight or NBU foreign-exchange intervention beyond established bands would mark the rupture scenario activating. Investors long Ukrainian reconstruction exposure on freeze expectations are implicitly short this tail.

### The Sanctions Fracture: The Single Most consequential Policy Risk

The transatlantic sanctions split is not a background condition—it is an active process with a dated trigger.

Three consequences follow. First, a full US-EU rupture would collapse price-cap enforcement: any legally effective oil-cap waiver would reprice Russian supply, and Russian crude is already flowing at scale to Asia, the pattern documented in sanctions-decay analysis of Russia's turn to China [S67]. The bearish oil case does not require a peace deal; it requires only that enforcement decay to the point where barrels re-flow freely at a discount. Second, Congress is a check: a vote to override the waivers is a key indicator for Brent moving toward the lower band. The November 3 midterms therefore matter twice over—once for waiver durability, once for the congressional arithmetic of any override attempt. The strategic implication for Brussels: sequencing the maritime ban's adoption to coincide with the license expiry converts a coincidence of calendars into bargaining power. The risk, of course, is that oil spikes into that window—the maritime ban's adoption and enforcement would lift oil prices toward the escalation band—so the EU faces its own trade-off between leverage and domestic energy prices at the ECB's most sensitive moment.

Kiel Institute data already shows Europe leading on aid while remaining structurally dependent on US weapons [S12]—the gap is not hypothetical. Failure to close it cuts Ukraine's war-fighting floor and shifts weight toward the escalation state; The structural dependency compounds this: because European aid runs on US weapons and munitions, European "strategic autonomy" in aid is currently more aspiration than fact, and any US aid disruption—election-driven or punitive—would expose that dependency within weeks.

### Decision Implications: Four Actors, Four Asymmetries

The trap is asymmetric. failing to hike into persistent second-round effects would un-anchor expectations. a Brent monthly average above $117.69—the five-year high—would reprice the energy-inflation channel in every war state and force the issue regardless of core readings. [S23]

**For EU governments**, the defense path versus fiscal space question is less open than it looks.

**For investors**, the central insight is that freeze states and armistice states are priced as if similar but are not. A signed armistice re-rates the reconstruction complex—Ukrainian sovereign paper, CEE equities and currencies, construction and defense-adjacent industrials—because it unlocks financing at scale and normalizes legal risk, while only partially unwinding the safe-haven premium because durability remains unproven (recall that only strong, monitored agreements significantly lengthen peace, against the ETH/PRIO base rate in which only a small fraction of ceasefires last a year without violation). The trap is the middle: under reduced-intensity attrition, the modal state, both legs bleed—war premium decays slowly while reconstruction never arrives.

Ukrainian strategy—freeze now, guarantees later, per Zelensky's October 21 formulation —maximizes the chance of reaching those dates with a stable front (the ISW assessment of Russia's Donetsk offensive as operationally defeated provides the current basis [S2]). The caution for both capitals: every month of US unilateral relief without a deal strengthens Putin's incentive to keep waiting, because the current configuration—sanctions relief flowing, EU ratcheting, Ukraine's manpower eroding—delivers him most of what a freeze would, without conceding anything. Prudent positioning prices the mode but buys the tails cheaply.
