# Forecast the state of the Iran conflict and nuclear standoff by December 31, 2027, and its consequences for the global economy, macroeconomics, financial…

## Executive Summary

This report scores the state holding on 31 December 2027, not the worst event of the year: "by" a date means on or before it, "on" a date means the state holds that day; prices settle at the last trading day's official close, averages at means of daily fixings. "Renewed large-scale strikes" means a US or Israeli campaign of direct strikes on Iranian nuclear, military or regime targets beyond the limited exchanges traded [S1], met with Iranian retaliation, settled on CENTCOM and CRS reporting [S1][S2]. "Low-intensity conflict" requires no such campaign for at least 90 days while blockade, sabotage, cyber and proxy attacks persist; "Durable ceasefire" a verified ceasefire held at least six months without a nuclear text; "Negotiated framework" a signed text with partial sanctions relief and restored IAEA access; "Regime crisis" a leadership change outside the March 2026 procedure, a coercive-apparatus rupture, or a month-long protest wave.

As of late October 2026, the baseline is CRS's "war of attrition": the US and Iran "again trading limited strikes" after the June 2026 Memorandum of Understanding collapsed and formally expired in August [S1]; the US naval blockade, renewed in mid-July 2026 [S3], has effectively halted Iranian crude exports since mid-July, per Vortexa [S4]. The shock is already visible: in September 2026, IMF monthly-average Brent was 102.0181 U.S. dollars per barrel [S5], US CPI ran at 3.717% year-on-year [S6] with energy the largest euro-area inflation contributor [S7], the federal funds midpoint ended the month at 3.875% [S8], EU gas at 25.494 U.S. dollars per million metric British thermal units [S9], and gold at 4334.7467 U.S. dollars per troy ounce [S10].

Central thesis: managed-standoff continuation dominates comparable post-strike episodes, so the modal end-2027 state is Low-intensity conflict; the full frame:

- Low-intensity conflict — 45%: the blockade, sabotage, cyber and proxy attacks continue without renewed state-on-state strikes; Iran is held suppressed but unresolved under a persistent, bounded war premium.
- Renewed large-scale strikes — 25%: centrifuge reconstruction or breakout progress crosses Israeli or US red lines and re-strikes trigger Iranian retaliation; shifted up from a single-digit base rate by the verified 2025–2026 strike precedent and collapsed IAEA access.
- Durable ceasefire — 15%: economic exhaustion and blockade attrition push Tehran to accept verification without nuclear diplomacy.
- Negotiated framework — 10%: US or Chinese-mediated talks produce a signed text with partial sanctions relief and restored IAEA access; none are under way.
- Regime crisis — 5%: blockade-driven economic collapse triggers succession upheaval; a tail absent a visible challenger coalition.

Priced implications — this run's forecasts, since no market-implied end-2027 distribution exists in the evidence: weighting the scenario centrals yields roughly $4,150 per troy ounce for end-2027 gold and roughly $20 per MMBtu for end-2027 TTF, below the September 2026 readings [S10][S9]; state ranges: roughly 3,650–4,800 dollars per ounce, 10.5–34 dollars per MMBtu. The modal path holds Brent's blockade premium; Renewed large-scale strikes is the fat right tail — Hormuz disruption and a war premium on the 1990, 2019 Abqaiq and 2025–2026 precedents — an inflation pass-through keeping the Fed and ECB restrictive, dragging growth and hitting GCC and Israeli markets; Durable ceasefire and Negotiated framework deflate the premium. The falsifier for the low-premium states is renewed state-on-state strikes; for the high-premium states, a signed ceasefire or framework text.

Leading indicators: IAEA access and centrifuge reinstallation at Fordow, Natanz and Isfahan (reinstallation points to Renewed large-scale strikes; restored access to Negotiated framework); Iranian flows at loadings, blockade crossings and Chinese arrivals (Vortexa, Kpler, UANI; a sustained rebound signals the blockade is failing); Red Sea transits and proxy attacks (IMF PortWatch, ACLED; reconstitution raises shipping costs); the October 27–28, 2026 FOMC and October 29 ECB meetings [S11][S12]; and Assembly of Experts succession signals (an open rupture lifts the Regime crisis tail).

### Nuclear program after two strike rounds

Israel struck Iran's nuclear facilities on June 13, 2025; in the 12-day US-Israeli campaign that followed, all three then-operating enrichment plants were destroyed or badly damaged [S13]. On the strike's eve the IAEA estimated Iran held 440.9 kg of uranium enriched to up to 60% — enough, if enriched further, for ten nuclear weapons by an IAEA yardstick [S13]. Iran declared a new enrichment plant at Isfahan, in tunnels, one day before that attack; the tunnels were largely unscathed by US bombing, and the IAEA does not know the plant's status or whether it contains nuclear material [S13]. Surviving holdings are estimates, not measurements: Grossi said in March 2026 that "a bit more than 200 kg" of up-to-60% uranium remains in the Isfahan tunnel complex, with some still at Natanz [S13]; ISIS's post-ceasefire imagery puts 265–287 kg in those tunnels, over half the 60% stock [S14]. No post-strike breakout-time estimate exists in the record gathered here.

Reconstruction is partial. Above-ground centrifuge-production sites, including TESA Karaj which was struck again in the 2026 phase, show no evidence of rebuilding as of mid-May 2026, and tunnel entrances at Esfahan, Fordow and Natanz sealed before March 2026 remain sealed, leaving stocks "bottled up" underground [S14][S19]. Activity around the Fordow above-ground support site is high, and a planned two-cascade building there, nominally for iridium/tellurium separation, could be used for uranium enrichment if its feed stocks were not yet introduced [S14]. Construction continues at the unattacked Pickaxe Mountain complex south of Natanz, not yet operational [S14].

### Verification blackout

The IAEA cannot verify Iran's enriched-uranium stockpile or centrifuge status and cannot conclude that no declared material has been diverted [S15][S16]. Iran replied on February 2, 2026 that normal safeguards implementation was "legally untenable and materially impracticable" [S19], and refuses design-information and inventory verification at Isfahan on the ground that the sites were "subjected to illegal military attacks" [S16][S19]. The Board of Governors urged cooperation on June 10 and again on September 9, 2026 (GOV/2026/61) [S20].

The naval blockade of Iranian ports began April 13, 2026, after the war's February 28, 2026 start and failed talks, stopping Iranian vessels and cargoes while letting neutral traffic pass [S4]. An easing under the June agreement let some barrels move — two tankers carrying a combined 3.8 million barrels left Iran unimpeded as the deal took effect [S22] — but the US renewed the blockade on July 13, 2026 as the deal broke down [S3], and Vortexa says exports have been effectively halted since mid-July [S4]. Iran entered the blockade with crude pre-positioned on tankers in Asia; deliveries to China have disconnected from loadings as the offshore buffer nears exhaustion [S4] — the New York Post, summarizing a Wall Street Journal interview, quotes Kpler's Homayoun Falakshahi: "There's no more oil to discharge to China anymore" [S41]. The readings diverge on measurement point, not direction; an aggregator's 475,000 bpd for September with a stop after September 26 [S42] is uncorroborated.

### Chinese purchases and substitution

Treasury states that China purchases approximately 90 percent of Iran's oil exports, with teapot refineries the majority [S43]. As flows fell, Chinese independent refiners bought at least 12 million barrels of Iraqi and Qatari crude for October–November 2026 delivery from Mercuria, Totsa and Trafigura (traders estimated 15–20 million barrels), after more than 20 million barrels of West African, Canadian and Colombian crude in late August–early September [S40]. Vortexa expects China's seaborne crude imports around 8.8 mbd in October 2026, up from a 7.5 mbd third-quarter average but below the 2025 average of 10.6 mbd; Saudi arrivals above 2 mbd (2025 average 1.6 mbd) and Iraqi near 1.1 mbd put the two above 35 percent of China's October seaborne imports [S44].

### Sanctions architecture and the shadow fleet

Before the blockade, Iranian flows had rebuilt to roughly 1.5–2 mbd in 2024–25 via opaque ownership, AIS manipulation, ship-to-ship transfers and a concentrated Chinese buyer base [S4]. Secretary Bessent announced "Operation Economic Outcast" on August 24, 2026 — "Economic D-Day" — expanding sanctions exposure for non-US persons dealing with Iran [S45]; on October 8, 2026 OFAC designated 17 shadow-fleet vessels moving Iranian crude, products, petrochemicals and bitumen to South and East Asia, an action Treasury says "effectively neutralizes the vast majority of Iran's remaining shadow fleet network" [S45]. That is an enforcement claim, not an observation: Kpler still counted 590,000 bpd reaching China in September and 45 million barrels afloat outside the blockade [S40]. The same action delisted two vessels sold to US-aligned operators (HAKUNA MATATA, IMO 9354167; PINOCCHIO, IMO 9400112) [S45]. Sectoral sanctions on auto and rail took effect October 1, 2026 [S23]; the World Bank projects a 7.7% contraction for Iran's fiscal year 2026-27, a projection Iranian authorities reject [S46].

### Shipping conditions and market effect

Middle East exports recovered despite the blockade: Kpler's 7-day average was 18.3 million bpd on September 30, 2026 versus roughly 18 million bpd in the twelve pre-war months; Vortexa's 14-day average hit 18.6 million bpd, above the 10-year seasonal average [S47]. The costs are visible: at least seven tanker incidents in the week to October 5, 2026, including the VLCC Kazimah III struck in the Strait of Hormuz on October 1 [S47]; longer China-bound voyages via ship-to-ship transfers east of Hormuz or near India [S44]; Iraq arranged passage for its own tankers in August 2026 [S47]. The Africa Center reports the Houthis resumed attacks in July 2026 after a half-year hiatus — twelve on commercial vessels in the southern Red Sea since — leaving transits below one-third of pre-October-2023 levels [S48]; Reuters reports war-risk insurance costs surging after the Houthi blockade announcement on Saudi ports and Bab al-Mandeb shipping [S49]. WTI at Cushing averaged 97.3081 dollars per barrel in September 2026 against 63.959 a year earlier, printing 107.89 on October 20, 2026 [S18]; US real exports of goods and services were 2814.538 billions of chained 2017 dollars in 2026-Q2, up 5.837% from 2659.32 in 2025-Q2 — no visible US-trade contraction from the campaign [S30].

What would change the view: documented loadings back above roughly 500 kbd would signal blockade erosion (Vortexa/Kpler); an OFAC designation of a named Chinese teapot refiner would test Beijing's tolerance (Treasury); exhaustion of the 45-million-barrel buffer would complete China's substitution toward Gulf and Atlantic barrels (Kpler); a Houthi halt would reopen Red Sea routing and compress freight and insurance.

## Israeli and US Red Lines and Strike Doctrine

Definition: "renewed large-scale strikes" means direct US or Israeli state-on-state strikes on Iranian nuclear, military or regime targets beyond the current limited exchange; "by D" means on or before D, settled on CENTCOM releases and CRS reporting [S1][S2]. As of 2026-10-23 the state is CRS's "war of attrition": the US and Iran "again trading limited strikes" after the June 2026 Memorandum of Understanding collapsed and formally expired in August [S1].

The doctrinal baseline was set in 2026: strikes beginning February 28 killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei and dozens of senior leaders and hit IRGC, Basij and regime-security targets — decapitation is now precedented — under the US operation designated "Operation Epic Fury" [S1]. The Assembly of Experts installed Mojtaba Khamenei, a figure with deep IRGC ties, within about a week [S17]. The EU Institute for Security Studies assesses that the June 2025 Twelve-Day War "broke the taboo" on direct confrontation, so pre-2025 shadow-war base rates understate current escalation propensity [S66].

### Succession and the internal balance

The succession was rapid and institutionally clean. An interim council — President Masoud Pezeshkian, the Supreme Court chief and Ayatollah Alireza Arafi — bridged the gap per constitutional procedure, according to accounts of the council [S88][S89]. Competing nominees reportedly included Hassan Rouhani and Hassan Khomeini [S84], but the Council on Foreign Relations notes that arrests of key reformist politicians after the December 2025–February 2026 protests foreclosed a reformist outcome [S87]. External patrons reinforced consolidation: Putin pledged "unwavering" support for Mojtaba's appointment and China opposed any targeting of the new supreme leader [S17]. The Foundation for Strategic Research (FRS) assesses that despite the war and the killing of senior officials, the Islamic Republic did not fragment: the coercive apparatus stayed cohesive, killed officials were replaced, and US negotiations proceeded intermittently [S85].

Under Mojtaba, practical authority runs through a coalition centred on the Supreme National Security Council (SNSC), the Defense Council, the Armed Forces General Staff, the IRGC, the presidency, parliamentary leadership and the judiciary-security apparatus, per FRS [S85]; the August swap of Zolghadr for Rezaei as the Leader's representative and SNSC secretary keeps IRGC figures central while the coalition still changes [S85]. The reformist-leaning presidency survives and carries the US channel, whose record is shaky: a Pakistan-mediated two-week ceasefire took effect 8 April 2026 [S21], and a June 2026 Memorandum of Understanding — reported terms included ceasefire extension, a 60-day nuclear-deal clock and a US blockade lift [S22] — collapsed within weeks and formally expired in August 2026 [S1]. Iran's current proposal, conveyed through Qatari mediators, offers to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days under Iranian control in exchange for lifting the maritime blockade, easing oil-export restrictions, releasing frozen assets and ending hostilities; sequencing remains disputed [S23]. Iran pre-delegated missile-launch authority to lower-ranking officers before the February strikes, so retaliation no longer requires a supreme leader's authorization [S69].

Forbes on 28 February 2026 rated the probability of IRGC consolidation around a collective leadership with a clerical figurehead at roughly 35% [S69]. Forbes put the probability of a protracted power contest at about 30% on 28 February 2026 [S69]. Forbes put the probability of a popular uprising seizing the vacuum at 25% on 28 February 2026 [S69].

### Stability through 2027

The economy is the stress point: the blockade has cut off oil-export revenues [S23], loadings were 210 kbd (thousand barrels per day) in mid-September 2026 per Vortexa [S4], and Treasury Secretary Bessent warned in August 2026 that Iran "could face economic collapse in months," a claim Iranian authorities reject [S46]. CRS assesses Iran faces an increasingly perilous economic situation and may have limited ability to continue attacks; the regime does not appear at risk of imminent collapse, but that possibility remains [S1]. Minor protests continue after the January 2026 crackdown [S86].

The revealed record supports the canonical weights. Regime crisis — 5% stays a tail: the apparatus held, succession was orderly and no challenger coalition is visible [S85][S1]. Negotiated framework — 10% needs the diplomatic faction in office plus a signed text; talks are deadlocked and deliberately prolonged [S23]. Renewed large-scale strikes — 25% is fed by the IRGC-heavy coalition, pre-delegated launch authority [S69] and the September 2026 preemption doctrine [S1]; Low-intensity conflict — 45% matches the attrition posture CRS describes [S1]. Netanyahu's campaign line that "there's only one way to remove this threat once and for all — overthrow the regime in Iran" is a stated Israeli aim, not a forecast [S26].

### Spare capacity: effective versus headline

On the IEA's effective metric the buffer is nearly spent: OPEC-8 (excluding Iran, Libya and Venezuela) produced 13.71 million b/d in June 2026 against 22.83 million b/d of sustainable capacity, yet effective spare capacity was only 0.04 million b/d, because shut-in Iranian and Russian crude is excluded from the effective-capacity measure [S100]; headline claims of 4–6 million b/d of OPEC spare capacity [S101] therefore overstate what is idle. The September ramp — Saudi-led OPEC+ adding 2.1 million b/d month-on-month, global supply up 2.5 million b/d to 102.3 million b/d, Gulf crude exports up 4.4 million b/d to 13.9 million b/d, over 75% of pre-war levels [S57] — is capacity already deployed, not a reserve.

### Inventories: the binding constraint

Cumulative global observed inventories were 509 million barrels below pre-war levels by end-September 2026, with on-land stocks at their lowest since at least 2016 [S57]. June 2026 alone saw a 62 mb OECD draw, including an estimated 44 mb of government releases, while oil-on-water rose 117 mb [S100]. The EIA estimates draws of 1.9 million b/d on average in 3Q26 and projects a further 0.7 million b/d in 4Q26 [S99]. Emergency tools are engaged: on October 15, 2026 the IEA agreed to release over 20 million barrels of diesel, alongside remaining volumes of a record March 2026 release [S50].

### Chinese substitution: quick and cheap, total imports depressed

US Treasury assesses that China purchases approximately 90 percent of Iran's oil exports, mostly via teapot refineries [S43]. Chinese imports of Iranian crude nearly halved year-on-year to 590,000 b/d in September 2026 (Kpler data via Reuters), the lowest since January 2023, and Iranian crude stored on vessels outside the blockade zone more than halved to 45 million barrels, from 100 million in late July [S40]; Reuters, citing Vortexa, reports zero Iranian exports in October [S39]. Substitution is fast: teapots bought at least 12 million barrels of Iraqi and Qatari crude for October–November delivery (traders estimated 15–20 million barrels), after more than 20 million barrels of West African, Canadian and Colombian crude [S40]. Vortexa expects October Saudi arrivals above 2 million b/d (2025 average 1.6) and Iraqi near 1.1 million b/d — over 35% of China's seaborne imports — though total seaborne imports of about 8.8 million b/d sit far below the 10.6 million b/d 2025 average [S44]. US real exports of goods and services — 2814.538 billions of chained 2017 dollars in 2026-Q2, up 5.837% year-on-year [S30] — point to non-Gulf supply growth, though the series is not oil-specific.

### Absorption in both directions

Offline, the crude loss is largely replaced: with Vortexa putting loadings at 210,000 b/d [S4], Middle East exports ran above pre-war levels on 14 days of September (Kpler 7-day 18.3, Vortexa 14-day 18.6 million b/d) [S47]; the binding constraints are products — Hormuz product transits of 677,000 b/d against roughly 3.6 million b/d prewar [S56] — and the inventory floor [S57]. In the return case, the EIA assumes Middle East export workarounds (pipeline bypasses, ship-to-ship transfers, new UAE bypass capacity in 2027) restore most regional production to pre-conflict averages by end-2Q27, with Brent averaging $105/b in 4Q26, $87/b by 2Q27 and $74/b in 4Q27, and 2027 production of 109.6 million b/d against consumption of 104.6 million b/d [S99]. The IEA instead projects 2027 supply up 7.7 million b/d, demand still 620 kb/d below 2025, and a possible surplus [S57]; on either ledger, 1.5–2 million b/d of returning Iranian barrels would land in a balanced-to-long market.

## Historical War-Premium Base Rates

### Definitions and measurement

A war premium is the move in a price from its last pre-event observation to its episode peak; its persistence is the time for at least half of that move to decay. Daily episodes settle on EIA and Reuters settlement data; monthly episodes on the IMF monthly Brent series [S5]. An escalation counts only when renewed large-scale state-on-state strikes occur by the two-year mark of a named start event, where "by" means on or before that date. The escalation counts below are derived from the cited episode lists, not from a conflict dataset.

### The discriminating variable is realized supply loss

Counting on the stored IMF window (2006-10 to 2026-09), of five distinct Middle-East shock episodes — 2011-12 Libya and Iran sanctions, the 2019 attack months, 2022 Russia-Ukraine, and 2025-26 Iran strikes — only the two with sustained realized supply loss kept monthly Brent more than $10/b above the pre-shock level for six months or longer, and even the 2022 spike decayed from 117.6927 US dollars per barrel (2022-06) to 81.5032 (2022-12) [S5]. Premiums without loss decayed in days to weeks: the 2019 attack months show essentially no net monthly premium (59.6182, 62.431 and 59.6313 US dollars per barrel in August-October 2019) [S5].

### Gas and gold premiums

Gas requires a Europe-relevant supply threat: the Abqaiq month shows none — the EU natural gas monthly average was 3.0868 U.S. Dollars per Million Metric British Thermal Unit in September 2019 [S9] — while the 2026 war lifted it to 17.67 in March 2026 and 25.494 in September 2026, against 11.003 a year earlier, a 131.7% year-on-year rise [S9]; Reuters reported a 46% intraday jump in front-month TTF on March 2, 2026 [S59]. Gold's episodic premium is modest: about 1.5% on the day of the June 13, 2025 strikes and 4% for that week, per Reuters [S112][S113]; the Guardian's "$34,434 an ounce" print for that date is inconsistent with the roughly $3,430-3,450 level then and is likely a typo [S112]. The 2026 move is a level shift, not a spike: the IMF monthly gold average peaked at 5014.645 US dollars per troy ounce in February 2026 and averaged 4334.7467 in September [S10], while the IMF all-commodity index averaged 222.4285 (2016=100) in September 2026 against 167.4645 a year earlier, +32.821% [S29].

## Inflation Pass-Through and Policy-Rate Paths

### Pass-through already realized

The 2026 shock has already re-accelerated both economies. US headline CPI was 3.717% year-on-year in September 2026, up from 2.391% in January 2026, after a 2.325% trough in April 2025 and a 4.167% peak in May 2026 [S6]. Euro-area annual inflation was 3.8% in September 2026 per Eurostat, with energy the largest contributor at +1.71 percentage points [S7]; the FRED harmonized series computes 3.75% for the same month, up 1.534 points year-on-year [S35], after 3.249% in August on the BIS series [S33]. The year to September 2026 took IMF monthly Brent from 67.6091 to 102.0181 US dollars per barrel, +50.894% [S5]; over the same window US CPI rose 0.695 points [S6]. Europe carries a second channel: the EU natural gas monthly average was 25.494 U.S. Dollars per Million Metric British Thermal Unit in September 2026 against 11.003 a year earlier, +131.7% [S9]. Credit stress is creeping with the tightening: the ICE BofA US high-yield option-adjusted spread averaged 2.755% in September 2026 and stood at 3.27% on October 21, 2026 [S32].

### Policy baseline and the relief toolkit

The federal funds target midpoint was 3.875% at end-September 2026 [S8], after the FOMC raised the range by a quarter point to 3-3/4 to 4 percent on September 16, 2026 by a 12-0 vote, citing elevated inflation and geopolitical uncertainty [S11]; Reuters reports officials signaling at least one more hike possible before year-end, with the next meeting scheduled for October 27-28, 2026 [S12][S63]. The ECB deposit rate was 2.5% at end-September 2026, after 2% from February through May and 2.25% in June [S28]. Markets assign an approximately 96% probability to the ECB holding its deposit rate at 2.50% at its October 29, 2026 Governing Council meeting [S65]. Supply-side relief is also in use: the IEA agreed on October 15, 2026 to release over 20 million barrels of diesel [S50], and President Trump temporarily lifted sanctions on Russian diesel on October 12, 2026 [S50]. The IEA assesses cumulative observed inventories were 509 million barrels below pre-war levels by end-September 2026, with balances in deficit through end-2026 and 2027 demand forecast 620 kb/d below 2025 [S57] — pass-through will decay only as fast as this balance turns.

## Scenario Frame for End-2027

### Scenario Probability Distribution
- **Low-intensity conflict (45% probability):** The blockade, sabotage, cyber and proxy attacks continue without renewed state-on-state strikes, holding Iran suppressed but unresolved; prior: managed-standoff continuation dominates comparable post-strike episodes.
- **Renewed large-scale strikes (25% probability):** Iran's centrifuge reconstruction or breakout progress crosses Israeli or US red lines and re-strikes on nuclear or regime targets trigger Iranian retaliation; shifted up from a single-digit base rate by verified 2025-2026 strike precedent and collapsed IAEA access.
- **Durable ceasefire (15% probability):** Economic exhaustion and blockade attrition push Tehran to accept a durable ceasefire with verification but no nuclear diplomacy, freezing the conflict below the strike threshold.
- **Negotiated framework (10% probability):** US or Chinese-mediated talks convert the ceasefire into a partial nuclear framework with limited sanctions relief and restored IAEA access; requires a signed text, so weighted modestly absent current talks.
- **Regime crisis (5% probability):** Economic collapse under the blockade triggers succession upheaval or internal regime crisis that reshapes the conflict; single-digit weight as a tail absent a visible challenger coalition.

The frame scores the state holding on 31 December 2027, not the worst event of the year; "by" a date means the event occurred on or before it. Renewed large-scale strikes requires a US or Israeli campaign of direct strikes on Iranian nuclear, military or regime targets — beyond the limited exchanges the sides already trade [S1] — met with Iranian retaliation. Low-intensity conflict requires no such campaign for at least 90 days while blockade, sabotage, cyber and proxy attacks persist. Durable ceasefire requires a ceasefire held at least six months with verification but no signed nuclear text; Negotiated framework requires a signed text with partial sanctions relief and restored IAEA access; Regime crisis requires the death, removal or contested succession of Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei [S17]. Settling sources are CRS reporting, CENTCOM releases and IAEA documents [S1][S20].

### State of play and most-likely trajectory

CRS describes the United States and Iran "again trading limited strikes" in a war of attrition after an April 2026 ceasefire and a June 2026 memorandum of understanding collapsed and expired in August 2026 [S1]. The IAEA has had no access to any declared enrichment facility since January 2026 and can verify neither stockpile nor centrifuges [S16]. Vortexa put loadings at 210 thousand barrels per day in mid-September 2026 [S4]; Reuters cites a Vortexa report of zero October exports [S39]. Talks are deadlocked, with Iranian officials prolonging them past the 3 November midterms [S23][S24], while the New York Times reports drafted plans for a "short but intensive" post-midterm operation [S24]. Markets already carry the standoff: Brent ran from 69.4095 U.S. dollars per barrel in February 2026 to 102.0181 in September [S5], WTI printed 107.89 dollars per barrel on 2026-10-20 [S18], gold averaged 4334.7467 U.S. dollars per troy ounce in September 2026 [S10], and the IMF all-commodity index read 222.4285 (2016=100) [S29]. The most-likely trajectory is Low-intensity conflict — 45%: the midterm gate passes without a campaign, and EIA projects Brent easing to an $84/b average in 2027 and $74/b in 4Q27 as export workarounds restore regional flows — a projection consistent with managed standoff rather than renewed war [S99].
