# US election mechanics

Load this for chamber rules, calendars, certification, recounts, runoffs, redistricting, ratings, money and actor classes.

Rules here are durable. Re-check anything marked "verify", and take every current count (seats held, seats up, maps in force) from S1 before use.

## 1. Control rules

**House**
- 435 voting seats; a majority of the full House is 218. Non-voting delegates (DC and the territories) never count.
- Vacancies are filled only by special election, never by appointment, so the working majority can shrink for months.
- The Speaker needs a majority of members voting for a named person, not a fixed 218. Multi-ballot Speaker contests have occurred (2023).
- Name the convention the question uses:
  - seats won at the election (certified winners reaching 218);
  - majority at swearing-in on January 3;
  - majority on a later date.

**Senate**
- 100 seats in three classes. About a third (33 or 34) are up each even year, plus special elections for vacated seats. A seat filled by a governor's appointment goes to a special election under state law (verify each state).
- Control is 51 seats, or 50 plus the vice president's tie-breaking vote (Art. I §3). The VP belongs to the president's party, so in a midterm the in-party controls at 50.
- Independents count with the caucus they join. The organizing resolution sets committee ratios. 50–50 chambers have run under power-sharing agreements (2001, 2021).
- Control can change mid-Congress (a 2001 party switch did it).
- The 60-vote cloture threshold is not control; "filibuster-proof" is a separate question.

**Presidential**
- 538 electors; 270 wins. Maine and Nebraska give two electors to the statewide winner and one per congressional district.
- With no majority, the House chooses, voting by state delegation (12th Amendment).
- The Electoral Count Reform Act (2022) governs certificates and objections (verify details).
- Inauguration is on January 20.

**States**
- Some state senates break ties with the lieutenant governor.
- Some chambers are run by cross-party coalitions (e.g. Alaska).
- Nebraska's legislature is unicameral and nonpartisan.
- Fetch each chamber's own rules.

## 2. Calendar and resolution conventions

**Dates.** Election Day is the Tuesday after the first Monday in November of even years (2 U.S.C. §7). A new Congress convenes on January 3 (20th Amendment) unless a law sets another day.

**Primaries.** Primary dates and filing deadlines vary by state (NCSL; state election offices). After the filing deadline the field is fixed; replacing a withdrawn nominee follows state law.

**Calls** are projections by the AP, network decision desks and Decision Desk HQ.
- Slow-counting states, and states that accept mail ballots arriving after Election Day, can delay the House-control call by more than a week (2022).
- Ballot-receipt deadlines have been litigated: verify the rules in force.

**Certification.** County canvass → state certification on statutory deadlines (NCSL compiles them) → certificates of election. The Clerk of the House publishes official statistics months later.

**Recounts.**
- Automatic-recount thresholds are statutory and narrow (often ≤0.5 points); other recounts are by request (NCSL table).
- Statewide recounts have rarely changed the winner and usually move margins by hundreds of votes (FairVote's recount studies; verify).

**Runoffs and ranked-choice voting.**
- Georgia requires a majority in general elections, so a runoff follows weeks later. Its January 2021 runoffs decided Senate control.
- Louisiana's congressional system changed recently: verify the rules in force.
- Ranked-choice tabulation adds days in Maine (federal races) and Alaska (top-four primary; verify its status).

**Contested elections.** Each chamber judges its own members' elections (Art. I §5; the Federal Contested Elections Act for the House). This is rare.

**Write the convention into each binary forecast:**
- called versus certified versus seated;
- on which date;
- party label versus caucus;
- whether specials are included.

## 3. Exposure and the map

**Senate.** List:
- the seats up;
- each party's holdovers;
- open seats (retirements);
- specials;
- crossover seats, held by the party that lost that state at the last presidential election.
Holdovers plus seats won give the new total.

**House.** List:
- open seats;
- retirements by party (members retire more often ahead of bad years: Jacobson & Kernell's strategic-politician theory);
- crossover districts;
- seats won by under 5 points last time.

**Candidate quality.** Prior elected office is the classic measure (Jacobson). Ideologically extreme nominees cost their party votes (Hall, APSR 2015). Primary results reshape the ratings.

**Sources.** Clerk of the House and Senate.gov party-division and class pages (S1); state election offices (S1); Cook PVI (S2); Ballotpedia (S3 map; verify).

## 4. Redistricting

**Status per state.** One of: enacted, court-ordered, referendum pending, enjoined, or under appeal. Use the map in force on Election Day.

**Law.**
- Partisan-gerrymandering claims are non-justiciable in federal court (Rucho v. Common Cause, 2019), but state constitutions and courts may police them.
- Section 2 vote-dilution claims under the Voting Rights Act (Allen v. Milligan, 2023) have been litigated since: verify current doctrine.
- Under Purcell v. Gonzalez (2006), federal courts avoid changing election rules close to an election.
- Mid-decade redraws are allowed unless state law forbids them.

**Measure the effect.**
- Count districts by last presidential margin under the old and new lines: seats at +5 or more each way, and seats within ±5.
- Note the shift in the median seat.
- Note incumbents paired or displaced.

**Traps (misreading → discriminating observation):**
- "Same district number = same district" → compare the new district's partisanship.
- "A court struck the map" → check whether the ruling is stayed and which map the state will actually use.

**Sources.**
- Map files from the state legislature or commission, and court dockets (S1).
- All About Redistricting (Loyola Law School) for litigation status (S2).
- The Downballot / Daily Kos Elections and Dave's Redistricting for presidential results by new district (S2/S3).
- PlanScore and the Princeton Gerrymandering Project for bias metrics (S2).

## 5. Ratings, models, money, specials, turnout

**Ratings.** Cook Political Report, Sabato's Crystal Ball (UVA Center for Politics) and Inside Elections.
- Categories: Solid, Likely, Lean, Toss-up (Inside Elections adds Tilt). Record the as-of date; the direction of rating changes is the signal.
- Documented pattern (verify against the raters' track-record posts):
  - the favoured party wins nearly all Likely and Solid races and most Lean ones;
  - in wave years, toss-ups break disproportionately toward the wave party.

**Models** published by data journalists and outlets are REPORTED. Record the method (polls-only or blended with fundamentals) and the date.

**FEC (S1).**
- Candidate reports: quarterly in election years; pre-general 12 days before; post-general 30 days after; 48-hour notices for large late contributions.
- Independent-expenditure reports (24- and 48-hour), from party committees (DCCC, NRCC, DSCC, NRSC) and super PACs.
- Read cash on hand, not total raised. Separate individual money, self-funding and transfers. Track outside spending per race.
- Ad reservations and cancellations by party committees and allied super PACs (AdImpact, S2) reveal private polling: money follows competitiveness.
- Traps:
  - joint fundraising committees inflate totals;
  - 501(c)(4) "dark money" falls outside FEC detail.

**Special elections.**
- Compare each result's margin with the district's last presidential margin. Trackers (The Downballot, Daily Kos Elections) average this overperformance.
- Prior to verify on the trackers' archives: in recent cycles it pointed the right way for the next midterm environment but overstated the size, since specials draw low-turnout, high-propensity electorates.
- Discount by turnout relative to the last midterm.

**Early vote and turnout.**
- Sources: state election offices (S1); US Elections Project (S2); vendors of modelled partisanship (S3).
- Compare with the same voting mode last cycle.
- After the vote, coalition composition comes from the Census CPS Voting and Registration Supplement (S1), and from AP VoteCast, exit polls and Pew validated-voter studies (S2).

## 6. Actor classes for the simulation (US)
Map each actor to the extractor's role classes.

| Role class | US election actors | Facts to record |
|---|---|---|
| principal | President and administration; congressional party leaders; party campaign committees; nominees in tipping-point races | Goal (majority, seat target); levers (agenda, endorsements, recruitment, money, ads); constraints (approval, filing deadlines, contribution limits); stated versus revealed behaviour (spin versus reservations and spending) |
| arbiter | State election officials and canvass boards; state and federal courts; FEC; chambers judging contested elections | Rules applied, deadlines, past rulings, pending litigation |
| stakeholder | Voter blocs (education × race, age, suburban/rural, Latino, Black, union households, independents); interest groups | Share of the electorate, partisan lean, turnout propensity, trend since last cycle, salient issues; source (AP VoteCast, exit polls, Pew, CPS) |
| intermediary | Super PACs; state parties; field and turnout organizations | Spending, reservation changes, targets |
| amplifier | Media, pollsters, raters, market venues, platforms, influencers | Reach, lean, track record |

**Behavioural base rates the simulation can use:**
- incumbent re-election rates;
- strategic retirement ahead of bad years;
- toss-ups breaking together;
- losing candidates litigating or delaying concession in close races (document actual cases; do not assume).

Model voter blocs as stakeholder actors with measured attributes, never as invented individuals.
